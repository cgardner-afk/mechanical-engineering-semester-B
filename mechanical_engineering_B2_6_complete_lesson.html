Engineering design starts with requirements

A hydraulic lift is useful because liquid transmits pressure between pistons. But selecting a hydraulic system requires more than finding a large output force. Engineers begin with an engineering requirement, such as lifting a specified load by a minimum height. They then identify design constraints, including maximum pressure and available actuator travel. A feasible design satisfies every mandatory constraint. Only after confirming feasibility can engineers compare designs using an optimization objective. In this challenge, the objective is minimizing input force among feasible candidates.

Suppose a system can exert 2200 N but moves its load only 0.07 m, while the task requires 1500 N and 0.15 m. The design fails despite its impressive force. A competing design that delivers 1550 N through 0.16 m could pass. You must judge the entire configuration, not one attractive measurement. This principle applies to machines well beyond hydraulics: successful engineering means meeting the specified need under the specified limits.

Pressure and circular piston area

The input pressure change is ΔP = F₁/A₁. A circular piston has piston area A = πd²/4. Convert diameter from millimeters to meters before calculating area in square meters. A 20 mm diameter piston has area π(0.020)²/4 ≈ 0.00031416 m². A 200 N input creates 200/0.00031416 ≈ 636,620 Pa, or 636.6 kPa.

A smaller input piston creates greater pressure for the same applied force. This is not automatically beneficial: high pressure can violate the modeled pressure limit. Pressure is force divided by area, not the output force itself. The virtual studio reports input pressure separately from useful output force so you can diagnose a pressure failure without confusing it with a lifting failure.

Pascal's principle and force multiplication

Pascal's principle describes the transmission of an applied pressure change through enclosed fluid at rest. For ideal pistons at the same elevation, F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂. The area ratio R = A₂/A₁ is the ideal mechanical advantage. Thus ideal output force is F₂ = F₁R.

If the output piston diameter is three times the input diameter, its area is nine times as great because area depends on diameter squared. Therefore R = (d₂/d₁)² = 9. With 180 N input, the ideal output is 1620 N. An error that uses three rather than nine as the ratio comes from confusing diameter with area. The fluid transmits a pressure increment; unequal piston areas produce unequal forces.

Volume conservation and travel

An incompressible liquid in an ideal sealed system transfers equal volumes at both pistons. The volume conservation equation is A₁s₁ = A₂s₂. The input stroke s₁ is the input piston movement, while the output stroke s₂ is the lift distance. Rearranging gives s₂ = s₁/R.

A force ratio of nine means the output travels one-ninth as far as the input. With a 1.35 m input stroke, the output moves 0.15 m. If only 0.90 m of input stroke is available, output travel is 0.10 m and fails a 0.15 m requirement. Increasing input force will not correct this geometric travel shortage. The engineer must adjust the area ratio or available stroke.

Energy, work, and efficiency

Mechanical work equals force times displacement in its direction. Input work is Win = F₁s₁. In an ideal hydraulic system, F₂s₂ = F₁s₁: increased output force is balanced by reduced output travel. No extra energy appears. Real systems lose energy through friction, flow resistance, leakage, and other mechanisms.

The efficiency η is useful output work divided by input work. In our educational model, useful output force equals ηF₁R while output travel remains s₁/R. Therefore useful output work is ηF₁s₁. This simplified constant-efficiency relationship is transparent and useful for comparison, but actual hydraulic efficiency depends on operating conditions. A slider value is not a certified machine performance measurement.

Four simultaneous feasibility checks

The studio checks useful output force against required load, output travel against minimum lift, input pressure against maximum allowed pressure, and input stroke against maximum allowed stroke. Each comparison has a numerical constraint margin. Load margin equals useful force minus required force. Travel margin equals output travel minus required lift. Pressure margin equals allowed pressure minus actual pressure. Stroke margin equals maximum stroke minus actual input stroke.

A nonnegative margin passes its associated condition. Zero margin passes mathematically but provides no buffer. A negative margin identifies failure and suggests what to change. If pressure is too high, increasing input piston area may help; if output travel is too small, decreasing area ratio may help. Changes often affect multiple outputs, so recalculate all four conditions after every modification.

Solving backward from requirements

An inverse design begins with the required output force rather than an arbitrary input force. For chosen efficiency and ratio, minimum input force is F₁,min = Frequired/(ηR). To obtain required output travel, the input stroke must be at least s₁,req = Rsrequired. Check whether that stroke fits the actuator limit. Then calculate input pressure from the chosen input diameter.

For example, with 1500 N required, 90% efficiency, and R = 9, the minimum theoretical input force is 1500/(0.9 × 9) = 185.19 N. A 0.15 m lift requires 1.35 m input stroke. If the maximum input stroke is 1.50 m, that condition passes. Yet the pressure calculation is still essential. This sequence prevents the common mistake of declaring success after calculating output force alone.

Comparing candidate designs

An engineering trade-off arises when improving one outcome worsens another. Increasing area ratio can reduce required input force, but increases required input travel. Decreasing input diameter may increase force ratio for a fixed output diameter, but also increases pressure at a given input force. An effective designer explores these competing effects systematically.

The studio allows you to save several candidate designs and compare their force, travel, pressure, and feasibility. The lowest input force among saved feasible designs is the best observed candidate. It is not necessarily the mathematical global optimum, because sliders sample a finite set of values. A defensible report explains the search process and its resolution rather than claiming to have proven an optimum without sufficient evidence.

Pressure margin and robustness

Two configurations can meet the same load and lift requirements while operating at different pressures. Suppose both use a diameter ratio of three, 190 N input, and 1.40 m input stroke at 90% efficiency. Both deliver 1539 N and 0.1556 m lift. But a 20 mm input piston creates approximately 604.8 kPa, whereas a 25 mm input piston creates approximately 387.1 kPa.

Both pass an 800 kPa limit, yet the second configuration has a larger remaining pressure margin. When the primary optimization objective ties, the margin provides a reasonable secondary criterion. Engineers also consider cost, mass, packaging, durability, and other factors not included in this model. A numerical result becomes useful only when connected to a stated decision rule.

Sensitivity to efficiency

Sensitivity analysis examines how predictions respond when assumptions change. For fixed diameters, input force, and stroke, reducing modeled efficiency from 95% to 85% reduces useful output force and useful work. It does not change ideal geometric output travel or input pressure. A design that barely passes the load at 95% may fail at 85%.

Test a passing candidate at multiple efficiency settings and record the load margins. This gives a simple robustness check. A larger margin may justify choosing a design that requires slightly more input force. The appropriate balance depends on the assignment's priorities, so distinguish the mandatory constraints from any optional preference for additional margins.

Limits of the hydraulic model

The simulation assumes equal piston elevations, incompressible fluid, rigid pistons, no leakage, no pipe pressure losses, and a constant efficiency factor. Real equipment requires rated cylinders, hoses, seals, valves, structural supports, stability checks, inspection, and protection against uncontrolled load movement. Hydraulic pressure can cause severe injury, including fluid injection injuries.

A virtual PASS means only that the specified equations and numerical constraints pass. It does not mean a real lift is safe to build or operate. Do not use the simulation to specify or test physical pressure equipment. Engineering professionalism requires communicating model limitations as clearly as calculations.

Writing the final engineering recommendation

Your design justification should identify selected piston diameters, input force, input stroke, assumed efficiency, useful output force, output travel, and pressure. Include all four margins. Compare at least three saved candidates, explain why an alternative failed, and identify the best feasible candidate you observed.

A strong conclusion is specific: “Candidate B is recommended because it meets the 1500 N load and 0.15 m lift requirements, stays below 800 kPa, and uses the lowest input force among our feasible trials.” A stronger conclusion also identifies uncertainty, explains the force-versus-travel trade-off, and states that actual equipment safety requires further professional engineering analysis.

Seven worked design examples

Each example shows the intermediate steps required to turn formulas into an engineering decision. Work the example yourself before checking the calculation and interpretation.

Worked example 1 · Calculate piston areas and ratio

Situation: Input diameter 20 mm and output diameter 60 mm.

  1. Convert diameters to 0.020 m and 0.060 m.
  2. Input area A₁ = π(0.020)²/4 = 0.00031416 m².
  3. Output area A₂ = π(0.060)²/4 = 0.00282743 m².
  4. Ratio R = (60/20)² = 9.

Interpretation: Diameter must be squared to determine area ratio.

Worked example 2 · Pressure and ideal force

Situation: Input force 200 N with 20/60 mm pistons.

  1. Pressure = 200/0.00031416 ≈ 636,620 Pa.
  2. Convert to 636.6 kPa.
  3. Ideal output force = 200 × 9 = 1800 N.
  4. Compare pressure with the specified limit separately.

Interpretation: Equal transmitted pressure does not mean equal force.

Worked example 3 · Required travel

Situation: Area ratio 9, input stroke 1.35 m, minimum lift 0.15 m.

  1. Use s₂ = s₁/R.
  2. Substitute 1.35/9.
  3. Output travel is 0.150 m.
  4. Travel margin = 0.150 − 0.150 = 0 m.

Interpretation: Exact compliance has no travel margin.

Worked example 4 · Efficiency and work

Situation: 200 N input, ratio 9, 1.35 m stroke, 85% efficiency.

  1. Ideal force = 200 × 9 = 1800 N.
  2. Useful force = 0.85 × 1800 = 1530 N.
  3. Output travel = 1.35/9 = 0.15 m.
  4. Input work = 200 × 1.35 = 270 J.
  5. Useful work = 1530 × 0.15 = 229.5 J.

Interpretation: Useful work is 85% of input work.

Worked example 5 · Inverse force and stroke

Situation: Required load 1500 N, ratio 9, efficiency 90%, lift 0.15 m.

  1. Minimum input force = 1500/(0.90 × 9).
  2. Result is 185.19 N.
  3. Required input stroke = 9 × 0.15.
  4. Result is 1.35 m.
  5. Check pressure and stroke limits before declaring feasibility.

Interpretation: Solve from the requirement, then test every constraint.

Worked example 6 · Diagnose a failure

Situation: Diameters 15/75 mm, 90 N input, 1.50 m stroke, 90% efficiency.

  1. Ratio = (75/15)² = 25.
  2. Useful force = 0.90 × 90 × 25 = 2025 N; load passes.
  3. Output travel = 1.50/25 = 0.060 m; 0.15 m lift fails.
  4. Input pressure ≈ 509.3 kPa; 800 kPa limit passes.
  5. Reject: output travel is insufficient.

Interpretation: Large force does not guarantee usable lift.

Worked example 7 · Compare two designs

Situation: A: 20/60 mm; B: 25/75 mm. Both use 190 N input, 1.40 m stroke, 90% efficiency.

  1. Both ratios = 9.
  2. Both useful forces = 0.90 × 190 × 9 = 1539 N.
  3. Both output travels = 1.40/9 ≈ 0.1556 m.
  4. A pressure ≈ 604.8 kPa; B pressure ≈ 387.1 kPa.
  5. Both pass 1500 N, 0.15 m, 800 kPa, and 1.50 m constraints.
  6. Recommend B on greater pressure margin when input force ties.

Interpretation: State the secondary criterion when candidates tie.

Phase A · Explore

Hold input force and input piston diameter constant. Double output diameter and predict the change in ideal force, useful force, and output travel. Record your observations and explain the square relationship between diameter and area. Next, increase input force while holding geometry constant. Explain why pressure rises but area ratio does not.

Change efficiency from 100% to 90% to 80%. Compare useful output force and work while keeping geometry and stroke fixed. Identify which values remain unchanged and why. Verify that useful output work is no greater than input work.

Phase B · Meet requirements

Reset to the default challenge. Save the starting candidate, then deliberately produce a design that passes load but fails travel. Save it and record its negative travel margin. Next produce a configuration that fails pressure or load, and explain the cause using an equation.

Improve a failing candidate by changing piston diameters, input force, or input stroke. Recalculate every requirement after each change. Explain why a single modification can solve one problem while creating another.

Phase C · Compare and optimize

Save at least three distinct candidates, including two feasible ones if possible. The table compares recorded performance. The best-observed result identifies the lowest input force among feasible candidates using the current specifications; it does not prove the global optimum.

Try reducing input force in 5 N increments until a constraint fails, then restore the smallest passing value. Report the search resolution. Compare the pressure and travel margins of the feasible candidates and decide whether a slightly higher input force is justified by greater margin.

Phase D · Recommend

Write a short engineering recommendation with chosen diameters, input force, stroke, efficiency, useful force, output travel, pressure, and all four margins. Identify a rejected candidate and explain the failure. Include a sensitivity test at a lower efficiency and discuss whether the recommendation changes.

Finish by identifying at least three unmodeled real-world concerns, such as hose ratings, cylinder strength, seal leakage, relief valves, stability, or uncontrolled load movement. State that the configuration passes modeled constraints only. Copy your recommendation for submission according to your teacher's directions.

Engineering analysis and common errors

Strong force but insufficient travel

A high area ratio increases force while reducing output travel. If lift height fails, reducing ratio or increasing input stroke may help. Simply adding input force will not correct a geometric travel failure.

Excessive pressure

Input pressure is F₁/A₁. A small input piston at high force can exceed the limit. Calculate area from diameter in meters and check pressure separately from output force.

Ignoring efficiency

The lab's useful output force is ηF₁R, not F₁R. A design that passes at 100% efficiency may fail at 85%. Test assumptions and report them.

Optimizing before checking constraints

A low-force design that fails travel or pressure is not feasible. Rank only passing candidates using the declared objective.

Confusing model feasibility with physical safety

This simulation omits structural strength, fluid dynamics, valves, leakage, and stability. A PASS is not a safety certification. Real hydraulic equipment requires professional design and rated components.

Unit B2 synthesis

Hydraulic lifts transmit pressure through a liquid. Piston area ratio determines ideal force multiplication, while volume conservation determines the corresponding reduction in output travel. Ideal work is conserved; the simplified efficiency model accounts for reduced useful work.

A successful design meets load, travel, pressure, and input-stroke constraints simultaneously. Optimization means comparing feasible designs using a stated objective and numerical evidence. Record margins, assumptions, and limitations alongside the selected configuration.

Across Unit B2, density, pressure, hydrostatics, hydraulics, and buoyancy provide complementary tools for understanding fluid forces. In Unit B3, you will extend these ideas to moving fluids and engineering flow systems.
