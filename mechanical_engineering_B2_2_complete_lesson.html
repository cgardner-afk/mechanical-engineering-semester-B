Unit B2 · Quantitative fluid engineering

Readiness checkpoint and retrieval

Recall from B2.1 that both liquids and gases are fluids. Density is mass divided by volume, pressure is normal force divided by area, and viscosity measures resistance to shear motion. Do not confuse these three properties. In Unit B1, you learned to substitute values into mechanical equations; the same disciplined method applies here.

Check your unit conversions before continuing: 500 g = 0.500 kg; 250 mL = 0.000250 m³; 30 cm² = 0.0030 m²; 300 kPa = 300,000 Pa. If any answer is surprising, write the conversion as a fraction and cancel the units. The area conversion is especially important because a squared unit requires a squared conversion factor.

For a simple algebra check, start with y = a/b. Multiplying by b gives a = yb, and dividing a by y gives b = a/y. Apply this reasoning when finding mass, volume, force, or area. Always substitute your calculated value into the original equation to verify it.

Why calculate fluid properties?

A mechanical engineer needs more than a description of how a fluid looks or feels. A hydraulic reservoir has to hold a specified amount of fluid without adding too much weight. A piston has to produce force without exceeding the rated pressure of its components. These questions require density, specific gravity, and pressure. Each property describes something different, and each is calculated from measurements.

Density connects mass and volume. Specific gravity compares a fluid's density with a reference. Pressure connects normal force and area. This lesson moves from the conceptual definitions in B2.1 to numerical engineering decisions. Your work should show a formula, consistent units, substitutions, a result, and a reasonableness check. A correct number without an explanation is not a complete engineering solution.

Mass, volume, and density

Mass is measured in kilograms and volume in cubic meters in the SI system. Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V. A 1.8 kg fluid sample occupying 0.002 m³ has density 900 kg/m³. The number describes the mass in each cubic meter of that uniform fluid under the stated conditions. It does not describe how difficult the fluid is to pump.

Density is an intensive property: if you take twice as much of a uniform fluid at the same conditions, mass and volume double together while density remains unchanged. Mass and volume are extensive properties because they depend on sample size. A large quantity of a low-density fluid can have more total mass than a small quantity of a high-density fluid. Always compare equal volumes when comparing density.

Rearranging the density formula

From ρ = m/V, multiply both sides by V to obtain m = ρV. If mass and density are known, divide by density to obtain V = m/ρ. These equations are algebraic rearrangements of one relationship, not separate scientific laws. A tank designer uses m = ρV to estimate the mass of a full tank; a technician uses V = m/ρ to estimate the volume of a weighed sample.

A dimensional check helps verify each arrangement. Density in kg/m³ multiplied by volume in m³ yields kg. Mass in kg divided by density in kg/m³ yields m³. If the resulting units do not match the requested quantity, revisit the rearrangement or conversion. Also check magnitude: a small sample should not occupy hundreds of cubic meters without an extraordinary reason.

Unit conversions for fluid calculations

One liter is 0.001 m³; one milliliter is 0.000001 m³, and one cubic centimeter equals one milliliter. One kilogram equals 1000 grams. Thus 750 mL = 0.000750 m³ and 450 g = 0.450 kg. A convenient density equivalence is 1 g/mL = 1000 kg/m³. For example, 0.85 g/mL is 850 kg/m³.

Conversion factors should be written with units so that unwanted units cancel. To convert 250 mL to m³, multiply 250 mL by (0.000001 m³/1 mL). The answer is 0.000250 m³. A common error is treating 250 mL as 0.250 m³, which changes density by a factor of 1000. Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round only the final result to a precision supported by the measurements.

Measurement precision and uncertainty

Real instruments have limited precision. A balance may resolve tenths of a gram, while a graduated cylinder may have markings every milliliter. The curved surface of a liquid is called the meniscus; read it at eye level using the appropriate convention for the liquid and instrument. If the liquid is weighed in a container, use the balance's tare function or subtract the empty container mass.

Suppose you measure 120 g and 100 mL, giving 1.20 g/mL. If the actual volume were 102 mL, the calculated density would be about 1.18 g/mL. This illustrates how a small volume error affects the answer. Record measurements with units and appropriate precision. Our laboratory generates mass from a preset density and volume, so it illustrates calculation rather than measuring physical uncertainty.

Specific gravity

Specific gravity (SG) is a density ratio: SG = ρsample/ρreference. For liquids, the reference is often water at a specified condition. If we use approximate water density 1000 kg/m³ and a sample density 850 kg/m³, SG = 0.85. Because both densities have the same units, the units cancel and SG is dimensionless.

The reference density must be stated. A value below one means the sample is less dense than the reference; above one means more dense. It does not tell you viscosity or whether two liquids will form layers. Separate stable layers require the liquids not to mix. In the virtual comparator we will explicitly assume immiscible fluids when interpreting possible layer order.

Density is not viscosity

Viscosity describes resistance to relative movement of fluid layers, not mass per volume. Honey often flows slowly because of its viscosity, but that observation alone does not give its density. Two fluids can have similar densities and very different viscosities. Engineers might use density to estimate tank mass and viscosity to estimate pumping resistance.

When a question asks for density, identify mass and volume. When it asks about flow resistance, look for viscosity data. Do not substitute a descriptive word such as thick for a measured density. Correct engineering communication distinguishes what was measured, what was calculated, and what was only observed.

Pressure, force, and area

Pressure is normal force per unit area. For uniform pressure, P = F/A. The SI unit is the pascal (Pa), equal to one newton per square meter. If 200 N acts uniformly across 0.020 m², pressure is 10,000 Pa or 10 kPa. A smaller area under the same force produces greater pressure.

Pressure is a scalar quantity, while force has a direction. A fluid pressure acting on a surface produces a force perpendicular to that surface. When pressure varies across a surface, dividing total normal force by area gives average pressure. In B2.3, you will learn why pressure changes with depth in a stationary liquid. Here we focus on uniform or average pressure with explicitly stated forces and areas.

Rearranging pressure equations

From P = F/A, rearrange to F = PA or A = F/P. These forms let you estimate piston force or select an area that does not exceed a modeled pressure limit. If a uniform 400 kPa pressure acts on 0.005 m², the force is 400,000 Pa × 0.005 m² = 2000 N.

Unit conversion is especially important with area. Since 1 cm = 0.01 m, 1 cm² = 0.0001 m², not 0.01 m². Thus 20 cm² = 0.0020 m². Also, 1 kPa = 1000 Pa and 1 MPa = 1,000,000 Pa. Failing to square the length conversion can make a pressure answer wrong by a factor of 100.

Gauge and absolute pressure

Gauge pressure is measured relative to surrounding atmospheric pressure. Absolute pressure is measured relative to a vacuum. Their relationship is Pabsolute = Pgauge + Patmospheric. At an atmospheric pressure of approximately 101 kPa, a gauge pressure of 200 kPa corresponds to about 301 kPa absolute.

The virtual piston lab uses the applied normal force divided by piston area as an idealized pressure difference, not as a prediction of absolute fluid pressure everywhere. Pressure values must be labeled with their reference. A pressure gauge can read zero even though the fluid is at atmospheric absolute pressure. This distinction will matter in later fluid-system lessons.

Engineering design constraints

A design constraint is a requirement that a design must satisfy, such as a maximum pressure, minimum force, or permitted piston size. In our laboratory, the piston must supply at least 1800 N while the modeled pressure remains at or below 400 kPa. Increasing piston area reduces the pressure needed for a fixed force, but may increase actuator size and fluid-volume requirements.

Real hydraulic design involves more than satisfying two simplified inequalities. Engineers must check component ratings, material strength, seals, dynamic loads, fluid compatibility, and appropriate safety factors. High-pressure fluid leaks can cause serious injury. A virtual pass result is evidence that a simple mathematical model meets its stated constraints, not certification of real equipment.

Checking a mathematical model

The equations in this lesson assume a reasonably uniform fluid density and a stated reference condition. The pressure equation assumes the normal force is distributed as described. A mixture with varying density or a surface with strongly varying pressure may require more advanced analysis. A reliable calculation identifies the assumptions as well as the numbers.

When evaluating an answer, check three things. First, do the units cancel correctly? Second, is the magnitude plausible? Third, does the trend match physics—for example, does a larger piston area lower pressure at constant force? These habits connect fluid calculations with the quantitative reasoning used earlier for gears, torque, and power.

Seven worked engineering examples

Follow each calculation from the givens through the formula and numerical substitution. The final interpretation connects the result to a physical decision. When a quantity is not in SI units, convert it before applying the formula.

Worked example 1 · Find density

Situation: A sample has mass 1.35 kg and volume 0.00150 m³.

  1. Choose ρ = m/V.
  2. Substitute: 1.35/0.00150.
  3. Calculate 900 kg/m³.

Interpretation: This is slightly less than reference water density.

Worked example 2 · Convert laboratory measurements

Situation: A 250 mL sample has mass 215 g.

  1. 215 g = 0.215 kg.
  2. 250 mL = 0.000250 m³.
  3. ρ = 0.215/0.000250 = 860 kg/m³.

Interpretation: Check: 0.86 g/mL = 860 kg/m³.

Worked example 3 · Find mass

Situation: A tank contains 0.075 m³ of fluid at 920 kg/m³.

  1. Rearrange m = ρV.
  2. Substitute 920 × 0.075.
  3. Calculate 69.0 kg.

Interpretation: The result is fluid mass, not weight in newtons.

Worked example 4 · Find specific gravity

Situation: Fluid density is 780 kg/m³; reference water density is 1000 kg/m³.

  1. Choose SG = ρsample/ρreference.
  2. Substitute 780/1000.
  3. Calculate 0.780.

Interpretation: The ratio has no units and does not measure viscosity.

Worked example 5 · Find pressure

Situation: A piston receives 600 N over 0.0030 m².

  1. Choose P = F/A.
  2. Substitute 600/0.0030.
  3. Calculate 200,000 Pa = 200 kPa.

Interpretation: Check by multiplying pressure by area.

Worked example 6 · Find area

Situation: A piston must exert 1500 N at a modeled pressure of at most 300 kPa.

  1. Convert 300 kPa = 300,000 Pa.
  2. Rearrange A = F/P.
  3. Substitute 1500/300,000.
  4. Calculate 0.0050 m² = 50 cm².

Interpretation: This is a minimum idealized area at the pressure limit.

Worked example 7 · Combined engineering calculation

Situation: A 40 L tank holds oil at 850 kg/m³. A 0.004 m² piston receives 1200 N.

  1. 40 L = 0.040 m³.
  2. m = 850 × 0.040 = 34 kg.
  3. P = 1200/0.004 = 300 kPa.
  4. SG = 850/1000 = 0.85.

Interpretation: The three results describe mass, pressure, and a density ratio.

Engineering analysis and common errors

Density is not mass

Doubling the amount of uniform fluid doubles mass and volume, not density. If your simulated density changes with sample size, inspect the units and arithmetic.

Density is not viscosity

A fluid's resistance to flow cannot be inferred from its mass per volume. Honey may be both dense and viscous, but those properties answer different questions.

Pressure is not total force

The same force produces different pressure when area changes. At fixed force, a larger area means smaller pressure. At fixed area, larger force means larger pressure.

Area conversion is squared

Since 1 cm = 0.01 m, 1 cm² = 0.0001 m². Treating 1 cm² as 0.01 m² makes a calculated pressure wrong by a factor of 100.

Model pass does not mean safe construction

The lab checks only two mathematical inequalities. Real equipment requires pressure-rated parts, materials checks, seals, appropriate safety factors, and protection against high-pressure fluid injection hazards.

Seven independent practice problems

Solve each problem before expanding the answer. These are genuine numbered step-by-step solutions, not condensed answer keys.

Practice 1

A 3.6 kg liquid sample occupies 0.0040 m³. Find density.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. m = 3.6 kg; V = 0.0040 m³.
  2. Select equation. ρ = m/V.
  3. Substitute. ρ = 3.6/0.0040.
  4. Calculate. 900 kg/m³.
  5. Check. 900 × 0.0040 = 3.6 kg, as given.

Practice 2

A fluid has mass 480 g and volume 600 mL. Find density in kg/m³.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. 480 g and 600 mL.
  2. Convert mass. 480 g = 0.480 kg.
  3. Convert volume. 600 mL = 0.000600 m³.
  4. Calculate. ρ = 0.480/0.000600 = 800 kg/m³.
  5. Check. 0.8 g/mL is also 800 kg/m³.

Practice 3

A tank contains 0.060 m³ of liquid with density 1050 kg/m³. Find mass.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. ρ = 1050 kg/m³; V = 0.060 m³.
  2. Rearrange. m = ρV.
  3. Substitute. m = 1050 × 0.060.
  4. Calculate. 63 kg.
  5. Check units. kg/m³ × m³ = kg.

Practice 4

A 2.7 kg fluid sample has density 900 kg/m³. Find volume in liters.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. m = 2.7 kg; ρ = 900 kg/m³.
  2. Rearrange. V = m/ρ.
  3. Calculate. V = 2.7/900 = 0.0030 m³.
  4. Convert. 0.0030 m³ = 3.0 L.
  5. Check. 900 × 0.0030 = 2.7 kg.

Practice 5

Find SG of a liquid at 1120 kg/m³ relative to water at 1000 kg/m³.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify reference. ρsample = 1120; ρreference = 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Select equation. SG = ρsample/ρreference.
  3. Substitute. SG = 1120/1000.
  4. Calculate. 1.12.
  5. Interpret. The fluid is 12% denser than reference water; SG has no units.

Practice 6

A piston receives 900 N over 30 cm². Find pressure in kPa.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. F = 900 N; A = 30 cm².
  2. Convert area. 30 cm² = 0.0030 m².
  3. Select equation. P = F/A.
  4. Calculate. 900/0.0030 = 300,000 Pa.
  5. Convert and check. 300 kPa; 300,000 × 0.0030 = 900 N.

Practice 7

A design needs 1800 N at no more than 400 kPa. Find minimum piston area and test 40 cm².

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify constraints. F ≥ 1800 N; P ≤ 400,000 Pa.
  2. Rearrange. A = F/P.
  3. Calculate. Amin = 1800/400,000 = 0.0045 m².
  4. Convert. Minimum area is 45 cm².
  5. Test proposal. 40 cm² = 0.0040 m²; P = 1800/0.0040 = 450 kPa.
  6. Decide. 40 cm² fails because 450 kPa exceeds 400 kPa.

Lesson summary and next steps

Density is mass per volume. Specific gravity is a dimensionless ratio of a sample density to a stated reference density. Pressure is normal force divided by area. These equations can be rearranged to calculate mass, volume, force, and area. Each answer needs consistent units and a physical interpretation.

For reliable calculations, convert grams to kilograms, milliliters to cubic meters, kilopascals to pascals, and square centimeters to square meters. Check results using dimensional analysis and expected physical trends. A larger piston area lowers pressure for a fixed force in the simplified model.

The laboratory illustrates how engineers compare fluid samples and satisfy modeled force and pressure constraints. In B2.3 you will combine fluid density with gravity and depth to calculate hydrostatic pressure.
