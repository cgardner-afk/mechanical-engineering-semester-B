Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  1. Identify driving gears, driven gears, and idler gears in a gear train.
  2. Calculate output rotational speed from gear tooth counts.
  3. Calculate ideal output torque from gear ratios.
  4. Predict rotational direction through external gear meshes.
  5. Analyze compound gear trains and justify gear selections.
Mechanical Engineering B · Unit B1

Math and vocabulary checkpoint

Recall that rpm means revolutions per minute. A shaft turning at 600 rpm completes 600 revolutions in one minute. Lesson B1.2 introduced angular speed; B1.3 explained torque and power. This lesson combines those ideas in a mechanical transmission.

A ratio is a comparison. If a gear has 20 teeth and another has 40, 20/40 = 1/2. Multiply 600 rpm by 1/2 to obtain 300 rpm. When rearranging ratios, first predict whether the follower should turn faster or slower. A calculation that contradicts that prediction probably has the ratio inverted.

An ideal, lossless machine does not dissipate useful mechanical energy. In the real world, gears and bearings generate heat and noise. We calculate ideal gear relationships first, then discuss how losses affect design.

Engineering concepts

Essential gear-train formulas · Quick reference

Use these equations for ideal, no-slip external spur gears. Identify the input and output before substituting numbers. The formulas calculate speed and torque magnitudes; determine direction by counting external meshes.

Output rotational speed
Nout = Nin × (Zin / Zout)
Speed reduction ratio
R = Zout / Zin = Nin / Nout
Ideal output torque
τout = τin × (Zout / Zin)
Compound train output speed
N4 = N1 × (Z1 / Z2) × (Z3 / Z4)
Ideal rotational power
P = τω

Symbols: N = rotational speed (rpm); Z = tooth count; τ (tau) = torque (N·m); R = reduction ratio (no units); P = power (W); ω (omega) = angular speed (rad/s). Subscripts in and out identify the input and output. In the compound formula, gears 2 and 3 are rigidly fixed to one shaft.

Direction rule: Each external gear mesh reverses direction. An odd number of meshes reverses the input direction; an even number preserves it. All directions must be judged from the same viewing side.

Gear anatomy and purpose

A gear is a toothed wheel that transmits rotational motion and torque. The driver supplies input motion; the follower receives it. Spur gears have teeth parallel to the shaft axis. Their pitch circles are imaginary reference circles that roll together in an ideal model. Tooth count is denoted Z. Gears enable a motor to operate at one speed while a load operates at another.

The shaft supports the gear and transmits torque. A gear with more teeth is normally larger when tooth size is the same. Pitch diameter, tooth geometry, lubrication, alignment, and bearing strength matter in real machines. For this introductory lesson, we assume compatible external spur gears with parallel shafts and negligible losses.

Why tooth counts determine speed

At the point where two pitch circles meet, their tangential speeds must match in an ideal no-slip model. The smaller gear must complete more revolutions to cover the same surface distance. Thus a small driver turning a large follower reduces speed. A large driver turning a small follower increases speed.

Let N mean rotational speed in revolutions per minute (rpm). Subscript in means input; out means output. Then Nout = Nin × Zin / Zout. The result gives speed magnitude, not direction. A 20-tooth driver at 600 rpm turning a 40-tooth follower produces 600 × 20/40 = 300 rpm. Predict slower rotation before calculating, then use that prediction to catch an inverted ratio.

Rotation direction

Two externally meshing gears turn in opposite directions when observed from the same side of their parallel shafts. A clockwise driver makes its follower counterclockwise. Count the meshes to determine the final direction: one reverses it, two restore it, and three reverse it again.

An idler is an intermediate gear between input and output. In a three-gear simple train, the idler creates a second external mesh. Consequently, the first and third gears turn in the same direction. The idler tooth count cancels out of the overall ideal speed ratio, although its size affects physical layout and its extra contacts add real losses.

Gear ratios and torque

Define the reduction ratio R = Zout/Zin. The ratio has no units because it compares tooth counts. A 3:1 reduction means three input turns for one output turn. The output speed is one-third of input speed. The ideal output torque is three times the input torque.

The Greek letter τ, pronounced tau, represents torque in newton-meters. Ideal output torque is τout = τin × Zout/Zin. This follows from conservation of power: rotational power P = τω, where ω is angular speed. If angular speed falls while ideal power remains unchanged, torque rises proportionally. No energy is created. Real gear meshes have losses, so actual useful output torque is lower.

Simple and compound trains

A simple gear train has one gear on each shaft. Intermediate idlers affect direction and spacing, but the ideal speed ratio between the first and last gears depends only on their tooth counts. For example, a 20-tooth driver, 30-tooth idler, and 60-tooth follower yield a 3:1 reduction regardless of the idler's 30 teeth.

A compound gear train places two or more gears rigidly on the same intermediate shaft. These attached gears share angular speed, even if their tooth counts differ. Each meshing pair forms a stage. Multiply the stage ratios to calculate the overall ratio. A 3:1 stage followed by a 2:1 stage produces a 6:1 reduction. Do not cancel the two different gears fixed to the intermediate shaft as though they were an idler.

Engineering design trade-offs

Start with a required output speed, torque, and direction. A motor running at 1200 rpm that must drive a load at 300 rpm needs an ideal 4:1 reduction. One possible gear pair is 20 teeth driving 80 teeth. A two-stage 2:1 plus 2:1 compound arrangement gives the same total ratio with different packaging constraints.

Always evaluate direction separately from the speed ratio. Adding an idler can reverse the output direction relative to a direct pair without changing the ideal ratio. Higher reduction increases torque but lowers speed. Gear diameter, tooth strength, center spacing, efficiency, and noise impose practical constraints. A design meeting a minimum torque exactly under ideal assumptions may fail once losses and starting loads are included.

Engineering case study: conveyor reducer

Consider a conveyor moving heavy cartons through a packaging line. The motor may be efficient at a relatively high shaft speed, while the conveyor roller must turn slowly enough to prevent packages from tipping. A gear reducer connects these different operating requirements. If a motor turns at 900 rpm and the roller needs 150 rpm, the overall speed reduction must be 6:1. The engineer may choose one large reduction stage or distribute the ratio across two smaller stages. A two-stage 3:1 and 2:1 train produces the same theoretical output speed but changes the arrangement of shafts and gears.

The speed requirement alone is not enough to approve a design. The roller also needs sufficient torque to overcome friction and accelerate the cartons. If the motor provides 5 N·m, a lossless 6:1 reducer predicts 30 N·m at the output. Actual output torque is lower because of mechanical losses. Engineers must also check whether the gear teeth can withstand the contact forces, whether the shafts can resist twisting, and whether bearings can support the loads. The virtual laboratory focuses on ratios, but the final design requires mechanical strength and operating data.

Why the pitch circle model works

The pitch circle is not usually a visible line on a real gear. It is a geometric reference that allows engineers to treat the gear pair as if two circles were rolling against one another without slipping. At the pitch point, the surface speed of one circle matches the surface speed of the other. Since tangential speed equals angular speed multiplied by radius, the larger circle rotates more slowly. For compatible gears, the pitch diameter is proportional to the number of teeth. Substituting tooth count for diameter gives the speed equation used throughout this lesson.

A gear's individual teeth do not act like simple blocks that push one another in a straight line. Their profiles are shaped to transfer motion smoothly as contact moves along the tooth surface. An involute profile is common in modern gears because it maintains an approximately constant speed ratio despite changing contact position. Students do not need to calculate involute geometry here, but should recognize that the ideal ratio is supported by deliberate tooth design. Poorly matched or damaged teeth can cause noise, wear, and unreliable motion.

Checking a compound train carefully

When reading a compound train diagram, first mark which gears actually mesh and which gears are fixed together. Two gears drawn next to one another are not necessarily meshing. A shared shaft is a rigid mechanical connection: both gears have the same angular speed and direction. A mesh is a toothed contact: the two gears have related speeds and opposite directions. Confusing these connections is one of the most common reasons students obtain incorrect compound ratios.

A reliable method is to label the gears 1, 2, 3, and 4. Write the first stage as N2 = N1 × Z1/Z2. Because gears 2 and 3 share a shaft, N3 = N2. Write the second stage as N4 = N3 × Z3/Z4. Combining them gives N4 = N1 × (Z1/Z2) × (Z3/Z4). This derivation explains why both ratios are multiplied and why the two intermediate tooth counts do not cancel unless the gears happen to have the same number of teeth. It also helps you locate arithmetic errors when checking a solution.

Gear selection and real-world constraints

The same overall ratio can be achieved with several tooth-count combinations, but those combinations are not mechanically interchangeable. A very small gear may have weaker teeth or wear more quickly under heavy load. A very large gear may require a housing too wide for the machine. Multiple stages add shafts and bearings, increasing complexity, cost, and opportunities for power loss. Engineers often balance these competing effects rather than choosing the mathematically simplest ratio.

Direction is another design constraint. A conveyor may need its output shaft to rotate clockwise to move material in the intended direction. A direct external gear pair reverses direction, so the motor's direction, an added idler, or a different arrangement may be necessary. In a compact robot, gear trains may also need to fit around other components. A useful engineering report therefore states the chosen ratio, predicts output speed and ideal torque, traces direction, and identifies what additional information is needed to confirm that the design will work safely.

Reading and checking laboratory evidence

A virtual laboratory is most useful when each trial tests one prediction. For example, hold the input gear at 20 teeth and input speed at 600 rpm while increasing the follower from 40 to 60 teeth. The output speed should change from 300 rpm to 200 rpm. The ideal torque multiplier should increase from 2 to 3. Record both results and explain the pattern instead of merely copying the displayed values. Then hold tooth counts constant and double the input rpm. The output rpm should double while the ratio stays unchanged.

For the idler investigation, change only the idler tooth count and leave the first and last gear tooth counts fixed. You should see the same output speed each time, because the idler tooth count appears once in a numerator and once in a denominator. The direction should remain the same as the input because the train still has two external meshes. For a compound investigation, changing the tooth count on a gear attached to the intermediate shaft can change the second-stage ratio even though the shaft speed itself is shared. These patterns help distinguish mathematical relationships from accidental correlations.

Seven worked engineering examples

In each example, identify input and output tooth counts, select the relationship, calculate, and interpret speed, torque, or direction. All examples use ideal external gears.

Worked example 1 · Reduction

Given and find: A 16-tooth driver turns at 720 rpm and meshes with a 48-tooth follower.

Equation, substitution, and result: Nout = 720 × 16/48 = 240 rpm. The larger follower is slower and turns in the opposite direction.

Worked example 2 · Speed increase

Given and find: A 60-tooth driver at 180 rpm drives a 20-tooth follower.

Equation, substitution, and result: Nout = 180 × 60/20 = 540 rpm. Output speed triples while ideal torque becomes one-third.

Worked example 3 · Direction

Given and find: Three external gears mesh in a row. The first turns clockwise.

Equation, substitution, and result: First clockwise → second counterclockwise → third clockwise. Two meshes reverse direction twice.

Worked example 4 · Idler cancellation

Given and find: A 24-tooth input at 500 rpm drives a 36-tooth idler and then a 60-tooth output.

Equation, substitution, and result: Nout = 500 × (24/36) × (36/60) = 200 rpm. The idler count cancels; output direction matches input.

Worked example 5 · Ideal torque

Given and find: An 8 N·m input turns a 15-tooth driver meshing with a 45-tooth follower.

Equation, substitution, and result: τout = 8 × 45/15 = 24 N·m. Ideal torque triples as speed falls to one-third.

Worked example 6 · Compound reduction

Given and find: A 12-tooth input drives a 36-tooth gear. A 16-tooth gear on the same intermediate shaft drives a 48-tooth output at 900 rpm input.

Equation, substitution, and result: Nout = 900 × (12/36) × (16/48) = 100 rpm. Total reduction is 9:1; two external meshes restore direction.

Worked example 7 · Design constraint

Given and find: A motor provides 6 N·m at 1200 rpm; a load needs 400 rpm and at least 18 N·m ideally.

Equation, substitution, and result: Use a 20-tooth driver and 60-tooth follower: Nout = 400 rpm; τout = 18 N·m. The ideal result meets both targets, but a real design needs margin.

Engineering analysis and common errors

A common error is to invert the tooth-count ratio. If the follower has more teeth, output speed must decrease. Check that physical prediction before accepting a numerical answer. The correct speed formula is Nout = Nin × Zin/Zout; the torque multiplier is the reciprocal.

Another error is to use the speed magnitude equation to infer direction. The magnitude calculation does not carry direction. Count the external meshes: odd counts reverse direction; even counts restore it. Always keep the same viewing side for all shafts.

Do not confuse an idler with a compound intermediate shaft. A freely rotating idler cancels from the ideal overall speed ratio. Two different gears rigidly mounted to one shaft do not cancel; they form separate ratio stages. Finally, do not claim ideal torque multiplication creates power. Speed decreases in proportion to ideal torque gain.

Independent practice — check your work

Solve each visible question independently. Reveal only the solution you want to check. Include units and an engineering interpretation.

Practice 1

A 20-tooth driver at 840 rpm drives a 35-tooth follower. Find output speed.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. List the givens. Input speed 840 rpm; driver 20 teeth; follower 35 teeth.
  2. Select the gear-speed formula. Nout = Nin × Zdriver/Zfollower.
  3. Substitute. Nout = 840 × 20/35.
  4. Calculate. 20/35 = 4/7, so Nout = 480 rpm.
  5. Check. The follower has more teeth and should turn more slowly; 480 rpm is below 840 rpm.

Practice 2

A 48-tooth driver at 250 rpm drives a 16-tooth follower. Find output speed.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. List the givens. Input speed 250 rpm; driver 48 teeth; follower 16 teeth.
  2. Choose the formula. Nout = Nin × Zdriver/Zfollower.
  3. Substitute and simplify. Nout = 250 × 48/16 = 250 × 3.
  4. Calculate. Nout = 750 rpm.
  5. Check. The follower has one-third as many teeth, so it rotates three times as fast.

Practice 3

Three external gears mesh in a row. The first rotates counterclockwise. Determine the third gear's direction.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the starting direction. The first gear rotates counterclockwise.
  2. Apply the mesh-direction rule. Each pair of externally meshing gears reverses rotation.
  3. Trace the first mesh. First counterclockwise → second clockwise.
  4. Trace the second mesh. Second clockwise → third counterclockwise.
  5. Check. Two reversals restore the input direction. The third gear rotates counterclockwise.

Practice 4

A 22-tooth driver at 600 rpm meshes with a 33-tooth idler and a 44-tooth output. Find output speed.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the train. Driver 22 teeth, idler 33 teeth, output 44 teeth; input speed 600 rpm.
  2. Write both mesh ratios. Nout = 600 × (22/33) × (33/44).
  3. Cancel the idler factor. The 33-tooth factors cancel, leaving 600 × 22/44.
  4. Calculate. Nout = 600 × 0.5 = 300 rpm.
  5. Check. The output has twice the driver tooth count, so it rotates at half the speed; two external meshes also preserve direction.

Practice 5

A 10-tooth driver supplies 5 N·m to a 40-tooth follower. Find ideal output torque.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the quantities. Input torque 5 N·m, driver 10 teeth, follower 40 teeth; ideal lossless transmission.
  2. Select the ideal torque formula. τout = τin × Zfollower/Zdriver.
  3. Substitute. τout = 5 × 40/10 N·m.
  4. Calculate. τout = 5 × 4 = 20 N·m.
  5. Check. The 4:1 speed reduction gives a fourfold ideal torque increase, consistent with constant power in a lossless model.

Practice 6

Compound stages are 18→54 teeth and 20→40 teeth. Input speed is 960 rpm. Find output speed.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the stages. Stage 1: 18-tooth driver to 54-tooth follower. Stage 2: 20-tooth driver to 40-tooth follower. Input 960 rpm.
  2. Choose the compound speed equation. Nout = Nin × (Z1/Z2) × (Z3/Z4).
  3. Substitute. Nout = 960 × (18/54) × (20/40).
  4. Simplify and calculate. 960 × (1/3) × (1/2) = 160 rpm.
  5. Check. Stage reductions are 3:1 and 2:1, giving 6:1 overall; 960/6 = 160 rpm.

Practice 7

A motor at 900 rpm delivers 4 N·m; the load needs 300 rpm and 12 N·m ideally. Choose a gear pair and assess direction.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify requirements. Motor input 900 rpm and 4 N·m; ideal output target 300 rpm and 12 N·m.
  2. Find required reduction. R = 900/300 = 3:1.
  3. Choose tooth counts. Select a 20-tooth driver and 60-tooth follower; 60/20 = 3.
  4. Check speed. Nout = 900 × 20/60 = 300 rpm.
  5. Check ideal torque. τout = 4 × 60/20 = 12 N·m.
  6. Check direction and limitations. One external mesh reverses direction. The design meets ideal speed and torque targets, but real losses would reduce actual output torque.
Vocabulary & glossary

Vocabulary and key concepts

Select a term to expand its definition. Each definition opens independently, so you can consult vocabulary without leaving your place in the lesson.

Driver

The input gear supplying motion to the gear train.

Follower

The gear receiving motion from another gear; often the output gear.

Spur gear

A gear with straight teeth parallel to its rotation axis.

Pitch circle

An imaginary reference circle used to describe the effective rolling motion of meshing gears.

Tooth count (Z)

The number of teeth on a gear; tooth counts are dimensionless.

Rotational speed (N)

The number of revolutions per unit time, often given in revolutions per minute (rpm).

Reduction ratio (R)

For a single gear pair in this lesson, R = Zout/Zin; R greater than one indicates speed reduction.

Torque (τ)

A turning effect transmitted by a shaft, measured in newton-meters (N·m).

Idler gear

An intermediate gear that changes direction or spacing without changing the overall ideal ratio of a simple train.

Simple gear train

A train with one gear on each shaft; intermediate idler tooth counts cancel from the ideal overall ratio.

Compound gear train

A train with two gears rigidly mounted on at least one common shaft; stage ratios multiply.

Efficiency (η)

Useful output power divided by input power, often reported as a percentage.

External mesh

Engagement between teeth on the outer edges of two gears; each external mesh reverses rotation direction.

Lesson summary

Gear tooth counts determine ideal output speed: multiply input rpm by input teeth divided by output teeth. Each external mesh reverses rotation direction. An idler changes the direction sequence but cancels from the overall ideal speed ratio of a simple train.

Ideal output torque changes inversely with output speed. Compound gear trains multiply stage ratios because paired gears on one intermediate shaft share angular speed. Engineering selection must consider speed, torque, direction, layout, efficiency, and safety margins.
