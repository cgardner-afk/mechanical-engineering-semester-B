Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Conservation of energy as an engineering rule

The First Law of Thermodynamics expresses conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, although it can cross a system boundary or change form. In Lesson B5.1, you identified the system, its surroundings, and the types of transfer crossing its boundary. Now you will quantify those transfers. If energy enters a system faster than it leaves, the amount of energy stored within the system increases. If more leaves than enters, stored energy decreases.

An energy balance is a systematic accounting of energy transfers and storage. The broad idea is energy in minus energy out equals the change in stored energy. A balance does not imply that energy always stays in one place; it explains where energy goes. For a closed system, heat and work are the main boundary transfers. For an open system, flowing matter also transports energy. The first task in every problem is to identify the system boundary and the relevant transfers.

2. Closed systems and sign conventions

A closed system does not exchange mass with its surroundings. It may still exchange heat transfer and work transfer. We use the convention Q positive when heat enters the system and W positive when the system does work on its surroundings. The First Law is then ΔE = Q − W. The sign of each term tells a physical story: positive Q adds energy, while positive W removes energy because the system performs work.

If heat leaves the system, Q is negative. If the surroundings do work on the system, W is negative. This means a compressor doing work on a trapped gas can increase its energy even if no heat enters. Always write the sign convention before substituting numerical values. Some engineering references choose a different work convention, but either is valid if applied consistently. Here we will keep heat in positive and work out positive throughout the lesson.

ΔE = Q − W   ·   Q positive in   ·   W positive out

3. Total stored energy and internal energy

The total energy E of a system can be represented as the sum of internal energy U, kinetic energy KE, and potential energy PE. Internal energy reflects microscopic molecular behavior. Kinetic energy reflects bulk motion, while gravitational potential energy depends on elevation. For a mass m moving at speed v, KE = ½mv². Near Earth's surface, PE = mgz relative to a selected height reference.

Many stationary laboratory devices experience negligible changes in bulk speed and height. In those cases, ΔKE ≈ 0 and ΔPE ≈ 0, so ΔE ≈ ΔU. The simplified balance becomes ΔU = Q − W. This approximation is not automatically valid for a moving vehicle or elevated water reservoir. State why terms are neglected instead of treating internal energy and total energy as universally identical. A negative ΔU means internal energy decreased, not that internal energy itself is necessarily negative.

E = U + KE + PE   ·   KE = ½mv²   ·   PE = mgz

4. Solving energy balances step by step

A reliable solution begins by identifying the system and labeling the directions of heat and work. Next, list known quantities in joules or kilojoules. Choose the appropriate equation, substitute signed values, solve for the unknown, and interpret the sign. To find heat, rearrange ΔE = Q − W into Q = ΔE + W. To find work, use W = Q − ΔE. These are algebraic rearrangements of the same conservation principle.

Suppose a sealed container gains 900 J of energy while doing 250 J of work. Because the container's stored energy increases, ΔE = +900 J. The work is performed by the system, so W = +250 J. The heat input must be Q = 900 + 250 = 1150 J. A quick reasonableness check confirms that the system received enough heat both to store 900 J and to deliver 250 J as work. Such checks often reveal sign mistakes before they become design mistakes.

Q = ΔE + W   ·   W = Q − ΔE

5. Boundary work during expansion and compression

A gas inside a piston-cylinder device can push a piston outward. The moving piston transfers boundary work across the system boundary. For a process against constant external pressure, W = P(V₂ − V₁), where P is pressure, V₁ is initial volume, and V₂ is final volume. When volume increases, ΔV is positive and the gas does positive work. During compression, ΔV is negative and work done by the gas is negative.

Use consistent units. Pressure in pascals multiplied by volume change in cubic meters produces joules because 1 Pa·m³ = 1 J. Likewise, 1 kPa·m³ = 1 kJ. For example, a gas expanding by 0.020 m³ against 150 kPa performs 3.0 kJ of work. This equation assumes constant resisting pressure and a suitable quasi-static or externally specified process; it is not a universal formula for every pressure–volume path. A real piston also has friction and mechanical losses.

W = P(V₂ − V₁)   ·   1 kPa·m³ = 1 kJ

6. Heat, work, and thermal behavior

Heat and work are both ways of transferring energy, but they are not interchangeable descriptions. Heat is driven by a temperature difference; work is transferred through organized interactions such as force and displacement, shaft rotation, or electrical input. A sealed gas may gain internal energy when heated, when compressed, or through a combination of both. A gas that expands and performs work may cool if the work is not offset by sufficient heat input.

An adiabatic process has negligible heat transfer, so Q ≈ 0. For a closed adiabatic system with negligible changes in kinetic and potential energy, ΔU = −W. Expansion work performed by the gas reduces internal energy, while compression work performed on the gas raises internal energy. An adiabatic process is not necessarily an isothermal process, which has constant temperature. The two terms describe different conditions and should not be confused.

7. Open systems and steady-flow energy accounting

An open system permits matter to cross its boundary. A turbine receives high-energy fluid and may deliver shaft work, while a compressor receives shaft work to raise fluid pressure. A control volume is the region selected for this flow analysis. Flowing mass carries energy into and out of the control volume. The property enthalpy, h = u + Pv for a simple compressible substance, combines internal energy and flow-related energy per unit mass.

A complete steady-flow energy equation may include heat, shaft work, enthalpy, kinetic energy, and potential energy. For a simplified one-inlet, one-outlet steady-flow device with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes, q − w = h₂ − h₁, where q and w are energy transfers per unit mass. In this convention, q is positive into the device and w is positive out. If a heater adds 40 kJ/kg of heat and delivers no shaft work, the outlet enthalpy rises by 40 kJ/kg. This equation is a conceptual introduction; detailed property calculations will come later.

q − w = h₂ − h₁   (steady flow, negligible KE and PE changes)

8. Power and energy over time

Energy is measured in joules, while power is the rate of energy transfer, measured in watts. One watt equals one joule per second. A heater delivering 600 W for 120 seconds transfers 72,000 J, or 72 kJ, if its power remains constant. Engineers must not substitute watts directly into an equation expecting joules. To find transferred energy at constant power, multiply by elapsed time.

Power is important when comparing how quickly equipment can heat or cool a system. Two heaters may transfer the same total energy over different times. A 1.2 kW heater operating for 60 seconds transfers 72 kJ, the same as a 600 W heater operating for 120 seconds. This does not guarantee identical temperature changes because losses, materials, and process conditions may differ. The energy balance tracks transfers; further property information is needed to calculate temperature.

Energy transferred = power × elapsed time

9. Engineering constraints and limitations

A mathematically correct energy balance does not automatically prove a device is safe or efficient. An ideal piston-cylinder model may ignore friction, leakage, changing gas pressure, and heat lost through the wall. A thermal system can satisfy an energy requirement while exceeding pressure, temperature, volume, or equipment power limits. Engineers therefore evaluate multiple constraints together and document the assumptions behind their model.

The efficiency of a real machine relates useful output to supplied input, but the First Law alone does not determine the maximum possible efficiency. That topic will be developed in later lessons on heat engines and the Second Law. In this lesson, focus on conserving energy, tracking its direction, and explaining what additional information would be needed to predict real performance.

Seven Worked Examples

1. Energy gained by a sealed heater

Situation: A closed system receives 850 J of heat and performs 200 J of work. Determine its total energy change.

Q = +850 J; W = +200 J. ΔE = Q − W = 850 − 200 = +650 J.

Engineering interpretation: Stored energy increases because energy entering exceeds energy leaving.

2. Energy lost during cooling

Situation: A closed container loses 420 J of heat and does 90 J of work.

Q = −420 J; W = +90 J. ΔE = −420 − 90 = −510 J.

Engineering interpretation: Heat loss and work output both decrease stored energy.

3. Finding unknown heat input

Situation: A closed system gains 900 J of energy while doing 250 J of work.

Q = ΔE + W = 900 + 250 = 1150 J into the system.

Engineering interpretation: Heat input must supply both the stored increase and work output.

4. Finding work performed

Situation: A gas receives 600 J of heat while its internal energy increases by 175 J; KE and PE changes are negligible.

W = Q − ΔU = 600 − 175 = 425 J done by the gas.

Engineering interpretation: Part of the heat input is transferred outward as work.

5. Constant-pressure piston expansion

Situation: Gas expands from 0.030 m³ to 0.055 m³ against a constant external pressure of 160 kPa.

ΔV = 0.055 − 0.030 = 0.025 m³. W = 160(0.025) = 4.0 kJ.

Engineering interpretation: The positive work corresponds to outward piston movement.

6. Including motion and elevation

Situation: A 10 kg system gains 300 J internal energy, accelerates from rest to 4 m/s, and rises 2 m.

ΔKE = ½(10)(4²) = 80 J; ΔPE = 10(9.81)(2) = 196.2 J. ΔE = 300 + 80 + 196.2 = 576.2 J.

Engineering interpretation: Internal, kinetic, and potential changes all contribute.

7. Piston-cylinder design constraint

Situation: A piston expands by 0.018 m³ at constant external pressure 140 kPa. Heat input is 5.0 kJ. Find work and stored-energy change.

W = 140(0.018) = 2.52 kJ. ΔE = 5.0 − 2.52 = +2.48 kJ.

Engineering interpretation: The model must also be checked against pressure and volume limits.

Independent Practice

Solve each problem before opening its worked solution. Use g = 9.81 m/s² where needed.

Problem 1: A closed system receives 720 J of heat and performs 260 J of work. Find ΔE.

Worked solution: ΔE = 720 − 260 = +460 J.

Problem 2: A closed system loses 350 J of heat while 120 J of work is done on it. Find ΔE.

Worked solution: Q = −350 J; W = −120 J. ΔE = −350 − (−120) = −230 J.

Problem 3: A sealed system gains 450 J of energy and performs 180 J of work. Find Q.

Worked solution: Q = ΔE + W = 450 + 180 = +630 J.

Problem 4: A gas receives 1000 J of heat and gains 640 J of internal energy. Find work done by the gas.

Worked solution: W = Q − ΔU = 1000 − 640 = +360 J, assuming negligible KE and PE changes.

Problem 5: A piston compresses from 0.080 m³ to 0.050 m³ against 120 kPa constant pressure. Find work done by the gas.

Worked solution: W = 120(0.050 − 0.080) = −3.6 kJ. Negative means work is done on the gas.

Problem 6: A 5 kg system accelerates from 2 to 6 m/s and rises 1.5 m. Find the combined KE and PE increase.

Worked solution: ΔKE = ½(5)(36−4) = 80 J; ΔPE = 5(9.81)(1.5) = 73.575 J; combined = 153.575 J ≈ 153.6 J.

Problem 7: A steady-flow heater adds 55 kJ/kg of heat, performs no shaft work, and has negligible KE and PE changes. Find Δh.

Worked solution: q − w = h₂ − h₁; Δh = 55 − 0 = +55 kJ/kg.

Lesson Summary

The First Law of Thermodynamics is an expression of conservation of energy. For a closed system, ΔE = Q − W when heat into the system is positive and work done by the system is positive. Energy may be stored as internal, kinetic, or potential energy; when changes in motion and height are negligible, the balance simplifies to ΔU = Q − W. Negative heat or work values describe transfers opposite to the positive directions.

For constant external pressure, piston-cylinder boundary work is W = P(V₂ − V₁). Expansion produces positive work and compression negative work under our convention. Open systems also transport energy with flowing matter, requiring an extended energy balance. A good engineering calculation labels the boundary, states assumptions, uses consistent units, checks every constraint, and explains what the simplified model cannot predict.

ΔE = Q − W   ·   E = U + KE + PE
W = P(V₂ − V₁)   ·   1 kPa·m³ = 1 kJ
q − w = h₂ − h₁   (simplified steady-flow case)
