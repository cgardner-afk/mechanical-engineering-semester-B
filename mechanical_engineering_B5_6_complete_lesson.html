UNIT B5 · THERMODYNAMICS AND ENERGY SYSTEMS

Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Selecting the correct thermal machine

Mechanical engineers choose a thermal machine according to the useful service required. A heat engine receives heat from a hot source, delivers net mechanical work, and rejects remaining heat to a colder sink. A refrigerator consumes work to remove heat from a cold region. A heat pump consumes work to deliver heat to a warm region. These devices use the same thermodynamic principles but perform different tasks.

Consider three facilities. A generating station wants shaft power to drive an electric generator; it needs a heat engine. A food-storage room must remove heat to keep produce cold; it needs refrigeration. A house needs heat delivered indoors during winter; it may use a heat pump. It would be misleading to rank all three by one number without specifying the task. The engineer first identifies the desired output, then selects the appropriate device and performance metric.

2. Complete energy-flow diagrams

An energy-flow diagram identifies the chosen system boundary and shows every energy stream crossing it. For a heat engine, high-temperature heat input splits into useful net work and rejected heat. For a refrigerator, work input and heat absorbed from the cold region combine to form heat rejected to the warm region. For a heat pump, those same streams are present, but the useful output is the warm-side heat.

A Sankey diagram can display the streams with widths proportional to energy magnitudes. Suppose an engine receives 1000 kJ, delivers 350 kJ of work, and rejects 650 kJ. The outgoing streams must add to 1000 kJ. Suppose a heat pump receives 200 kJ of work and absorbs 600 kJ of outdoor heat. Its heating output is 800 kJ. These diagrams reveal conservation errors immediately, but do not by themselves prove Second Law feasibility.

3. First Law balances for opposite work directions

The First Law of Thermodynamics requires conservation of energy. For a cyclic heat engine, Wout = QH − QL. For a cyclic refrigerator or heat pump, QH = QL + Win. In this lesson QH, QL, and work are positive magnitudes, and the work direction is stated explicitly.

The equations look different because the machines do different jobs. A heat engine supplies work to the surroundings, whereas a refrigerator or heat pump requires work from the surroundings. For example, an engine receiving 1200 kJ and rejecting 800 kJ produces 400 kJ of work. A refrigerator removing 800 kJ while consuming 400 kJ rejects 1200 kJ. Both calculations conserve energy, but the direction of the useful energy conversion is reversed.

Heat engine: Wout = QH − QL   ·   Cooling/heating: QH = QL + Win

4. Choosing the correct performance measure

Thermal efficiency is the ratio of heat-engine net work output to heat input: η = Wout/QH. A coefficient of performance is the useful heat transfer divided by work input. Refrigerator COP is COPR = QL/Win; heat-pump COP is COPHP = QH/Win.

A refrigerator COP of 4 is not equivalent to a heat engine that converts 400% of heat input into work. The refrigerator transfers four units of heat out of a cooled space per unit of work consumed. The heat engine converts a fraction of supplied heat into work. Comparing the numbers directly is a category error. Engineers compare similar devices under similar conditions, and they always report the relevant desired output.

η = Wout/QH   ·   COPR = QL/Win   ·   COPHP = QH/Win

5. Second Law feasibility and Carnot limits

The Second Law of Thermodynamics adds restrictions beyond conservation. A cyclic engine operating between fixed reservoirs cannot exceed its Carnot efficiency, ηC = 1 − TL/TH. A reversible refrigerator has the maximum COPR,C = TL/(TH − TL), while a reversible heat pump has maximum COPHP,C = TH/(TH − TL).

The temperatures must be in Kelvin, with TH greater than TL and both positive. At 600 K hot and 300 K cold, an engine's ideal efficiency limit is 50%. A claim of 60% efficiency would violate the Second Law even if its heat and work streams balanced. At 320 K warm and 280 K cold, ideal refrigerator and heat-pump COP limits are 7 and 8, respectively. Real machines operate below the corresponding reversible limit.

ηC = 1 − TL/TH   ·   COPR,C = TL/(TH−TL)   ·   COPHP,C = TH/(TH−TL)

6. Ideal models versus actual performance

A reversible process is an ideal limiting case with no net entropy generation. Actual systems contain irreversibilities including friction, fluid resistance, electrical losses, mixing, and heat transfer across finite temperature differences. These reduce useful performance relative to reversible benchmarks.

For an actual heat engine, the thermal efficiency is below the Carnot efficiency for the same reservoir temperatures. For a real refrigerator or heat pump, the actual COP is below its Carnot limit. A mathematical model can enforce these upper bounds without predicting all practical losses. Passing a Carnot check means the design is not ruled out by that ideal limit; it does not mean that a particular compressor, turbine, or heat exchanger can achieve the proposed performance.

7. Energy, power, and capacity

Energy is an amount transferred or stored, commonly measured in kilojoules or kilowatt-hours. Power is energy per unit time, measured in kilowatts. Cooling capacity and heating capacity are rates of heat transfer, also expressed in kilowatts. One kilowatt equals one kilojoule per second; one kilowatt-hour equals 3600 kilojoules.

If a heat pump supplies 12 kW of heat for five hours, it delivers 60 kWh of thermal energy. At COPHP = 4, electrical input is 12/4 = 3 kW, and five hours of operation uses 15 kWh of electricity. Students must not treat 12 kW as 12 kWh or mix work per cycle with power per second. Consistent time intervals and units are essential for meaningful comparisons.

1 kW = 1 kJ/s   ·   1 kWh = 3600 kJ   ·   Energy = power × time

8. Operating cost and fair comparisons

Operating cost can be estimated by multiplying electrical energy consumption in kWh by the electricity rate in dollars per kWh. Fuel-fired systems require a fuel-energy input and a price per unit of fuel energy. When comparing devices, the engineer should use the same delivered service, operating duration, and cost assumptions.

Suppose two heat pumps each provide 12 kW of heating for eight hours. At COP 3, one uses 4 kW electrical power or 32 kWh. At COP 4, the other uses 3 kW or 24 kWh. At $0.15/kWh, their operating costs are $4.80 and $3.60, respectively. The second costs less to operate for this task, but purchase price, maintenance, cold-weather performance, and reliability could change the overall choice.

9. Environmental and safety considerations

A machine's environmental impact depends on more than its efficiency or COP. A fuel-burning heat engine may emit pollutants and greenhouse gases, depending on the fuel and combustion process. Electrically driven heat pumps depend partly on how electricity is generated. Refrigerant leakage can have climate impacts, and different refrigerants have different safety and environmental properties.

Waste heat may sometimes be recovered for a useful purpose. A combined heat-and-power plant, for example, can use rejected thermal energy for building or process heating while generating electricity. Such a system should be evaluated with clearly defined useful outputs and boundaries rather than by applying one simple engine-efficiency ratio to every energy stream. Engineers must also consider noise, pressure safety, equipment durability, and maintenance requirements.

10. Structured engineering selection

A sound engineering design process begins with a defined task. The designer lists constraints, selects candidate technologies, calculates performance, checks the First and Second Laws, compares operating costs, and justifies a choice. A design constraint is a required limit such as maximum input power, minimum useful capacity, or an allowable temperature range.

A good recommendation is not merely “choose the highest COP.” It might be “choose Heat Pump B because it delivers the required 12 kW, draws 3 kW rather than 4 kW, stays below the Carnot limit, and saves $1.20 over eight hours under the stated tariff.” The recommendation should also acknowledge uncertainty: performance can vary with outdoor temperature, equipment cycling, and actual component losses. Engineering conclusions are strongest when the calculations and assumptions are visible.

Seven Worked Examples

1. Compare engine and refrigerator energy balances

Situation: An engine receives 1500 kJ and rejects 900 kJ. A refrigerator removes 900 kJ using 600 kJ work.

Engine Wout = 1500 − 900 = 600 kJ. Refrigerator QH = 900 + 600 = 1500 kJ.

Engineering interpretation: The same magnitudes can appear with opposite work directions.

2. Heat-engine efficiency

Situation: An engine receives 2000 kJ of heat and produces 700 kJ net work.

η = 700/2000 = 35%; rejected heat = 2000 − 700 = 1300 kJ.

Engineering interpretation: Efficiency measures work produced per heat supplied.

3. Cooling and heating COP

Situation: A refrigeration cycle absorbs 1200 kJ and consumes 300 kJ work.

COPR = 1200/300 = 4. QH = 1500 kJ; COPHP = 1500/300 = 5.

Engineering interpretation: Useful output changes with the device's intended purpose.

4. Carnot feasibility comparison

Situation: An engine operates between 700 K and 350 K and claims 55% efficiency.

ηC = 1 − 350/700 = 50%. Claimed 55% exceeds the limit.

Engineering interpretation: The proposal violates the Second Law even if energy is conserved.

5. Capacity converted to energy

Situation: A heat pump delivers 10 kW of heat for 6 hours at COP 4.

Thermal energy = 10 × 6 = 60 kWh. Electrical power = 10/4 = 2.5 kW; electrical energy = 15 kWh.

Engineering interpretation: Do not confuse a capacity rate with energy over time.

6. Compare heat-pump operating costs

Situation: Two heat pumps each deliver 12 kW for 8 h. A has COP 3; B has COP 4. Electricity costs $0.15/kWh.

A: 12/3 = 4 kW, 32 kWh, $4.80. B: 12/4 = 3 kW, 24 kWh, $3.60.

Engineering interpretation: B saves $1.20 for this operating period under the stated assumptions.

7. Select a feasible heating system

Situation: A building needs at least 12 kW heating with at most 3.5 kW electrical input. At 320 K warm and 280 K cold, candidate A has 12 kW and COP 3; candidate B has 12 kW and COP 4.

Carnot heat-pump COP = 320/40 = 8. A draws 4 kW (fails); B draws 3 kW (passes).

Engineering interpretation: Candidate B meets capacity, power, and ideal feasibility constraints.

Independent Practice

Attempt each question independently before expanding the worked solution.

Problem 1: An engine receives 1400 kJ and rejects 910 kJ. Find work and efficiency.

Worked solution: Work = 1400 − 910 = 490 kJ; η = 490/1400 = 35%.

Problem 2: A refrigerator removes 800 kJ using 200 kJ work. Find rejected heat and COP.

Worked solution: QH = 1000 kJ; COPR = 800/200 = 4.

Problem 3: A heat pump delivers 1800 kJ and uses 450 kJ work. Find COP and heat absorbed.

Worked solution: COPHP = 1800/450 = 4; QL = 1800 − 450 = 1350 kJ.

Problem 4: An engine operates between 800 K and 400 K and claims 60% efficiency. Is it possible?

Worked solution: Carnot limit = 1 − 400/800 = 50%; 60% exceeds the limit and is impossible.

Problem 5: A heat pump provides 8 kW heating for 5 h at COP 4. Find electrical energy.

Worked solution: Electrical power = 8/4 = 2 kW; electrical energy = 2 × 5 = 10 kWh.

Problem 6: Two heat pumps deliver 10 kW for 10 h. A has COP 2.5; B has COP 4. Electricity costs $0.20/kWh. Compare costs.

Worked solution: A: 4 kW × 10 h = 40 kWh = $8.00. B: 2.5 kW × 10 h = 25 kWh = $5.00. B saves $3.00.

Problem 7: A cooling system needs 9 kW at no more than 3 kW input. Candidate A has COP 2.5 and B has COP 3.5. Which meets the requirement?

Worked solution: A requires 9/2.5 = 3.6 kW (fails); B requires 9/3.5 ≈ 2.57 kW (passes), subject to temperature limits.

Lesson Summary

Heat engines, refrigerators, and heat pumps all obey the First and Second Laws, but their intended outputs differ. Heat engines receive heat and produce work; refrigerators use work to remove heat from cold regions; heat pumps use work to deliver heat to warm regions. The First Law balances every energy stream, while Carnot limits establish theoretical upper bounds on performance for specified reservoir temperatures.

Thermal efficiency measures heat-engine work divided by supplied heat. Refrigerator COP measures useful cooling divided by work input, and heat-pump COP measures useful heating divided by work input. Engineering decisions also require consistent units, capacity, operating time, cost, physical feasibility, and realistic assumptions. A strong design recommendation identifies the task, checks all constraints, and uses calculated evidence rather than one isolated performance number.

Wout = QH − QL   ·   QH = QL + Win
η = Wout/QH   ·   COPR = QL/Win   ·   COPHP = QH/Win
Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h)   ·   Cost = energy (kWh) × rate ($/kWh)
