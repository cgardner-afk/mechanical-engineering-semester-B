Readiness and retrieval

Math and vocabulary checkpoint

This unit builds on force and motion from Semester A and power transmission from Unit B1. A force is measured in newtons, an area in square meters, and a mass in kilograms. Pressure is not the same as force, just as density is not the same as mass. Distinguishing a quantity from its unit is essential for interpreting fluid properties.

Check the following unit relationships before continuing: 1 L = 0.001 m³; 1 mL = 0.000001 m³; 1 kPa = 1000 Pa. A sample occupying 2 L has volume 0.002 m³. If it has mass 2 kg, density is 2/0.002 = 1000 kg/m³. If these conversions are difficult, write each quantity with its unit and use multiplication by a conversion factor.

Percent changes are ratios. A reduction from 100 mL to 90 mL is a 10 mL decrease, or 10% of the original volume. In the signed convention used in this lesson, ΔV = −10 mL and ΔV/V₀ = −0.10. A percentage change and a physical volume have different units; do not confuse them.

Engineering concepts

What fluids are and how their properties matter

What counts as a fluid?

A fluid is a substance that continues to deform when a shear stress acts on it. A solid can resist a modest steady shear force while maintaining an approximately fixed shape. A fluid instead keeps changing shape for as long as the shear stress acts. This behavior explains why water pours and air moves through a ventilation duct. It does not mean every fluid flows at the same speed or that a solid never deforms.

Both liquids and gases are fluids. A liquid normally has a nearly fixed volume but takes the shape of its container. A gas spreads to occupy the available container volume and is much more easily compressed. A liquid can have a free surface, such as the top of water in a glass. A gas usually fills the space around it rather than forming a persistent free surface. These are useful introductory distinctions, although unusual temperatures and pressures can produce more complicated behavior.

Particles, forces, and fluid behavior

On a microscopic scale, fluid particles move and interact. Liquid molecules remain close together but can rearrange relative to one another; gas molecules are generally farther apart and move throughout their container. This picture helps explain why gases compress much more readily than ordinary liquids. It also helps explain why fluid properties can change when temperature or pressure changes.

Engineers use a continuum model when they treat a fluid as if its properties vary smoothly from point to point rather than tracking every molecule. This model works well for many everyday mechanical systems. It allows us to describe density, pressure, and velocity at locations in a pipe or hydraulic cylinder. The model has limits for very rarefied gases and extremely small scales, but it is an effective starting point for this course.

Mass, volume, and density

The mass of a fluid sample is the amount of matter it contains, usually measured in kilograms. Its volume is the space it occupies, commonly measured in cubic meters or liters. Density connects the two: ρ = m/V. If two samples have the same volume, the denser one has more mass. Water near room temperature has a density close to 1000 kg/m³, while air near room temperature and ordinary atmospheric pressure is roughly 1.2 kg/m³.

Density is not the same as weight or thickness. A large container of a low-density fluid can have more mass than a small container of a high-density fluid. In later lessons, density helps calculate pressure changes with depth and buoyant forces. Here the main goal is to recognize what density measures, how to interpret its units, and why density can help an engineer identify a fluid or predict how it behaves.

Specific gravity and comparison

Specific gravity is a ratio of a substance's density to a stated reference density. For liquids, the usual reference is water at a specified condition. If an oil has density 850 kg/m³ and the reference water density is 1000 kg/m³, the oil's specific gravity is 0.85. The units cancel, so specific gravity has no units. A value below one means the sample is less dense than the reference.

A specific gravity value is meaningful only when the reference is clear. Gases may be compared with air rather than water, so a number without its reference can be misleading. A fluid with lower density than water may float on water if the fluids do not mix, but floating behavior also depends on whether they remain separate. We will calculate density and specific gravity in greater detail in B2.2.

Pressure and how fluids transmit force

Pressure describes normal force per unit area: P = F/A. The SI unit is the pascal, equal to one newton per square meter. A force of 100 N distributed uniformly over 0.02 m² gives an average pressure of 5000 Pa. Pressure is not the same as total force; the same force over a smaller area creates greater average pressure.

In a fluid at rest, pressure acts perpendicular to surfaces. Pressure can also vary with position, especially with depth in a liquid. We will calculate hydrostatic pressure in B2.3 and analyze pressure transmission in hydraulic systems in B2.4. For now, notice that pressure can be present in both liquids and gases, and that pressure is a property of the local fluid state rather than a synonym for the force on an entire piston.

Viscosity and resistance to motion

Viscosity describes a fluid's internal resistance to relative motion between neighboring layers. Honey often flows much more slowly than water under comparable conditions because its viscosity is much greater. A high-viscosity fluid may require more force to pump through a narrow passage. A low-viscosity fluid may flow more readily but may not provide the lubrication performance a machine requires.

The relevant material property for a simple shear-flow relationship is dynamic viscosity, commonly written μ and measured in Pa·s. Another measure, kinematic viscosity, is ν = μ/ρ and has units of m²/s. These two are not interchangeable. A fluid's density tells how much mass occupies a volume; its viscosity tells how strongly it resists relative motion. A liquid can be dense without being especially viscous, and a viscous fluid need not be the densest one.

Temperature and viscosity

For many common liquids, viscosity decreases as temperature increases. Warm honey usually pours more readily than cold honey. For gases, dynamic viscosity often increases with temperature under ordinary conditions, even though gas density may decrease at fixed pressure. This difference is important: engineers should not assume that heating every fluid always makes it less viscous.

Temperature can also change fluid density and other properties. A machine designed for cold outdoor operation may need a working fluid that still flows through small passages at low temperature. A system operating at high temperature may require a fluid that resists chemical breakdown and maintains adequate lubrication. Our virtual lab uses a simple illustrative temperature-response model; it does not replace measured property tables or manufacturer data.

Newtonian and non-Newtonian behavior

A Newtonian fluid has a dynamic viscosity that does not depend on shear rate at a fixed temperature and pressure. Water and many simple oils behave approximately this way over common operating ranges. For a Newtonian fluid, shear stress is proportional to the rate at which neighboring fluid layers slide past one another.

A non-Newtonian fluid has a more complicated relationship between shear stress and shear rate. Some mixtures become less resistant to flow when stirred or sheared; others become more resistant. Ketchup, some paints, and cornstarch-water mixtures are familiar examples, but their behavior depends on formulation and conditions. The word non-Newtonian does not simply mean 'very thick.' It describes how the flow response changes with the applied deformation rate.

Compressibility and volume change

Compressibility describes how much a fluid's volume changes when pressure changes. Gases are usually highly compressible compared with liquids. A sealed syringe filled with air can often be compressed noticeably, while a syringe filled completely with water is much harder to compress. Liquids are not perfectly incompressible, but their volume changes are often small enough to ignore in introductory hydraulic calculations.

A useful introductory measure is volumetric strain, ΔV/V₀, the change in volume divided by the original volume. Compression produces a negative ΔV if volume change is defined as final minus initial; the magnitude expresses the fractional shrinkage. The bulk modulus K relates pressure change to volumetric strain for small changes: K = −ΔP/(ΔV/V₀). A larger K means a fluid is harder to compress. This relation is a simplified local model; gas behavior over large pressure changes requires additional assumptions.

Surface tension and fluid interfaces

Surface tension arises from molecular interactions at a liquid interface. It helps explain why small water droplets tend toward rounded shapes and why a light object may sometimes be supported at a liquid surface under suitable conditions. Surface tension has units of N/m, not pascals or kilograms per cubic meter.

Surface effects matter in tiny tubes, spray nozzles, inkjet printing, and other systems where liquid interfaces are important. They are generally less dominant in large hydraulic cylinders. This contrast illustrates why the most important fluid property depends on the engineering problem. An engineer must decide whether the system is governed primarily by pressure, viscosity, density, compressibility, surface tension, or several of these together.

Hydraulics, pneumatics, and selection

A hydraulic system commonly uses a liquid to transmit forces and motion. A pneumatic system uses compressed gas, usually air. Because a liquid's volume changes relatively little with pressure, hydraulics can provide stiff, controlled motion and substantial force. Compressed air can store energy through compression and is convenient in many factory actuators, but its compressibility can make precise positioning more challenging.

Selecting a working fluid is not simply choosing the thickest or densest option. Engineers consider viscosity across operating temperatures, compatibility with seals and hoses, corrosion, contamination, flammability, environmental consequences, cost, and maintenance. Water is readily available but can cause corrosion or freezing problems. Hydraulic oils may lubricate components but require spill management and suitable fire precautions. The correct choice depends on the whole system.

Model assumptions and engineering judgment

A virtual model can isolate one variable and reveal patterns, but it cannot reproduce every property of a real fluid. Our lab compares representative fluids using illustrative values near ordinary conditions. It models liquid flow time as proportional to a selected viscosity index and models chamber compression with a simple pressure-volume relation. These assumptions support comparison, not precise performance predictions.

When analyzing results, distinguish an observation generated by the model from a claim about real equipment. For example, a modeled flow time doubling when viscosity doubles does not prove that an actual pipe system will behave exactly that way; geometry, pressure difference, and flow regime matter. Strong engineering explanations name the controlled variables, the changed variable, the measured response, and the limits of the model.

Worked engineering methods

Six guided fluid-property examples

Follow each example from the given information to the equation or physical principle and then to the interpretation. The numerical examples introduce relationships that will be developed further in B2.2.

Worked example 1 · Classify a material

Situation: Water in a cup holds nearly the same volume when poured into a wider cup. Air released into a larger sealed container expands to fill it.

Reasoning and result: Water is a liquid fluid with a nearly fixed volume; air is a gas fluid that expands to occupy available volume.

Engineering interpretation: Both flow under shear stress, but their compressibility and volume behavior differ.

Worked example 2 · Calculate a simple density

Situation: A liquid sample has mass 1.8 kg and volume 0.002 m³.

Reasoning and result: ρ = m/V = 1.8 kg / 0.002 m³ = 900 kg/m³.

Engineering interpretation: This is less dense than reference water at 1000 kg/m³; the calculation prepares for B2.2.

Worked example 3 · Interpret specific gravity

Situation: An oil's density is 850 kg/m³. Use reference water density 1000 kg/m³.

Reasoning and result: SG = 850/1000 = 0.85, with no units.

Engineering interpretation: The oil is 85% as dense as the specified reference water; density and viscosity remain different properties.

Worked example 4 · Interpret average pressure

Situation: A fluid applies a uniform normal force of 120 N over an area of 0.030 m².

Reasoning and result: P = F/A = 120/0.030 = 4000 Pa = 4.0 kPa.

Engineering interpretation: Pressure measures force per area. It does not tell us the total force without the area.

Worked example 5 · Compare compressibility

Situation: A chamber initially contains 100 mL of gas. A model predicts its volume falls to 80 mL under added pressure.

Reasoning and result: ΔV = 80 − 100 = −20 mL. Volumetric strain ΔV/V₀ = −20/100 = −0.20, or a 20% volume decrease.

Engineering interpretation: A large fractional volume change is typical of a gas under suitable pressure changes, not an ordinary liquid.

Worked example 6 · Select an engineering fluid

Situation: A factory actuator must deliver controlled, high-force movement with limited compression. A second device needs quick low-force motions with readily available compressed air.

Reasoning and result: The first is generally a good candidate for a hydraulic liquid; the second may be a good candidate for a pneumatic gas.

Engineering interpretation: The decision must also consider safety, leaks, cost, maintenance, temperature, and required precision.

Engineering analysis

Common misconceptions and design decisions

Density is not viscosity

Density is mass per volume, while viscosity measures resistance to relative motion. A dense fluid is not automatically thick or slow-flowing. In the laboratory, compare reference density and viscosity index separately and use the correct property to support each conclusion.

Pressure is not total force

Pressure equals normal force divided by area. A piston can produce different forces at the same pressure if its area changes. Conversely, the same force distributed over a different area produces a different pressure. This distinction prepares for Pascal's principle and hydraulic systems.

Liquids are not perfectly incompressible

A liquid's volume generally changes much less than a gas's volume under the same moderate pressure change, but the change is not exactly zero. A model that treats liquid volume as fixed is an approximation. State when that approximation is reasonable rather than presenting it as a universal law.

Flow time is not a universal viscosity equation

The laboratory's flow-time index is an illustrative comparison. Actual flow depends on pipe geometry, pressure difference, fluid rheology, and whether flow is laminar or turbulent. A viscosity index is not a substitute for dynamic viscosity measurements or a full fluid-flow model.

Temperature effects differ across fluid classes

Many liquids become less viscous when warmed, while the dynamic viscosity of many gases increases. Avoid applying a rule learned from honey to every fluid. Identify the fluid and the property before predicting the effect of temperature.

Independent practice

Seven problems with genuine step-by-step solutions

Solve each problem on paper or in your notes before opening its solution. Each disclosure includes the given information, the relevant concept or equation, the calculation or reasoning, and a final check.

Practice 1

A student says, 'Air cannot be a fluid because it has no visible surface.' Explain whether the statement is correct.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the claim. The student assumes that every fluid must have a visible free surface.
  2. Recall the definition. A fluid continuously deforms under applied shear stress; both liquids and gases meet this definition.
  3. Compare behavior. A liquid may have a visible free surface, while a gas normally spreads to fill its container.
  4. State the conclusion. The claim is incorrect. Air is a gas and a fluid.
  5. Check the explanation. The definition is based on deformation and flow, not whether the fluid can be seen.

Practice 2

A sample has mass 2.4 kg and volume 0.003 m³. Find its density and compare it with 1000 kg/m³ water.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. m = 2.4 kg and V = 0.003 m³; reference water density = 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Choose the formula. ρ = m/V.
  3. Substitute. ρ = 2.4 / 0.003 kg/m³.
  4. Calculate. ρ = 800 kg/m³.
  5. Interpret and check. 800 is less than 1000, so the sample is less dense than reference water. Units are kg/m³.

Practice 3

An oil has density 920 kg/m³. Calculate specific gravity relative to water at 1000 kg/m³.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. List givens. ρoil = 920 kg/m³; ρreference = 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Select the formula. SG = ρsample/ρreference.
  3. Substitute. SG = 920/1000.
  4. Calculate. SG = 0.92.
  5. Check units and meaning. Units cancel. The oil is 92% as dense as the stated water reference; SG does not measure viscosity.

Practice 4

A piston experiences a uniform fluid force of 150 N over 0.025 m². Find average pressure.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the values. F = 150 N and A = 0.025 m².
  2. Choose the pressure equation. P = F/A.
  3. Substitute. P = 150 N / 0.025 m².
  4. Calculate. P = 6000 Pa, or 6 kPa.
  5. Check the units. One pascal equals one N/m². A smaller area under the same force would produce greater pressure.

Practice 5

A sealed gas chamber decreases from 120 mL to 90 mL. Find volumetric strain and percent volume decrease.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify initial and final volume. V₀ = 120 mL; Vfinal = 90 mL.
  2. Calculate change. ΔV = 90 − 120 = −30 mL.
  3. Choose the relationship. Volumetric strain = ΔV/V₀.
  4. Substitute and calculate. −30/120 = −0.25.
  5. Interpret. The negative sign indicates compression; the volume decreased by 25%.

Practice 6

A machine must pump a fluid through narrow passages in cold weather. Explain why viscosity matters.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify the requirement. The fluid must flow through narrow passages at low temperature.
  2. Recall the property. Viscosity measures internal resistance to relative motion and can influence pressure losses and pumping requirements.
  3. Apply temperature behavior. Many liquids become more viscous when cooled, making flow more difficult.
  4. Make a design recommendation. Choose a fluid with a suitable specified low-temperature viscosity range and compatible materials.
  5. Check limitations. Also evaluate safety, lubrication, seal compatibility, and the actual pump; viscosity alone does not decide the design.

Practice 7

Choose between hydraulic liquid and compressed air for a heavy, precisely positioned lift. Explain the trade-off.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify performance needs. The lift requires high force and controlled positioning.
  2. Compare compressibility. Liquid working fluids are usually much less compressible than gases, supporting stiffer motion.
  3. Select a candidate. A hydraulic liquid system is generally a better starting choice for this application.
  4. Identify trade-offs. Hydraulics require attention to leaks, seals, maintenance, fluid selection, and guarding; pneumatics can be simpler for other tasks.
  5. Qualify the decision. Real selection depends on load, precision, available power, environment, and safety engineering.
Vocabulary and glossary

Individual expandable definitions

Each vocabulary term below also appears in bold yellow within the instructional reading. Open definitions individually as needed.

Fluid

A material that continues to deform under an applied shear stress.

Solid

A material that can resist a sustained modest shear stress without continuous flow.

Liquid

A fluid that normally maintains nearly fixed volume but takes its container's shape.

Gas

A compressible fluid that normally fills its available container volume.

Continuum model

A description that treats fluid properties as smooth fields rather than individual molecular motions.

Mass

Quantity of matter, measured in kilograms.

Volume

Space occupied, measured in cubic meters or liters.

Density

Mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V.

Specific gravity

Density ratio to a specified reference fluid; dimensionless.

Pressure

Normal force per unit area, P = F/A.

Pascal

SI pressure unit equal to one newton per square meter.

Viscosity

Internal resistance to relative motion between fluid layers.

Dynamic viscosity

Viscosity μ, with units of pascal-seconds.

Kinematic viscosity

Dynamic viscosity divided by density, ν = μ/ρ.

Newtonian fluid

A fluid whose viscosity does not depend on shear rate at fixed conditions.

Non-Newtonian fluid

A fluid with a shear-stress/shear-rate relation that is not the Newtonian constant-viscosity form.

Compressibility

Degree to which volume changes as pressure changes.

Volumetric strain

Fractional volume change, ΔV/V₀.

Bulk modulus

Measure of resistance to compression; for small changes K = −ΔP/(ΔV/V₀).

Surface tension

Interfacial force per unit length caused by molecular interactions at a liquid surface.

Hydraulic system

A system using liquid to transmit force and motion.

Pneumatic system

A system using gas, often compressed air, to transmit force and motion.

Engineering synthesis

Lesson summary

A fluid continuously deforms under applied shear stress. Both liquids and gases are fluids, but they differ in typical volume behavior and compressibility. Density measures mass per volume; pressure measures normal force per area; viscosity describes resistance to shear motion; and surface tension describes effects at liquid interfaces. Each property answers a different engineering question.

Temperature, pressure, and fluid composition can change properties. Engineers choose hydraulic liquids or pneumatic gases based on force requirements, motion control, operating conditions, materials compatibility, safety, and maintenance. Simplified virtual models can reveal relationships, but their assumptions and limitations must be stated.

In B2.2, you will calculate density, specific gravity, and pressure more extensively. Keep the formula reference and your recorded laboratory observations available for that lesson.
