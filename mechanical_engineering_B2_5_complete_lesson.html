Why water pushes upward

A submerged object experiences pressure from the liquid around it. At greater depth, pressure is higher, so the bottom of the object experiences more pressure than the top. The resulting upward net fluid force is the buoyant force. This is why a heavy ship can float and why lifting an object underwater often feels easier than lifting it in air.

In B2.3 you learned that hydrostatic pressure increases with depth. Consider a rectangular block with a horizontal top and bottom. The pressure on the bottom is greater than on the top by ρgh, where h is the vertical height of the block. Multiplying that difference by the face area gives ρghA, or ρgV. This physical argument introduces Archimedes' principle without treating buoyancy as a mysterious extra force.

Archimedes' principle

Archimedes' principle states that the upward buoyant force on an object immersed in a fluid equals the weight of the fluid it displaces. In symbols, FB = ρ_f gVd, where ρ_f is the fluid density and Vd is the displaced volume. The density belongs to the fluid, not automatically to the object.

If a fully submerged object displaces 0.004 m³ of freshwater with density 1000 kg/m³, its buoyant force is 1000 × 9.81 × 0.004 = 39.24 N. This upward force exists whether the object is steel, wood, or plastic. The object's mass matters separately when determining the net force. Always distinguish the displaced liquid's weight from the object's own weight.

Weight and net vertical force

An object of mass m has weight W = mg. For an object of uniform density ρ_o and total volume Vo, W = ρ_o gVo. If upward is positive, the net vertical force from weight and buoyancy is Fnet = FB − W. A positive result indicates an initial upward tendency, and a negative result indicates an initial downward tendency.

These predictions assume the object is free to move, with no supporting rope, floor contact, or other forces. If a dense block rests on the bottom, the floor's reaction force matters. If a floating object is initially pushed fully underwater, it may rise until only part of its volume remains submerged. A zero net force means vertical equilibrium under the stated force model, not necessarily stable orientation.

Three possible behaviors

An object is positively buoyant when buoyancy exceeds its weight in the current submerged state; it tends to rise. It is negatively buoyant when its weight exceeds buoyancy; it tends to sink. It has neutral buoyancy when buoyancy and weight balance while it is fully submerged, allowing it to remain suspended in the ideal stationary model.

For a uniform solid fully submerged in a uniform fluid, comparing object and fluid densities gives the same prediction: lower object density tends to rise, equal density gives neutral buoyancy, and greater density tends to sink. But for hollow objects or boats, use the average density of the entire object including enclosed air. A steel ship can have a low average density even though solid steel is much denser than water.

Floating is an equilibrium, not full immersion

A floating object generally settles until it displaces exactly enough liquid to support its weight: FB = W. If the object is partially submerged, Vd is smaller than its total volume. For a uniform object floating in a uniform fluid, the submerged fraction is Vd/Vo = ρ_o/ρ_f.

A block with average density 600 kg/m³ in freshwater at 1000 kg/m³ floats with about 60% of its volume underwater. The remaining 40% is above the waterline in the ideal model. A common error is to insert the object's entire volume into the buoyancy equation for an object that is floating partially submerged; instead solve for the volume needed to balance weight.

Freshwater versus saltwater

Denser water provides more buoyant force for the same displaced volume. A fully submerged block experiences greater buoyancy in illustrative saltwater at 1030 kg/m³ than in freshwater at 1000 kg/m³. A floating object therefore needs to displace less saltwater to support the same weight.

For a 600 kg/m³ block, the submerged fraction is 600/1000 = 0.600 in freshwater and 600/1030 ≈ 0.583 in saltwater. It rides slightly higher in the denser liquid. Actual water density varies with temperature, salinity, and pressure; the laboratory uses fixed illustrative values so you can study the relationship clearly.

Apparent weight underwater

When a submerged object hangs from a spring scale, the scale does not need to support the object's entire weight because the fluid pushes upward. The apparent weight or ideal supporting tension is Wapp = W − FB when the object is held motionless and buoyancy acts upward.

A 100 N object experiencing 30 N of buoyancy has an apparent weight of 70 N. If buoyancy exceeds weight, this formula gives a negative value; a hanging rope cannot provide negative tension. In that case a downward restraint would be needed to hold the object fully submerged. Explain the sign and the actual support arrangement rather than reporting a negative spring-scale reading.

Does buoyancy increase with depth?

For a fully submerged rigid object in a constant-density stationary liquid, buoyant force does not depend on depth. Moving it deeper increases the pressure on both its top and bottom, but the pressure difference across its height remains ρgh. Thus the net upward force remains ρ_f gVd if displaced volume is unchanged.

This is different from saying pressure itself stays constant. Pressure increases at every surface as the object descends. If the fluid density changes with depth or the object compresses, the displaced volume or density may change, and buoyancy can change. The lab deliberately keeps these quantities fixed to isolate the ideal principle.

Displacement and capacity

A floating platform must displace enough water to balance its own mass and its payload. If total supported mass is M, the required displacement volume is Vreq = M/ρ_f. A platform with a maximum allowable displaced volume Vmax can support at most ρ_f Vmax total mass in the ideal model.

The available payload mass is therefore ρ_f Vmax − mplatform, provided the platform geometry can actually reach that displacement without flooding. For example, 0.50 m³ allowable freshwater displacement corresponds to 500 kg total mass; if the platform itself is 180 kg, the ideal additional payload capacity is 320 kg. This is a mass calculation; multiplying by g converts the corresponding forces to newtons.

Reserve buoyancy and freeboard

Designers do not normally load a floating platform to the exact edge of its maximum displacement. Reserve buoyancy represents displacement capacity kept available beyond what is currently required. In this lesson the reserve fraction is (Vmax − Vreq)/Vmax. The lab asks for at least 20% reserve capacity, which means Vreq ≤ 0.80V_max.

The freeboard is the vertical distance from the waterline to a specified deck or upper edge. Our simple lab uses reserve displacement volume rather than calculating freeboard from detailed hull geometry. A design may meet the buoyancy reserve check yet still be unstable, structurally weak, or vulnerable to waves. Reserve buoyancy is one design criterion, not proof of safety.

Shape, average density, and stability

A solid metal cube typically sinks because its density is greater than water's. A hollow metal vessel may float because its overall average density, including enclosed air, is lower. Shape affects how much water can be displaced before flooding and where buoyant forces act.

Floating does not guarantee stability. A tall narrow platform can tip even if its upward buoyant force equals its weight. Real vessel analysis considers the centers of gravity and buoyancy, shifting loads, waves, and restoring moments. Those calculations are beyond the current introductory model; never use a virtual floating result to certify a boat or load-carrying structure.

Engineering decisions and limitations

When evaluating a buoyant design, identify fluid density, platform mass, payload, allowable displacement, and reserve requirement. Calculate required displacement and compare it with the limit. Report whether the model predicts flotation, whether reserve is adequate, and which assumption could affect real performance.

The design constraint is a requirement that must be met, such as minimum reserve or maximum payload. An engineering trade-off occurs when a change helps one criterion but hurts another: adding ballast can lower the center of gravity but increases required displacement; increasing hull volume adds capacity but may increase material mass and cost. The lab models force balance and reserve volume only, not stability or material strength.

Seven worked buoyancy examples

Each example explicitly identifies the relevant volume, density, and force reference. Follow the numbered steps and then explain the engineering interpretation.

Worked example 1 · Fully submerged block

Situation: A rigid block displaces 0.004 m³ of freshwater at 1000 kg/m³.

  1. Identify displaced volume Vd = 0.004 m³ and fluid density 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Use FB = ρ_f gVd.
  3. Substitute 1000 × 9.81 × 0.004.
  4. Calculate 39.24 N upward.

Interpretation: This buoyancy does not require knowing the block's material.

Worked example 2 · Compare two liquids

Situation: The same 0.003 m³ block is fully submerged in freshwater (1000 kg/m³) and saltwater (1030 kg/m³).

  1. Freshwater: 1000 × 9.81 × 0.003 = 29.43 N.
  2. Saltwater: 1030 × 9.81 × 0.003 = 30.3129 N.
  3. Subtract 30.3129 − 29.43 = 0.8829 N.
  4. Conclude saltwater gives more upward force at equal displaced volume.

Interpretation: Density difference, not depth, explains the difference.

Worked example 3 · Rise or sink?

Situation: A 0.002 m³ rigid block has mass 3.0 kg and is fully submerged in freshwater.

  1. Object weight W = 3 × 9.81 = 29.43 N.
  2. Buoyant force = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.002 = 19.62 N.
  3. Net upward force = 19.62 − 29.43 = −9.81 N.
  4. The block has 9.81 N net downward force.

Interpretation: With no support, it initially sinks.

Worked example 4 · Apparent weight

Situation: A block weighs 120 N in air and displaces 0.005 m³ of freshwater.

  1. Buoyancy = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.005 = 49.05 N.
  2. For a stationary hanging block, tension = W − FB.
  3. Calculate 120 − 49.05 = 70.95 N.
  4. The spring scale would read 70.95 N in this ideal model.

Interpretation: Buoyancy reduces the required supporting tension.

Worked example 5 · Submerged fraction

Situation: A uniform block of average density 650 kg/m³ floats in freshwater.

  1. Use Vd/Vo = ρ_o/ρ_f.
  2. Substitute 650/1000.
  3. Calculate 0.65, or 65% submerged.
  4. The remaining 35% is above water in the ideal model.

Interpretation: Do not assume the full block is submerged.

Worked example 6 · Maximum payload

Situation: A floating platform allows 0.70 m³ maximum freshwater displacement and has mass 220 kg.

  1. Maximum total supported mass = ρ_f Vmax.
  2. Calculate 1000 × 0.70 = 700 kg.
  3. Subtract platform mass: 700 − 220 = 480 kg maximum payload.
  4. This calculation provides no reserve allowance.

Interpretation: A real design would leave reserve and check stability.

Worked example 7 · Platform reserve decision

Situation: Platform mass 180 kg, payload 300 kg, allowable displacement 0.70 m³ in freshwater; minimum reserve 20%.

  1. Total mass = 180 + 300 = 480 kg.
  2. Required displaced volume = 480/1000 = 0.480 m³.
  3. Reserve fraction = (0.70 − 0.480)/0.70 = 0.3143.
  4. 31.4% reserve exceeds the 20% requirement.
  5. Check required displacement 0.480 ≤ 0.70 m³: flotation and reserve checks pass.

Interpretation: Buoyancy pass does not verify stability or structure.

Analysis and misconceptions

Buoyancy is not the object's weight

The buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid, not the object's weight in every situation. These forces become equal for an object floating in equilibrium.

Floating does not require full submersion

A floating object generally displaces only the fluid volume necessary to support its weight. Using total object volume for a partially submerged object overestimates buoyancy.

Greater depth is not automatically greater buoyancy

Pressure increases with depth, but for a rigid fully submerged object in uniform-density fluid, the difference in pressure across the object stays the same. Buoyant force remains constant if displaced volume is unchanged.

Mass and density answer different questions

Mass contributes to weight; average density helps predict floating behavior. A large hollow vessel can float even when its shell material is dense.

Flotation is not stability

The platform model tests buoyancy and reserve volume. It does not evaluate tipping, flooding, strength, or safety certification. A design decision must state these limits.

Practice 1

A fully submerged object displaces 0.006 m³ of freshwater. Calculate buoyant force.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. ρ_f = 1000 kg/m³, Vd = 0.006 m³, g = 9.81 m/s².
  2. Choose equation. FB = ρ_f gVd.
  3. Substitute. 1000 × 9.81 × 0.006.
  4. Calculate. 58.86 N upward.
  5. Interpret. This is the weight of 0.006 m³ of displaced freshwater.

Practice 2

A rigid object displaces 0.004 m³ in freshwater and illustrative saltwater (1030 kg/m³). Find the difference in buoyancy.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Freshwater force. 1000 × 9.81 × 0.004 = 39.24 N.
  2. Saltwater force. 1030 × 9.81 × 0.004 = 40.4172 N.
  3. Subtract. 40.4172 − 39.24 = 1.1772 N.
  4. Interpret. The denser saltwater produces greater buoyancy.

Practice 3

A 2.5 kg object with volume 0.002 m³ is fully submerged in freshwater. Predict its initial motion.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Find weight. W = 2.5 × 9.81 = 24.525 N.
  2. Find buoyancy. FB = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.002 = 19.62 N.
  3. Find net force. Fnet = 19.62 − 24.525 = −4.905 N.
  4. Conclude. 4.905 N downward; the unsupported object initially sinks.
  5. Check. Average density 2.5/0.002 = 1250 kg/m³ exceeds water density.

Practice 4

A fully submerged block weighs 90 N in air and displaces 0.003 m³ of water. What would a spring scale read while holding it still?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Find buoyancy. 1000 × 9.81 × 0.003 = 29.43 N.
  2. Choose equation. Wapp = W − FB.
  3. Substitute. 90 − 29.43.
  4. Calculate. 60.57 N.
  5. Interpret. The spring scale supports the remainder of the weight.

Practice 5

A uniform block of density 720 kg/m³ floats in freshwater. Find submerged and exposed fractions.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Choose equation. Vd/Vo = ρ_o/ρ_f.
  2. Substitute. 720/1000 = 0.72.
  3. Convert. 72% submerged.
  4. Find exposed fraction. 100% − 72% = 28% above water.
  5. Check. The block is less dense than water, so partial flotation is plausible.

Practice 6

A platform has mass 150 kg and maximum allowable freshwater displacement 0.60 m³. Find the ideal maximum payload without reserve.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Find total mass capacity. ρ_f Vmax = 1000 × 0.60 = 600 kg.
  2. Subtract platform mass. 600 − 150 = 450 kg.
  3. State result. 450 kg payload with zero reserve.
  4. Interpret. A practical design would require a margin and stability checks.

Practice 7

A platform of mass 240 kg carries a 360 kg payload in freshwater. Its maximum allowed displacement is 0.90 m³ and reserve must be at least 20%. Does it pass?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Find total mass. 240 + 360 = 600 kg.
  2. Find required displacement. Vreq = 600/1000 = 0.60 m³.
  3. Check flotation. 0.60 ≤ 0.90 m³, so displacement capacity passes.
  4. Calculate reserve. (0.90 − 0.60)/0.90 = 0.3333.
  5. Compare requirement. 33.3% reserve ≥ 20%; modeled reserve passes.
  6. Interpret. Stability, flooding, and structure remain untested.

Summary and transfer

Archimedes' principle states that buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid: FB = ρ_f gVd. The object's weight is W = mg. Comparing these forces predicts whether an unsupported submerged object initially rises or sinks. A floating object adjusts its submerged volume until buoyancy balances weight.

For a uniform floating object, the submerged fraction equals the ratio of object density to fluid density. Apparent weight is reduced by buoyancy. Under constant-density, constant-volume conditions, fully submerged buoyancy does not increase simply because an object moves deeper.

Floating-platform capacity can be estimated from maximum displaced volume and total supported mass. A reserve requirement adds a design margin, but neither floating equilibrium nor reserve capacity proves stability or structural safety.
