Mass conservation is the starting point

Water flowing into a full pipe does not vanish when the pipe becomes narrower. Under steady flow with no leaks, branches, or storage, the same mass of water enters and leaves each second. The continuity equation describes this conservation mathematically. It helps engineers predict the average speed at one pipe section when they know the speed and size of another.

In B3.1 you used Q = Av to calculate volumetric flow rate. A pipe transition makes that equation especially useful. The amount of water per second remains the same under the stated assumptions, but the cross-sectional area changes. Therefore the average speed must change to compensate. A narrower section requires a higher speed; a wider section permits a lower speed.

Choose a control volume

A control volume is an imagined region around part of a physical system. Draw one around a narrowing pipe, with an inlet at the left and an outlet at the right. The pipe walls prevent water from escaping sideways. If no water accumulates, mass flow entering equals mass flow leaving.

This simple boundary is a powerful problem-solving tool. A side branch would create another outlet, and a filling tank would create storage. Both would need additional terms in the balance. Before writing an equation, ask how many inlets and outlets exist, whether the fluid is stored, and whether the system changes over time.

General and incompressible continuity

The general steady one-inlet, one-outlet mass equation is ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂. The symbol ρ means density, A is cross-sectional area, and v is average velocity. Subscripts 1 and 2 identify the locations. Both sides have units of kg/s, so the equation compares mass transported per second.

For an incompressible fluid at essentially constant density, ρ₁ and ρ₂ cancel, leaving A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Each side now has units of m³/s. This simplified equation is the focus of the lesson. If density changes significantly, do not cancel it; mass flow may remain equal even when volumetric flow does not.

The inverse relationship

Rearranging A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ gives v₂ = (A₁/A₂)v₁. The outlet speed is multiplied by the ratio of inlet area to outlet area. If outlet area is one quarter of inlet area, outlet velocity must be four times greater to carry the same water volume each second.

This is an inverse relationship: for a fixed volumetric flow, velocity decreases as area increases. If the outlet area is twice the inlet area, outlet velocity is half the inlet velocity. Always check that the direction makes physical sense. A contraction should increase speed and an expansion should decrease it in this idealized comparison.

Why diameter must be squared

Circular pipe area is A = πd²/4, where d is the internal diameter. Substituting this into the continuity equation and canceling π/4 gives d₁²v₁ = d₂²v₂. Thus v₂ = v₁(d₁/d₂)². The ratio works with millimeters on both sides because matching units cancel.

Suppose water enters a 60 mm pipe at 1.5 m/s and leaves through a 30 mm opening. The diameter ratio is two, but the area ratio is four. Outlet velocity is 1.5 × 4 = 6 m/s. A common error is to double speed when diameter halves. Always square the diameter ratio.

Widening a pipe

A pipe that widens is an expansion. If water flows from a 25 mm opening at 8 m/s into a 50 mm section, v₂ = 8(25/50)² = 2 m/s. The larger area transports the same volume with less average speed. This result does not mean the water stops moving or that mass disappears.

A contraction does the opposite. An irrigation nozzle may accelerate water through a smaller opening, while a diffuser-like expansion slows it. The geometry predicts a velocity ratio when flow is known, but it does not establish the pressure or pump energy required to maintain that flow.

Calculate flow at both sections

The continuity relationship can be checked by calculating Q at each end. For a 40 mm inlet at 2 m/s, A₁ = π(0.040)²/4 = 0.0012566 m² and Q₁ = 0.0025133 m³/s. If the outlet is 20 mm, continuity predicts v₂ = 8 m/s. Its area is 0.00031416 m², so Q₂ = 0.0025133 m³/s.

The equality of the two independently calculated flows is a useful accuracy check. A result that makes Q₂ different from Q₁ indicates a calculation, unit, or assumption error. Small differences caused by rounding are acceptable, but a factor of two or four usually signals an incorrect area ratio.

Find an unknown inlet speed

Sometimes the outlet speed is measured while the inlet speed is unknown. Rearrange the equation: v₁ = v₂(A₂/A₁), or for circular sections v₁ = v₂(d₂/d₁)². A 25 mm outlet carrying water at 6 m/s from a 50 mm inlet requires v₁ = 6(25/50)² = 1.5 m/s.

Notice that the diameter ratio is reversed compared with the equation for v₂. Rather than memorizing two unrelated formulas, start from A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ and isolate the unknown. Algebraic reasoning makes it easier to solve unfamiliar configurations and reduces the chance of selecting the wrong ratio.

Find an unknown diameter

An engineer may know the allowable outlet velocity and need to choose an outlet diameter. From v₂ = v₁(d₁/d₂)², solve d₂ = d₁√(v₁/v₂). If the inlet is 60 mm at 1.5 m/s and outlet speed must not exceed 3 m/s, the minimum idealized diameter is 60√(1.5/3) ≈ 42.43 mm.

A 40 mm outlet would produce 3.375 m/s and fail. A 45 mm outlet would produce approximately 2.667 m/s and pass. Real pipe sizes may be specified by a nominal diameter that differs from actual internal diameter, so product selection requires checking the manufacturer's dimensions.

Pressure is not velocity

Pressure measures force per area in pascals. Velocity measures distance per second, and flow rate measures volume per second. The continuity equation links area and average velocity; it does not directly determine pressure. A faster outlet does not mean pressure has been multiplied by the same ratio.

To analyze how pressure, elevation, and velocity relate, engineers use energy principles with clearly stated assumptions. B3.3 introduces Bernoulli's principle and B3.4 considers flow resistance. The continuity equation is necessary for many pipe calculations, but it is not a complete model of the system.

Density changes and mass flow

For water under ordinary introductory conditions, constant density is usually a reasonable assumption. For a gas with changing density, the general mass equation must be retained. If two pipe sections have equal area but inlet density is 2 kg/m³ and outlet density is 1 kg/m³, steady mass flow requires the outlet speed to be twice the inlet speed.

The volume per second can change while mass per second stays constant. This is why mass flow rate is the fundamental conserved quantity. In this lesson the interactive model uses incompressible water, so it intentionally keeps the same Q at both sections. The general equation is included to clarify the limits of that simplification.

Engineering decisions and model boundaries

A design may require at least 2 L/s while limiting outlet velocity to 3 m/s. A candidate is feasible only if it satisfies both numerical requirements. A large outlet may reduce speed but cannot rescue an inlet flow that is already below the target. Increasing inlet speed may meet the flow target but cause an outlet speed violation.

The lab treats inlet speed as independently selected and assumes a full circular pipe, steady incompressible flow, no leaks, and no branches. It does not predict viscous losses, pump curves, cavitation, turbulence, or available pressure. A passing virtual design is a valid result under the simplified constraints, not an approval for physical construction.

Worked example 1: Narrowing pipe

60 mm inlet, 30 mm outlet, inlet speed 1.5 m/s

  1. Step 1: State steady incompressible flow without branches or leaks.
  2. Step 2: Write v₂ = v₁(d₁/d₂)².
  3. Step 3: Area ratio = (60/30)² = 4.
  4. Step 4: Outlet velocity = 1.5 × 4 = 6 m/s.
  5. Step 5: Interpret the fourfold speed increase with unchanged flow.

Worked example 2: Widening pipe

25 mm inlet at 8 m/s, 50 mm outlet

  1. Step 1: Use the diameter-squared relationship.
  2. Step 2: Calculate (25/50)² = 0.25.
  3. Step 3: Outlet velocity = 8 × 0.25 = 2 m/s.
  4. Step 4: Explain why speed decreases in the larger opening.

Worked example 3: Verify both flow rates

40 mm inlet at 2 m/s, 20 mm outlet

  1. Step 1: Convert inlet diameter to 0.040 m.
  2. Step 2: A₁ = π(0.040)²/4 = 0.0012566 m².
  3. Step 3: Q₁ = A₁ × 2 = 0.0025133 m³/s.
  4. Step 4: v₂ = 2(40/20)² = 8 m/s.
  5. Step 5: A₂ = π(0.020)²/4 = 0.00031416 m².
  6. Step 6: Q₂ = A₂ × 8 = 0.0025133 m³/s, matching Q₁.

Worked example 4: Unknown inlet speed

50 mm inlet, 25 mm outlet at 6 m/s

  1. Step 1: Rearrange to v₁ = v₂(d₂/d₁)².
  2. Step 2: Calculate (25/50)² = 0.25.
  3. Step 3: v₁ = 6 × 0.25 = 1.5 m/s.
  4. Step 4: The larger inlet has lower velocity.

Worked example 5: Minimum outlet diameter

60 mm inlet at 1.5 m/s, maximum outlet speed 3 m/s

  1. Step 1: Use d₂ = d₁√(v₁/v₂).
  2. Step 2: Substitute d₂ = 60√(1.5/3) mm.
  3. Step 3: Minimum diameter ≈ 42.43 mm.
  4. Step 4: A 40 mm outlet produces 3.375 m/s and fails.
  5. Step 5: A 45 mm outlet produces 2.667 m/s and passes.

Worked example 6: Changing density

Equal-area sections, inlet density 2 kg/m³, outlet density 1 kg/m³, inlet speed 3 m/s

  1. Step 1: Use ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂.
  2. Step 2: Cancel equal areas.
  3. Step 3: v₂ = (2/1) × 3 = 6 m/s.
  4. Step 4: Mass flow remains equal, but volumetric flow changes.
  5. Step 5: Explain why the incompressible shortcut is invalid here.

Worked example 7: Design comparison

50 mm inlet at 1.2 m/s, outlets 30 mm and 40 mm, speed limit 2.5 m/s

  1. Step 1: For 30 mm: v₂ = 1.2(50/30)² ≈ 3.333 m/s, so it fails.
  2. Step 2: For 40 mm: v₂ = 1.2(50/40)² = 1.875 m/s, so it passes.
  3. Step 3: Q = π(0.050)²/4 × 1.2 = 0.002356 m³/s = 2.356 L/s.
  4. Step 4: Recommend 40 mm for the stated speed constraint.
  5. Step 5: Identify pressure loss and pump capacity as untested.

Misconceptions and engineering interpretation

Misconception 1

A narrow pipe does not create water. Under steady incompressible flow without leaks or branches, the same volume per second passes each section, but the smaller area requires greater velocity.

Misconception 2

A halved diameter does not merely double speed. Circular area scales with diameter squared, so halving diameter produces four times the velocity when flow stays constant.

Misconception 3

Pressure is not interchangeable with speed or flow. Continuity alone cannot calculate a pressure difference. Bernoulli's principle and energy losses are introduced in subsequent lessons.

Misconception 4

Equal volume flow is not universal. The fundamental balance conserves mass; changing-density flow requires ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂.

Misconception 5

Passing the virtual constraints is not proof of real-world feasibility. Pump limits, friction, elevation, noise, materials, and operating energy must also be considered.

  3. Step 3: v₂ = 1 × 9 = 9 m/s.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Use v₂ = 7.2(20/60)².
  2. Step 2: The squared ratio is 1/9.
  3. Step 3: v₂ = 7.2/9 = 0.8 m/s.
  4. Step 4: The wider section is slower.

Practice 3

A 50 mm inlet at 2.4 m/s feeds a 40 mm outlet. Find Q and outlet velocity.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: A₁ = π(0.050)²/4 ≈ 0.0019635 m².

  3. Step 3: v₂ = 2.4(50/40)² = 3.75 m/s.
  4. Step 4: Both sections transport the same Q.

A 30 mm outlet has speed 5 m/s and the inlet is 60 mm. Find inlet speed.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: v₁ = v₂(d₂/d₁)².
  2. Step 2: (30/60)² = 0.25.
  3. Step 3: v₁ = 5 × 0.25 = 1.25 m/s.
  4. Step 4: The inlet is slower because it is wider.

A 60 mm inlet moves at 2 m/s. Find minimum outlet diameter for a 4 m/s limit.

Show numbered solution

  3. Step 3: 45 mm passes; 40 mm fails.
  4. Step 4: The calculation ignores pumping and pressure losses.

Practice 6

Equal areas: density changes from 1.5 to 1 kg/m³, inlet speed 4 m/s. Find outlet speed.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Use ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂.
  2. Step 2: Cancel equal areas.
  3. Step 3: v₂ = (1.5/1.0) × 4 = 6 m/s.
  4. Step 4: Mass flow, not volume flow, is conserved.

Practice 7

A 70 mm inlet at 1 m/s feeds either a 35 or 50 mm outlet; max outlet speed 2.2 m/s. Recommend one.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: 35 mm gives v₂ = (70/35)² = 4 m/s: fails.
  2. Step 2: 50 mm gives v₂ = (70/50)² = 1.96 m/s: passes.
  3. Step 3: Q = π(0.070)²/4 × 1 = 0.0038485 m³/s = 3.848 L/s.

Technical vocabulary glossary

Highlighted terms in the reading are defined below. Expand a term to check its meaning.

Continuity equation

Equation expressing mass conservation across flowing sections.

Volumetric flow rate

Fluid volume crossing a section per second.

Steady flow

Flow properties do not change with time at a fixed location.

Control volume

Chosen region for tracking incoming, outgoing, and stored mass.

Density

Mass per unit volume.

Cross-sectional area

Area perpendicular to the direction of flow.

Average velocity

Mean fluid speed across a section.

Incompressible fluid

Fluid modeled with constant density.

Inverse relationship

A mathematical relationship in which one variable increases as another decreases.

Internal diameter

Distance across the inside of a pipe.

Expansion

Transition from a smaller to a larger flow area.

Nozzle

Shaped passage used to direct or accelerate fluid.

Nominal diameter

Product size designation that may differ from true internal diameter.

Pressure

Force per unit area.

Mass flow rate

Mass crossing a section per second.

Feasible

Satisfies the constraints included in a model.

Viscous losses

Mechanical energy losses associated with fluid resistance.

Contraction

Transition to a smaller flow area.

Velocity limit

Maximum allowable average fluid speed.

Area ratio

Ratio between two cross-sectional areas.

Lesson synthesis

Steady one-inlet, one-outlet conservation of mass gives ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂. With constant density, A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Circular pipes permit the shortcut v₂ = v₁(d₁/d₂)². A contraction increases average speed at fixed Q, while an expansion decreases it.

Use units, area ratios, and physical reasonableness to check answers. A numerical design recommendation must identify requirements, calculations, margins, and assumptions. B3.3 adds Bernoulli's principle to investigate pressure, velocity, and elevation together.
