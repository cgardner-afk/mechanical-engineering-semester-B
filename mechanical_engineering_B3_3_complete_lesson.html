Energy conservation in a moving fluid

A moving liquid can store mechanical energy in several forms. It may be under static pressure, it may be moving with a measurable speed, and it may be at an elevation above a chosen reference. Bernoulli’s principle connects these three contributions. The principle is not an unrelated rule that fast water has low pressure. It is a carefully limited application of the conservation of mechanical energy to fluid moving along a streamline. This distinction matters because engineers must know when a calculation is defensible and when a more complete model is needed.

Imagine two locations along a smooth, full water pipe. If no pump adds energy, no turbine extracts energy, and losses can be neglected, a rise in one form of mechanical energy must be balanced by a fall in another. A contraction can increase the kinetic contribution, while a rise in elevation increases the gravitational contribution. Pressure may then decrease. The equation describes how these quantities balance; it does not say that all pressure decreases come from increasing speed.

What the three terms mean

The usual Bernoulli equation is P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgz₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgz₂. The first term, P, is pressure energy per unit volume and is measured in pascals. The second term, ½ρv², is the dynamic pressure or kinetic-energy term per unit volume. The third term, ρgz, is the elevation term or gravitational potential energy per unit volume. Here ρ is density in kg/m³, v is speed in m/s, g is gravitational acceleration, and z is elevation in meters.

Dividing Bernoulli by ρg gives equivalent head terms in meters: pressure head P/(ρg), velocity head v²/(2g), and elevation head z. Every term has the same dimensions: N/m², or Pa, which is also J/m³. This makes the equation a conservation statement rather than an arbitrary combination of numbers. When calculating, use ρ = 1,000 kg/m³ for water unless another value is specified and g = 9.81 m/s². A pressure of 200 kPa means 200,000 Pa. A kinetic term of 16,000 Pa means 16 kPa. Keep units consistent before adding or subtracting terms.

Conditions required for the simple equation

The simple equation is used for steady flow, an incompressible fluid, and negligible mechanical-energy losses, between two locations on the same streamline. There must be no pump or turbine adding or removing mechanical energy between those points. The usual introductory calculations also assume the velocity values represent the appropriate idealized flow speed at the selected sections. These are assumptions to check, not hidden guarantees about every real pipe.

A long rough pipe, a sharp elbow, or a valve creates frictional or local losses. A pump adds energy. A compressor may substantially change gas density. In such cases, the simple form does not describe the entire energy balance. Later lessons add losses and pumping effects. For now, explicitly state the idealization whenever you use Bernoulli. If the situation clearly violates an assumption, explain why the simple equation alone is insufficient.

Pressure versus velocity in a horizontal pipe

When two points have equal elevation, ρgz₁ and ρgz₂ cancel. The result is P₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ½ρv₂², or P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² − v₂²). In an ideal horizontal contraction, the fluid speeds up. Because the squared speed increases, the static pressure decreases. In an ideal horizontal expansion, the fluid slows and static pressure can recover.

The kinetic term depends on squared speed, not simply the speed difference. Always calculate v₁² − v₂², multiply by ½ρ, and convert pascals to kilopascals.

Elevation is another part of the balance

Water flowing uphill gains gravitational potential energy per unit volume. If the speed remains constant, its kinetic terms cancel and P₂ = P₁ − ρg(z₂ − z₁). A 5 m rise in water elevation therefore corresponds to an ideal pressure decrease of 1,000 × 9.81 × 5 = 49,050 Pa, or 49.05 kPa. The relationship is similar to the hydrostatic pressure changes studied in Unit B2, but it is now part of a flowing-fluid energy balance.

For downhill flow at unchanged speed, the elevation term decreases and pressure can rise. Always define a consistent {h('elevation datum')} and determine whether z₂ − z₁ is positive or negative. A common error is to subtract a pressure change for both uphill and downhill motion. Drawing a small sketch with the two heights labeled prevents many sign mistakes.

Continuity must be solved first when diameter changes

Bernoulli alone does not tell you the speed in a narrowing pipe. That information comes from B3.2: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ for steady incompressible flow. For circular pipes, v₂ = v₁(d₁/d₂)². After calculating the unknown velocity, substitute both speeds into Bernoulli to calculate the pressure change. This is a two-equation problem: continuity conserves mass, while Bernoulli conserves mechanical energy under its ideal assumptions.

For example, water entering a 60 mm section at 1.5 m/s will travel at 6 m/s in a 30 mm section under steady incompressible conditions. If the transition is horizontal and ideal, the static pressure change is ½ × 1,000 × (1.5² − 6²) = −16,875 Pa, or −16.875 kPa. The negative sign describes a decrease. In a real reducer, additional losses would generally make the downstream pressure lower than this ideal prediction.

Seven worked engineering examples

Every model follows the same routine: identify the two locations, state assumptions, select the appropriate equations, substitute consistent units, calculate, and interpret the result.

Worked example 1: Horizontal acceleration

Water at 200 kPa speeds from 2 m/s to 6 m/s at equal elevation.

  1. Step 1: Set ρ = 1,000 kg/m³; the elevation terms cancel.
  2. Step 2: Rearrange: P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² − v₂²).
  3. Step 3: Calculate v₁² − v₂² = 4 − 36 = −32 m²/s².
  4. Step 4: Pressure change = 500(−32) = −16,000 Pa = −16 kPa.
  5. Step 5: P₂ = 200 − 16 = 184 kPa. Lower static pressure accompanies greater ideal speed.

Worked example 2: Pressure recovery

Water slows from 7 m/s to 3 m/s; inlet pressure is 150 kPa and elevation is constant.

  1. Step 1: Use P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² − v₂²).
  2. Step 2: Compute 7² − 3² = 49 − 9 = 40.
  3. Step 3: Pressure change = 500 × 40 = 20,000 Pa = 20 kPa.
  4. Step 4: P₂ = 150 + 20 = 170 kPa.
  5. Step 5: Interpret this as ideal pressure recovery; real losses would reduce recovery.

Worked example 3: Flow uphill

Water rises 5 m at constant speed from a point where pressure is 180 kPa.

  1. Step 1: Write Bernoulli and cancel equal kinetic terms.
  2. Step 2: Use P₂ = P₁ − ρg(z₂ − z₁).
  3. Step 3: Substitute ρ = 1,000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s², and rise = +5 m.
  4. Step 4: Elevation contribution = 49,050 Pa = 49.05 kPa.
  5. Step 5: P₂ = 180 − 49.05 = 130.95 kPa.

Worked example 4: Flow downhill

Water descends 8 m at unchanged speed from 120 kPa.

  1. Step 1: Set z₂ − z₁ = −8 m; speed terms cancel.
  2. Step 2: Use P₂ = P₁ − ρg(z₂ − z₁).
  3. Step 3: Calculate −ρg(−8) = +78,480 Pa = +78.48 kPa.
  4. Step 4: P₂ = 120 + 78.48 = 198.48 kPa.
  5. Step 5: Check the sign: a drop in elevation permits a pressure increase.

Worked example 5: Continuity plus Bernoulli

A horizontal 60 mm pipe at 1.5 m/s and 250 kPa narrows to 30 mm.

  1. Step 1: Use continuity first: v₂ = v₁(d₁/d₂)².
  2. Step 2: v₂ = 1.5(60/30)² = 6 m/s.
  3. Step 3: Use P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² − v₂²).
  4. Step 4: Pressure change = 500(2.25 − 36) = −16,875 Pa = −16.875 kPa.
  5. Step 5: P₂ = 250 − 16.875 = 233.125 kPa.
  6. Step 6: State that frictional pressure losses have been neglected.

Worked example 6: Find speed from pressure

At equal elevation, water begins at 2 m/s and pressure drops from 210 kPa to 194 kPa.

  1. Step 1: Calculate pressure drop: P₁ − P₂ = 16,000 Pa.
  2. Step 2: Rearrange v₂² = v₁² + 2(P₁ − P₂)/ρ.
  3. Step 3: Substitute v₂² = 4 + 2(16,000)/1,000 = 36.
  4. Step 4: Take the positive square root: v₂ = 6 m/s.
  5. Step 5: Check that a pressure decrease accompanies a speed increase in this ideal case.

Worked example 7: Engineering recommendation

At 60 mm and 1.5 m/s, water enters horizontally at 220 kPa. Compare 30 mm and 45 mm outlets; minimum outlet pressure is 210 kPa.

  1. Step 1: Candidate A, 30 mm: v₂ = 1.5(60/30)² = 6 m/s.
  2. Step 2: A pressure drop is 16.875 kPa; P₂ = 203.125 kPa, which fails by 6.875 kPa.
  3. Step 3: Candidate B, 45 mm: v₂ = 1.5(60/45)² ≈ 2.667 m/s.
  4. Step 4: B pressure drop = ½(1,000)(1.5² − 2.667²) ≈ −2.431 kPa.
  5. Step 5: B outlet pressure ≈ 217.569 kPa; passes by 7.569 kPa.
  6. Step 6: Recommend B on this pressure constraint; actual pump and friction effects remain untested.

Engineering analysis and misconceptions

“Fast water always has low pressure”

Only an appropriate idealized comparison along a streamline supports a pressure–velocity trade-off. A pump can increase both pressure and flow energy, and different elevations or losses change the balance. Always specify the two locations and assumptions.

“The pressure change depends on the speed difference”

The kinetic contribution contains v². For speeds 2 and 6 m/s, the difference of squares is 32, not 4. Square first, then subtract, and convert pascals to kilopascals at the end.

“Rising water must speed up”

If the pipe diameter stays constant and flow is steady and incompressible, continuity keeps the average speed unchanged. An increase in elevation is balanced by lower pressure in the ideal case, not necessarily by a change in velocity.

“Pressure can be found without continuity”

When a pipe diameter changes and outlet speed is not given, continuity provides the missing velocity. Only then can Bernoulli determine the ideal pressure. Skipping this step creates an underdetermined or inconsistent calculation.

“The virtual pressure is the actual gauge reading”

The simulator excludes friction, pump curves, fittings, local losses, and cavitation. Its ideal pressure may be higher than the real downstream pressure. A numerical pass is a first screening result, not proof of equipment suitability.

Seven independent practice problems

Work each problem independently before expanding the solution. Draw a two-point sketch when elevations differ. Show units at each step, identify which terms cancel, and state whether the answer is physically reasonable.

Practice 1

Water speeds from 3 to 7 m/s in a horizontal ideal pipe; initial pressure is 300 kPa. Find final pressure.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Write P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² − v₂²).
  2. Step 2: Compute 3² − 7² = −40.
  3. Step 3: Pressure change = −20 kPa.
  4. Step 4: P₂ = 280 kPa; the speed increase lowers ideal static pressure.

Practice 2

Water slows from 8 to 4 m/s at equal elevation. If P₁ = 175 kPa, find P₂.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Compute 8² − 4² = 48.
  2. Step 2: Pressure change = 500 × 48 = 24,000 Pa = 24 kPa.
  3. Step 3: P₂ = 175 + 24 = 199 kPa.
  4. Step 4: This is ideal pressure recovery.

Practice 3

Water rises 6 m at constant velocity from a pressure of 250 kPa. Find pressure at the top.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Use P₂ = P₁ − ρgΔz.
  2. Step 2: ρgΔz = 1,000 × 9.81 × 6 = 58,860 Pa.
  3. Step 3: P₂ = 250 − 58.86 = 191.14 kPa.
  4. Step 4: An elevation rise lowers pressure in the ideal model.

Practice 4

Water drops 4 m at constant speed from 130 kPa. Find downstream pressure.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Take Δz = −4 m.
  2. Step 2: P₂ = 130 kPa − (1,000)(9.81)(−4)/1,000 kPa.
  3. Step 3: P₂ = 169.24 kPa.
  4. Step 4: The pressure increases as elevation decreases.

Practice 5

A 50 mm pipe carries water at 2 m/s and 240 kPa into a horizontal 25 mm outlet. Find speed and pressure.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Continuity: v₂ = 2(50/25)² = 8 m/s.
  2. Step 2: Bernoulli pressure change = 500(2² − 8²) = −30,000 Pa.
  3. Step 3: P₂ = 240 − 30 = 210 kPa.
  4. Step 4: The ideal model excludes transition losses.

Practice 6

A horizontal ideal flow begins at 1 m/s. Pressure decreases from 180 to 168 kPa. Find final velocity.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Pressure drop = 12,000 Pa.
  2. Step 2: v₂² = 1² + 2(12,000)/1,000 = 25.
  3. Step 3: v₂ = 5 m/s.
  4. Step 4: A positive square root represents speed magnitude.

Practice 7

A 60 mm inlet carries water at 1.5 m/s and 230 kPa. Compare horizontal 30 mm and 40 mm outlets for minimum pressure 215 kPa.

Reveal numbered solution
  1. Step 1: 30 mm gives v₂ = 6 m/s and P₂ = 230 − 16.875 = 213.125 kPa: fails.
  2. Step 2: 40 mm gives v₂ = 1.5(60/40)² = 3.375 m/s.
  3. Step 3: Pressure change = 500(2.25 − 11.390625) Pa ≈ −4.570 kPa.
  4. Step 4: 40 mm outlet P₂ ≈ 225.430 kPa: passes.
  5. Step 5: Recommend 40 mm on the stated ideal pressure criterion; verify real losses separately.

Technical vocabulary glossary

The highlighted technical terms in the reading are defined below. Expand each entry as needed and use the terms in your engineering recommendation.

Bernoulli’s principle

Mechanical energy balance linking pressure, velocity, and elevation along an idealized streamline.

Static pressure

Local thermodynamic pressure measured without the velocity contribution.

Conservation of mechanical energy

Principle that mechanical energy transfers among forms when no work or losses intervene.

Pressure energy per unit volume

Pressure contribution P to Bernoulli’s equation, measured in Pa.

Dynamic pressure

Kinetic contribution ½ρv², measured in Pa.

Elevation term

Gravitational contribution ρgz, measured in Pa.

Steady flow

Flow properties do not change over time at a fixed point.

Incompressible fluid

Fluid modeled with constant density.

Streamline

Curve tangent to local fluid velocity in a flow field.

Contraction

Pipe transition to a smaller cross-sectional area.

Expansion

Pipe transition to a larger cross-sectional area.

Elevation datum

Chosen zero level for comparing elevations.

Continuity

Conservation relationship connecting mass flow or volume flow across sections.

Pressure head

Equivalent energy per unit weight P/(ρg), measured in meters.

Velocity head

Equivalent kinetic energy per unit weight v²/(2g).

Elevation head

Height z above a chosen datum.

Total pressure

Sum of static and dynamic pressure for an ideal incompressible flow at a fixed elevation.

Pressure tap

Opening used to measure local static pressure.

Gauge pressure

Pressure relative to local atmospheric pressure.

Absolute pressure

Pressure relative to a perfect vacuum.

Venturi

Converging and diverging passage with a narrower throat.

Design constraint

Quantitative requirement that a candidate engineering design must satisfy.

Head loss

Mechanical energy per unit weight dissipated by flow resistance.

Cavitation

Formation and collapse of vapor cavities when local pressure becomes sufficiently low.

Synthesis and preparation for B3.4

Bernoulli’s equation expresses a balance among static pressure, kinetic energy per unit volume, and gravitational energy per unit volume. For ideal horizontal flow, an increase in speed corresponds to a decrease in static pressure. For constant-speed flow rising in elevation, pressure decreases by ρgΔz. In pipe transitions, continuity supplies the velocity change before Bernoulli supplies the pressure change.

These results are valid only within the stated model. The most important professional habit is to identify what the equation leaves out. Real pipe networks lose energy through friction, fittings, and turbulence; pumps may add energy. B3.4 will build on this ideal foundation by examining flow resistance and energy losses.
