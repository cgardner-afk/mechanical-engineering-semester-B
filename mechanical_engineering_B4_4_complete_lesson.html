Core Concepts

Conduction at the particle level

When one end of a metal spoon is placed in hot water, its handle gradually warms even though the spoon itself does not flow toward your hand. Neighboring particles exchange energy, and mobile electrons in metals also transport energy. This is conduction: heat transfer through matter without bulk movement of the material. The driving condition is a temperature difference. Energy moves spontaneously from a warmer region toward a cooler region.

The amount of energy moving each second depends on the temperature difference, the material, the cross-sectional area available for transfer, and the distance heat must travel. Engineers control these variables in insulated buildings, heated machinery, refrigerated containers, and electronic devices. Sometimes the goal is to reduce heat loss; other times the goal is to move heat away as quickly as possible.

Thermal conductivity

Thermal conductivity, symbol k, measures how readily a material conducts heat. Copper and aluminum have high conductivities, making them useful for heat spreaders and heat sinks. Insulating foam has low conductivity, making it useful for slowing unwanted transfer. For classroom calculations, representative conductivities include copper 400, aluminum 205, steel 45, brick 0.70, dry wood 0.12, mineral wool 0.040, and foam 0.035 W/(m·K). These values are approximations; actual materials vary with composition, temperature, moisture, and manufacture.

Fourier's law

Temperature gradient means the change in temperature per distance. A steep gradient indicates a large temperature change across a short distance. Fourier's law relates the gradient to the direction and rate of conductive heat transfer. The negative sign in the directional form indicates that heat moves toward decreasing temperature.

Q̇ = −kA(dT/dx)

For a flat, uniform wall with constant conductivity and fixed surface temperatures, we use a simpler expression for the positive magnitude of heat transfer rate:

Q̇ = kA(Thot − Tcold)/L

Q̇ is in watts, k in W/(m·K), A in m², and thickness L in meters. The temperature difference can be expressed in kelvins or Celsius degrees because differences have the same numerical size. Doubling area doubles heat rate; doubling thickness halves it when all other variables remain unchanged. The simplified model assumes steady-state, one-dimensional conduction and approximately constant material properties.

Thermal resistance

Thermal resistance combines the effects of conductivity, area, and thickness into one quantity. Unlike conductivity, resistance describes a particular layer or assembly, not merely a substance. Two foam panels made from the same material can have different resistances because their thicknesses or areas differ.

Rth = L/(kA)   ·   Q̇ = ΔT/Rth

Resistance is measured in K/W. A wall with resistance 0.50 K/W separating surfaces that differ by 20 K transfers heat at 40 W. Increasing thickness increases resistance; increasing area or conductivity decreases resistance. This electrical-circuit-like relationship is particularly useful for analyzing walls containing several layers.

Layers in series

Imagine a wall made of an inner panel, an insulation layer, and an outer protective panel. When heat passes through these layers one after another along the same path, their thermal resistances add. Under steady-state conditions without heat generation or storage at the interfaces, the same heat transfer rate passes through each layer. However, each layer need not have the same temperature drop: a higher-resistance layer accounts for more of the overall drop.

Rtotal = R1 + R2 + … + Rn
ΔTlayer = Q̇Rlayer

For example, if two layers have resistances of 0.20 and 0.80 K/W and the total temperature difference is 30 K, the heat rate is 30 W. The first layer drops 6 K and the second drops 24 K. With constant conductivity, temperature changes linearly through each individual layer. Engineers use these interface temperatures to evaluate material performance and possible thermal problems.

Thermal bridges and parallel heat paths

A wall made of insulation between metal studs may conduct substantially more heat than a uniform insulation slab. The metal studs create thermal bridges, allowing energy to follow a lower-resistance path. Our series-resistance equation applies when heat passes through one layer and then another along the same path. It does not automatically account for parallel paths through different materials. Engineers must identify the actual heat-flow geometry before choosing a calculation method. In this lesson, you will compare materials in series; a complete wall assembly would also require attention to framing and joints.

What the simplified model does not include

Real thermal systems exchange energy through conduction, convection, and radiation. Convection involves moving fluids such as air or water. Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and does not require matter. Air leaks and thermal bridges—conductive pathways that bypass insulation—can increase heat loss beyond a simple layered-wall prediction. In addition, curved pipes require a different conduction geometry than flat walls. The laboratory below models conduction only, between specified wall-face temperatures, so it should not be mistaken for a full building-energy simulation.

Temperature, time, and the meaning of steady state

Steady state does not mean that energy stops moving. It means that the temperature at each fixed location remains constant even while energy continuously enters and leaves. Consider a well-insulated hot chamber operating for several hours. Once its wall temperatures settle, the heat rate through the wall may become approximately constant. During startup, however, the wall itself warms and stores energy. The simple steady-state formula does not describe that transient period. Understanding this distinction prevents engineers from applying a steady-state prediction to a rapidly changing system without justification.

The rate of heat flow also differs from the total energy transferred. A barrier losing 40 W transfers 40 joules each second. After ten minutes, it has transferred 24,000 joules, assuming the rate stays constant. This cumulative energy may matter when estimating operating cost, but the heat transfer rate is what determines whether an insulation system meets a specified instantaneous performance requirement.

Engineering material choices

A low-conductivity material is desirable when preventing heat loss from a heated enclosure. A high-conductivity material is desirable when removing heat from electronics. Engineers also weigh thickness, weight, price, durability, moisture resistance, safety, and ease of construction. A design that satisfies a mathematical heat-loss target may still be impractical if it is too thick or expensive. This is why engineering design requires both calculations and judgment.

Seven Worked Examples

Example 1: Steel plate

Known information: A=0.50 m², L=0.020 m, k=45 W/(m·K), hot face 80°C, cold face 30°C.

Equation and substitution: Q̇=kAΔT/L=45×0.50×50/0.020=56,250 W.

Interpretation: A thin metal plate conducts rapidly under fixed-face-temperature assumptions.

Example 2: Foam wall

Known information: A=12 m², L=0.10 m, k=0.040, ΔT=20 K.

Equation and substitution: Q̇=0.040×12×20/0.10=96 W. In 1 hour, Q=96×3600=345,600 J.

Interpretation: Watts measure rate; joules measure energy.

Example 3: Copper versus wood

Known information: Both plates have A=0.20 m², L=0.050 m, ΔT=30 K. Copper k=400; wood k=0.12.

Equation and substitution: Copper Q̇=48,000 W; wood Q̇=14.4 W.

Interpretation: Conductivity is useful or undesirable depending on the design goal.

Example 4: Minimum insulation thickness

Known information: k=0.035, A=5 m², ΔT=35 K, allowable Q̇=50 W.

Equation and substitution: L≥kAΔT/Q̇=0.035×5×35/50=0.1225 m.

Interpretation: At least 122.5 mm is needed in this simplified model.

Example 5: Resistance of one layer

Known information: L=0.080 m, k=0.040, A=4 m², ΔT=20 K.

Equation and substitution: R=L/(kA)=0.080/(0.040×4)=0.50 K/W; Q̇=20/0.50=40 W.

Interpretation: Resistance depends on geometry as well as material.

Example 6: Two layers in series

Known information: A=2 m²; first layer L=0.040 m, k=0.20; second L=0.080 m, k=0.040; faces 50°C and 10°C.

Equation and substitution: R₁=0.10 K/W; R₂=1.00 K/W; Rtotal=1.10 K/W; Q̇=40/1.10=36.36 W; interface=50−36.36×0.10=46.36°C.

Interpretation: The layer with greater resistance has the larger temperature drop.

Example 7: Enclosure design

Known information: A=3 m², ΔT=35 K, foam k=0.035, L=0.080 m; target Q̇≤50 W.

Equation and substitution: R=0.080/(0.035×3)=0.7619 K/W; Q̇=35/0.7619=45.94 W.

Interpretation: The conductive heat-loss target is met, but budget and construction constraints still matter.

Engineering Analysis

Review your trials and identify which variables produced the greatest change in heat transfer rate. Explain why a thicker layer raises thermal resistance and why a larger wall area lowers it. If you used two layers, compare their individual temperature drops. The layer with greater resistance should account for the greater temperature change, even though the same heat transfer rate passes through both layers.

Recommend an insulation design that meets the challenge. Report its materials, total thickness, calculated resistance, heat transfer rate, and illustrative cost. Explain why you selected it instead of another design. Finally, identify at least two limitations of the conduction-only model, such as air leakage, convection, radiation, thermal bridges, or uncertain material properties. A professional engineering recommendation includes both numerical evidence and an explanation of model limitations.

Consider a second application: an electronic heat sink. Unlike an insulated wall, the heat sink is intended to move heat rapidly from a warm component toward cooler surroundings. Choosing a high-conductivity metal reduces resistance along that conductive path, but a real heat sink also depends on convection from its fins to the air. This illustrates why an engineer cannot simply label high conductivity as good or bad. The desired direction and magnitude of heat flow determine the design.

Lesson Summary

Conduction moves thermal energy through matter because of a temperature difference. Fourier's law predicts the heat transfer rate through a uniform flat wall: the rate increases with conductivity, area, and temperature difference, but decreases with thickness. Thermal resistance expresses the same relationship in a useful design form and allows multiple series layers to be analyzed by adding resistances.

In a steady-state series wall, each layer carries the same heat transfer rate, while its temperature drop depends on its resistance. Heat transfer rate is measured in watts, whereas transferred energy is measured in joules. Engineering designs must account for material properties, geometric constraints, costs, and the limits of simplified models. Insulation helps retain heat, while high-conductivity materials can be used to remove heat from devices.
