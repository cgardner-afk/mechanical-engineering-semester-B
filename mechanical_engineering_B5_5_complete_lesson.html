Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Why cooling requires work

The Second Law of Thermodynamics tells us that heat spontaneously moves from a warmer region toward a cooler one. A refrigerator accomplishes the opposite useful task: it removes heat from a cold compartment and delivers that heat to warmer surroundings. This transfer is possible because the machine receives work input, usually from an electric motor driving a compressor. It does not make heat disappear; it relocates thermal energy.

A kitchen refrigerator, for example, absorbs energy from food and air inside the cabinet and rejects that energy into the room. The rejected heat includes the heat removed from the compartment plus the energy supplied to operate the machine. This explains why leaving a refrigerator door open does not cool the kitchen: the appliance ultimately adds its electrical energy as heat to the room. Engineers define a system boundary and account for every energy stream before evaluating performance.

2. Refrigerators, air conditioners, and heat pumps

An air conditioner and a refrigerator both aim to remove heat from a cooler region. A heat pump uses a related cycle, but its desired output in heating mode is the heat delivered to a warm space. Many modern heat pumps use a reversing valve so that indoor and outdoor coils exchange roles between heating and cooling seasons.

The physical equipment can therefore serve different purposes. A refrigerator's useful output is cooling a food compartment. An air conditioner's useful output is cooling indoor air. A heat pump's useful output in winter is heating indoor air, even when the outside air feels cold. The performance metric must match the task: cooling performance uses heat removed from the cold side, while heating performance uses heat delivered to the warm side.

3. The vapor-compression cycle

A typical vapor-compression cycle contains four major components: an evaporator, a compressor, a condenser, and an expansion device. The refrigerant circulates through these components. In the evaporator, low-pressure refrigerant absorbs heat from the cooled region. The compressor raises the pressure and temperature of refrigerant vapor using mechanical work.

In the condenser, hot high-pressure refrigerant rejects heat to the warmer environment and typically condenses into liquid. The expansion device reduces pressure, producing a colder refrigerant mixture before it returns to the evaporator. These four stages repeat continuously. The compressor is the principal work-input device; the expansion device primarily lowers pressure and does not provide the work needed to drive heat against its spontaneous direction. A diagram of the components should be read as a loop, not as four unrelated machines.

4. Refrigerants and phase changes

A phase change occurs when a substance changes between liquid and vapor. During evaporation, refrigerant absorbs substantial energy while changing to vapor. During condensation, it releases energy while changing back to liquid. The energy involved in a phase change is often described as latent heat. This allows a refrigeration system to move significant amounts of energy through circulating refrigerant.

The boiling or saturation temperature of a refrigerant depends on its pressure. The compressor and expansion device create different pressure conditions so that evaporation can occur at a relatively low temperature and condensation at a higher temperature. Engineers choose refrigerants based on operating pressures, temperatures, safety, efficiency, environmental impact, and equipment compatibility. Actual refrigerant properties require detailed data, but the basic cycle can be understood without property tables.

5. The First Law energy balance

For a complete refrigeration cycle operating steadily, energy conservation gives QH = QL + Win. Here QL is the heat removed from the cold region, QH is the heat delivered to the warmer region, and Win is the net work input. These are positive magnitudes measured over the same time interval. The equation also applies to rates: Q̇H = Q̇L + Ẇin.

Suppose a refrigerator removes 600 kJ from its cabinet and requires 200 kJ of electrical work over a given period. It rejects 800 kJ to the kitchen. The extra 200 kJ is not created by the refrigerant; it comes from the electrical supply. A useful habit is to check that the warm-side heat output equals the cold-side heat input plus work input. This balance is required regardless of whether the system is being used for cooling or heating.

QH = QL + Win

6. Refrigerator coefficient of performance

The coefficient of performance, abbreviated COP, measures useful thermal transfer relative to the work required. For a refrigerator, COPR = QL/Win. If a system removes 900 kJ of heat while consuming 300 kJ of work, its refrigerator COP is 3.0. That means 3 kJ of cooling are provided for every 1 kJ of work input.

COP is dimensionless, but it is not the same as the thermal efficiency of a heat engine. A heat engine is judged by the fraction of heat input converted to work. A refrigerator is judged by the amount of heat moved for a given work input. A higher COP generally means less electricity is needed to provide the same cooling load, assuming comparable operating conditions and equipment boundaries.

COPR = QL/Win

7. Heat-pump coefficient of performance

In heating mode, the useful output is the heat delivered indoors. Thus COPHP = QH/Win. Because QH = QL + Win, the idealized relationship for the same cycle and operating conditions is COPHP = COPR + 1. For example, if a cycle has a cooling COP of 3, its heating COP is 4.

This relationship does not imply that the machine has become more efficient merely by changing the label. The heating and cooling COPs measure different useful outputs. In cooling mode, the useful output is heat removed from the cold space. In heating mode, the useful output is heat delivered to the warm space, which includes the supplied work. Students should always identify the intended service before choosing the COP equation.

COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1

8. Why COP can be greater than one

A heat pump with COPHP = 4 delivers four units of heat for each unit of work input. This does not violate the First Law because three of those units can be absorbed from the colder surroundings, while the remaining unit comes from the electrical work. The device transfers existing thermal energy rather than converting work into more energy than it contains.

For comparison, an ideal electric resistance heater converts approximately one unit of electrical energy into one unit of heat at the point of use. A heat pump can deliver more heat per unit of electricity because it draws additional thermal energy from the outdoor environment. This advantage depends on operating conditions; the amount of work required changes as the temperature difference increases.

9. Temperature lift and Carnot COP limits

The temperature lift is the difference between warm-side and cold-side reservoir temperatures. A larger lift generally makes it harder to move heat and lowers the maximum achievable COP. For ideal reversible devices operating between two fixed-temperature reservoirs, COPR,Carnot = TL/(TH − TL) and COPHP,Carnot = TH/(TH − TL). Both temperatures must be in Kelvin.

For example, if TL = 280 K and TH = 320 K, the ideal refrigerator COP is 280/40 = 7 and the ideal heat-pump COP is 320/40 = 8. Actual machines perform below these ideal reversible limits. Using Celsius values directly in these ratios is incorrect. A device cannot operate between equal-temperature reservoirs under this idealized expression because the denominator would be zero; the simulation will require a positive temperature difference.

COPR,Carnot = TL/(TH − TL)   ·   COPHP,Carnot = TH/(TH − TL)

10. Capacity, electricity cost, and design tradeoffs

Cooling capacity and heating capacity are rates of heat transfer, commonly measured in kilowatts (kW). Energy transferred over a period is capacity multiplied by time. Electricity cost can be estimated using electrical power in kW, operating time in hours, and the utility price in dollars per kilowatt-hour. For example, a 2 kW compressor operating for 5 hours uses 10 kWh of electricity.

Engineers compare equipment based on capacity, COP, operating temperature, initial cost, maintenance, refrigerant leakage, and environmental impacts. A system with excellent COP but insufficient capacity cannot meet the required heating or cooling load. A system that meets capacity but uses too much electricity may violate a cost constraint. Real COP varies with operating conditions, defrost cycles, and component losses; the simplified laboratory uses stated values so students can isolate the governing relationships.

Seven Worked Examples

1. Refrigeration energy balance

Situation: A refrigerator removes 750 kJ from a cold cabinet while using 250 kJ of work.

QH = QL + Win = 750 + 250 = 1000 kJ.

Engineering interpretation: The condenser rejects more heat than the evaporator absorbs because work is added.

2. Refrigerator COP

Situation: A cooling unit removes 1200 kJ of heat while using 300 kJ of work.

COPR = QL/Win = 1200/300 = 4.0.

Engineering interpretation: The system moves four units of heat from the cold space per unit of work.

3. Heat-pump COP

Situation: A heat pump delivers 1500 kJ of heat using 375 kJ of work.

COPHP = QH/Win = 1500/375 = 4.0.

Engineering interpretation: Each unit of work results in four units of delivered heating.

4. Required work input

Situation: A refrigerator must remove 1800 kJ of heat and has COPR = 3.0.

Win = QL/COPR = 1800/3 = 600 kJ.

Engineering interpretation: Required work can be found by rearranging the COP equation.

5. Cooling and heating COP for the same cycle

Situation: A system removes 900 kJ from a cold region and uses 300 kJ of work.

COPR = 900/300 = 3.0. QH = 1200 kJ; COPHP = 1200/300 = 4.0.

Engineering interpretation: The heating COP is one greater for the same idealized cycle.

6. Electrical consumption and cost

Situation: A 2 kW heat-pump compressor operates for 6 hours at $0.15 per kWh.

Energy = 2 × 6 = 12 kWh. Cost = 12 × $0.15 = $1.80.

Engineering interpretation: Power multiplied by time gives electrical energy; cost depends on the tariff.

7. Constrained heat-pump design

Situation: A heating system delivers 12 kW at COPHP = 4.0. Requirements: at least 10 kW heating, no more than 3.5 kW electric input, and COP at least 3.5.

Ẇin = 12/4 = 3 kW. Heating = 12 kW; COP = 4.0.

Engineering interpretation: All three stated requirements are satisfied in this model.

Problem 1: A refrigerator removes 640 kJ and consumes 160 kJ of work. Find rejected heat.

Worked solution: QH = 640 + 160 = 800 kJ.

Problem 2: A refrigerator removes 1500 kJ and uses 500 kJ of work. Find COP.

Worked solution: COPR = 1500/500 = 3.0.

Problem 3: A heat pump delivers 2400 kJ and uses 600 kJ of work. Find COP.

Worked solution: COPHP = 2400/600 = 4.0.

Problem 4: A cooling system must remove 2100 kJ at COPR = 3.5. Find required work.

Worked solution: Win = 2100/3.5 = 600 kJ.

Problem 5: A device removes 1200 kJ and uses 400 kJ of work. Find both COP values.

Worked solution: COPR = 1200/400 = 3; QH = 1600 kJ; COPHP = 1600/400 = 4.

Problem 6: A 1.5 kW compressor runs 8 hours at $0.20/kWh. Find energy and cost.

Worked solution: Energy = 1.5 × 8 = 12 kWh; cost = 12 × $0.20 = $2.40.

Problem 7: A heat pump supplies 9 kW at COPHP = 3.0. It must supply at least 8 kW with no more than 2.5 kW electrical input. Does it qualify?

Worked solution: Electrical input = 9/3 = 3 kW. Heating capacity passes, but electricity input exceeds 2.5 kW, so the design fails.

Lesson Summary

Refrigerators, air conditioners, and heat pumps use work input to transfer heat from a colder region to a warmer region. A typical vapor-compression cycle includes an evaporator, compressor, condenser, and expansion device. The refrigerant absorbs heat while evaporating, is compressed to a higher pressure, rejects heat while condensing, and returns through a pressure-reducing device. The First Law requires that heat delivered to the warm side equals heat removed from the cold side plus work input.

Refrigerator COP compares cooling with work input; heat-pump COP compares heating with work input. Both can exceed one because the machine transfers environmental heat in addition to using electrical energy. Carnot COP provides an ideal upper limit based on Kelvin reservoir temperatures, and real equipment must also meet heating or cooling capacity, power, cost, and safety requirements.

COPR = QL/Win   ·   COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1
