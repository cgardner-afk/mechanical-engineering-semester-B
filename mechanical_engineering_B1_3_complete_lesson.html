Torque, Rotational Work, and Power

Mechanical Engineering B · Unit B1

What you will learn

Mechanical engineering is not only about making things move. It is about producing useful motion under real constraints: how much turning force is available, how far a component rotates, how rapidly energy can be delivered, and whether the machine is capable of the job. By the end of this lesson, you will calculate torque, rotational work, and rotational power; convert rotational speed from revolutions per minute (rpm) into radians per second; and determine the torque required when power and speed are known.

This lesson supports the Unit B1 assessment and the cumulative engineering final. The important skill is not memorizing a particular set of numbers. It is choosing an appropriate equation, understanding every symbol, keeping units consistent, and explaining whether the calculated result makes engineering sense.

Engineering habit: Before substituting numbers, write down what is known, what is requested, and the units. A mathematically correct number with incorrect units is not a complete engineering answer.

Core engineering principles

1 · What torque means

Imagine trying to open a heavy door by pushing near its hinges. You may push hard, but the door barely turns. Move the same push to the handle and the door swings much more easily. The force has not changed; the distance from the turning axis has. This turning effect of a force is called torque, sometimes called a moment of force. Engineers use torque to describe what wrenches, crank arms, motors, and shafts do when they try to rotate something.

The axis of rotation is the line about which an object turns. For a wrench, think of the center of the bolt. The moment arm is the perpendicular distance from that axis to the force's line of action. The line of action is an imaginary straight line extending along the direction of the applied force. This distinction matters: the moment arm is not always the entire length of the wrench.

For a force applied at a right angle to a rigid lever, torque magnitude is τ = Fr. The symbol τ is the Greek letter tau and represents torque; F is force in newtons (N); and r is the perpendicular moment arm in meters (m). Torque is measured in newton-meters (N·m). A 20 N perpendicular push 0.30 m from a pivot creates 6 N·m of torque. The equation assumes the force and distance are measured relative to the same axis.

If the force is angled rather than perpendicular, only its turning component counts. The more general torque-magnitude equation is |τ| = rF|sin φ|. Here φ (phi) is the angle between the lever's radial direction and the applied force; sin is the sine function on your calculator; and the vertical bars mean magnitude, or the nonnegative size of a quantity. When φ = 90°, sin φ = 1 and the full force produces torque. When φ = 0°, sin φ = 0 and a force directed straight toward or away from the pivot produces no torque about that pivot.

2 · Which direction does the torque act?

A turning force can try to rotate an object clockwise or counterclockwise. In a flat drawing, we usually choose counterclockwise as positive and clockwise as negative. This is a sign convention: a shared rule for describing direction. For example, if one force produces +9 N·m and another produces −4 N·m about the same axis, their combined torque is +5 N·m. The result tends to turn the object counterclockwise. A negative answer does not mean the calculation failed; it tells you the turning direction under the chosen convention.

Torque can be present even when a machine is not moving. A person holding a wrench against a bolt may apply torque while the bolt remains stationary because resisting torque balances the applied torque. A motor may similarly generate torque at a stopped shaft. Therefore, torque alone does not tell us how much mechanical power is actually being transferred by rotation.

3 · From torque to rotational work

In straight-line motion, mechanical work occurs when a force causes displacement along its direction. For rotation, the corresponding idea is that torque acting through an angular displacement transfers energy. The Greek letter Δ (delta) means “change in,” and θ (theta) means angle. Therefore, Δθ is the angle turned, measured in radians. For a constant torque acting in the direction of rotation, W = τΔθ, where W is mechanical work measured in joules (J). The angle must be expressed in radians, not degrees.

Suppose a shaft delivers 8 N·m of constant torque while rotating through 3 radians. It transfers 24 J of work. If it rotates through 6 radians under the same torque, it transfers 48 J. This simple proportional relationship is useful, but it is an idealization: actual torque can vary during rotation, and energy may be lost to friction, heat, or deformation. When torque changes, the total work must be found by accounting for that variation rather than multiplying one arbitrary torque value by the whole angle.

Notice a subtle unit distinction. Torque is measured in N·m, and one joule also equals one N·m dimensionally. Nevertheless, torque and work are different quantities. Torque describes a turning effect; work describes energy transferred through motion. Engineers report work in joules and torque in newton-meters so the meaning is clear. A wrench applying 10 N·m to an unmoving bolt can create torque without doing rotational work on the bolt.

4 · Power is how quickly work happens

Two machines can transfer the same amount of energy yet differ greatly in usefulness. A hoist that lifts a load in two seconds delivers energy more quickly than one that takes twenty seconds. Power is the rate at which work is performed or energy is transferred. Average power is P = W / Δt, where P is power in watts (W), W is work in joules, and Δt is elapsed time in seconds. The symbol W is used both as the variable for work and as the abbreviation for watts; the context tells you which is intended. One watt equals one joule per second. One kilowatt (kW) equals 1,000 watts.

Consider two electric tools that each transfer 600 J of useful mechanical energy. If the first does so in 3 s, its average output power is 200 W. If the second takes 6 s, its average output power is 100 W. The work is equal, but the first tool delivers it at twice the rate. This distinction matters when specifying a machine for production speed, cycle time, or lifting performance.

5 · Rotational power links torque and angular speed

For steady rotation with torque acting in the direction of motion, the shaft's mechanical power is P = τω. Here τ is the torque in N·m and ω (omega) is angular speed in radians per second (rad/s). Their product gives power in watts. This follows from dividing rotational work, W = τΔθ, by time: P = τ(Δθ/Δt). Since Δθ/Δt is angular speed for steady one-direction rotation, P = τω. The equation connects a machine's turning effort with how fast it turns.

Motor and shaft labels often give speed in rpm, meaning revolutions per minute, rather than rad/s. Before using P = τω, convert rpm to rad/s: ω = 2πN/60, where N is the numeric rotational speed in rpm, π is approximately 3.14159, 2π radians make one revolution, and 60 seconds make one minute. The revolutions and minutes cancel during conversion. For example, 120 rpm becomes 2π(120)/60 = 4π ≈ 12.57 rad/s.

At the same torque, doubling rotational speed doubles power. At the same angular speed, doubling torque doubles power. However, an actual motor has a limited torque-speed operating range, so it may not be capable of doubling either quantity independently. The equation describes mechanical output at a given operating point; it does not promise that any particular motor can produce that point.

6 · Finding torque when power is known

Sometimes an engineer knows the power required by a machine and its intended shaft speed but needs to determine the torque. Rearrange the equation to τ = P/ω, provided angular speed is not zero. At a fixed nonzero power, lower angular speed requires greater torque; higher speed requires less torque. This is a key reason that mechanical transmissions can exchange rotational speed for torque, although real transmissions also have efficiency losses.

For instance, a shaft delivering 1,000 W at 20 rad/s must supply 50 N·m of torque. If it could deliver the same 1,000 W at 10 rad/s, it would need 100 N·m. These are operating-point calculations, not claims that the same motor can always sustain both conditions. At exactly zero shaft speed, the formula τ = P/ω cannot be used: division by zero is undefined, and shaft mechanical output power P = τω is zero even if a stationary motor produces torque.

7 · What real machines add

The equations in this lesson calculate idealized mechanical work and shaft output power. An electric motor takes in electrical power, but not all of it becomes useful shaft power. Some energy becomes heat in windings, bearings, and other components. A gearbox can change shaft speed and torque, but it cannot create energy. If an ideal gearbox halves output speed, its output torque may double while power remains the same. A real gearbox delivers somewhat less output power because of friction and other losses.

Engineers therefore distinguish input power, useful output power, and efficiency. Efficiency is the fraction of input power that emerges as useful output. If a machine receives 500 W and delivers 400 W of useful mechanical output, its efficiency is 400/500 = 0.80, or 80%. This concept prepares you for later lessons on gears, belts, and mechanical drives. Do not use a torque-speed equation to claim that a machine creates extra energy merely by increasing torque.

Worked engineering examples

Each example models the same problem-solving sequence: identify what is known, choose the appropriate relationship, substitute quantities with consistent units, and interpret the result. Work through each calculation before reading its explanation.

Worked example 1 · Perpendicular force

Problem: A technician pushes perpendicular to a 0.45 m wrench with 80 N. Find the torque.

Identify: Known: F = 80 N; r = 0.45 m; force is perpendicular.

Calculate: τ = Fr = (80 N)(0.45 m) = 36 N·m.

Interpret: Moving the hand farther from the bolt would increase torque for the same force.

Worked example 2 · Angled force

Problem: A 50 N force acts at 30° to a 0.40 m crank arm. Find the torque magnitude.

Identify: Known: F = 50 N; r = 0.40 m; φ = 30°.

Calculate: |τ| = rF|sin φ| = (0.40)(50)(0.5) = 10 N·m.

Interpret: Only the component perpendicular to the arm contributes to the turning effect.

Worked example 3 · Opposing torques

Problem: Two forces produce +14 N·m and −9 N·m about the same axis. Find net torque and direction.

Identify: Choose counterclockwise positive; clockwise negative.

Calculate: τ_net = +14 + (−9) = +5 N·m.

Interpret: The positive sign indicates a counterclockwise tendency, not necessarily actual motion.

Worked example 4 · Rotational work

Problem: A shaft supplies a constant 15 N·m of torque through half a revolution. Find the work.

Identify: Half a revolution = π radians; torque acts in direction of rotation.

Calculate: W = τΔθ = 15π = 47.12 J.

Interpret: The answer is energy transferred, so write joules rather than N·m.

Worked example 5 · Power using rad/s

Problem: A shaft delivers 22 N·m at a steady 8 rad/s. Find shaft power.

Identify: Torque and angular speed are already in compatible SI units.

Calculate: P = τω = 22 × 8 = 176 W.

Interpret: Power combines the turning effort with how quickly the shaft rotates.

Worked example 6 · Power using rpm

Problem: A drive shaft delivers 16 N·m at 150 rpm. Find shaft power.

Identify: First convert speed: ω = 2π(150)/60 = 5π rad/s.

Calculate: P = 16(5π) = 80π = 251.33 W.

Interpret: Using 150 directly as ω would be incorrect because rpm is not rad/s.

Worked example 7 · Torque from power

Problem: A machine requires 900 W at 300 rpm. Find the torque required.

Identify: ω = 2π(300)/60 = 10π ≈ 31.416 rad/s.

Calculate: τ = P/ω = 900/(10π) = 28.65 N·m.

Interpret: A motor must be able to deliver at least this torque at the specified operating speed, with allowance for losses.

Try every question before opening its solution. The questions are always visible; the step-by-step answers are deliberately hidden so you can practice selecting the equation yourself.

Practice 1

A mechanic applies a perpendicular 75 N force at the end of a 0.32 m wrench. Find torque.

Show step-by-step solution

τ = Fr = 75 × 0.32 = 24.0 N·m. Force is perpendicular, so no angle correction is needed.

Practice 2

A 90 N force acts at 30° to a 0.60 m lever. Calculate torque magnitude.

Show step-by-step solution

|τ| = rF sin φ = 0.60 × 90 × sin 30° = 54 × 0.5 = 27.0 N·m.

Practice 3

Two forces produce +18 N·m and −26 N·m about the same pivot. Find the net torque and its direction.

Show step-by-step solution

τ_net = +18 − 26 = −8 N·m. Under the counterclockwise-positive convention, the negative sign means clockwise.

Practice 4

A shaft turns through 4 radians under a constant 12 N·m torque in the direction of rotation. Find the work.

Show step-by-step solution

W = τΔθ = 12 × 4 = 48 J. Work is energy transferred and is reported in joules.

Practice 5

A shaft delivers 35 N·m of torque at 6 rad/s. Calculate power.

Show step-by-step solution

P = τω = 35 × 6 = 210 W. No speed conversion is required.

Practice 6

A machine shaft produces 24 N·m at 250 rpm. Find mechanical output power to the nearest watt.

Show step-by-step solution

ω = 2π(250)/60 ≈ 26.180 rad/s. P = 24 × 26.180 ≈ 628 W. Converting rpm first is essential.

Practice 7

A drive must deliver 1.5 kW at 600 rpm. Find the required torque to one decimal place.

Show step-by-step solution

Convert 1.5 kW to 1500 W. ω = 2π(600)/60 = 20π ≈ 62.832 rad/s. τ = 1500/62.832 ≈ 23.9 N·m.

Engineering vocabulary

Axis of rotation: the line about which an object turns. Force: a push or pull measured in newtons. Torque (τ, tau): the turning effect of a force about an axis, measured in newton-meters. Moment arm: the perpendicular distance from an axis to a force's line of action. Line of action: the straight line extending in the direction of an applied force. φ (phi): the angle between the lever's radial direction and an applied force in the general torque equation.

Angular displacement (Δθ): change in angle, usually measured in radians. Delta (Δ): Greek letter meaning “change in.” Theta (θ): Greek letter representing angle. Angular speed (ω, omega): rate of angular motion, measured in rad/s. Rotational work (W): energy transferred by torque through an angle, measured in joules. Power (P): rate of energy transfer, measured in watts. Elapsed time (Δt): change in time, measured in seconds. Revolutions per minute (rpm): number of complete turns in one minute; N is the numerical rpm value in the conversion equation.

Efficiency: useful output power divided by input power. Operating point: a particular combination of torque and speed at which a machine runs. Sign convention: an agreed choice for positive and negative directions. Idealized model: a simplified calculation that omits some real-world effects such as friction or heating.

What to remember

Torque tells you how strongly a force tends to turn something. The size of that turning effect depends on the force, the distance from the axis, and the force direction. Rotational work describes energy transferred when torque acts through an angle. Power describes how quickly that energy is transferred. These ideas connect in the relationship P = τω, which is especially useful for rotating shafts, motors, and drives.

Always define the axis, use a perpendicular moment arm, express angular displacement in radians, and convert rpm to rad/s before calculating shaft power. Check whether a negative torque result represents clockwise direction under your convention. Remember that a stationary shaft can experience torque without delivering rotational shaft power, and that increasing output torque through a transmission does not create additional energy.

The next lesson, B1.4, will apply these relationships to gears and gear trains. You will examine how connected rotating components change speed and torque while respecting the power and energy limits introduced here.
