Prerequisite support

Math and vocabulary checkpoint

Lesson B1.2 introduced rpm and angular speed. Lesson B1.3 established torque and rotational power. Lesson B1.4 used ratios to analyze gears. Belt drives use the same reasoning but compare pulley diameters instead of gear tooth counts. An input of 600 rpm means 600 complete shaft turns each minute.

To simplify a ratio, divide both numbers by the same factor. For example, 75/150 = 1/2. If a 75-mm driver turns a 150-mm follower, the output makes half as many turns in the ideal model. Both pulley diameters must use the same units; comparing 0.075 m to 150 mm without conversion would produce an incorrect ratio.

Percentages must be converted to decimal fractions before multiplication. A 4% slip factor is 0.04, so the retained speed factor is 1 − 0.04 = 0.96. An 85% efficiency is 0.85, so useful power equals 0.85 times input power. These percentages represent different measurements and should not be substituted for one another.

A pulley has a circumference of πD, where π is approximately 3.14159 and D is diameter. This fact connects pulley diameter to belt travel. Students should be comfortable multiplying a fraction by an rpm value and dividing watts by angular speed when calculating torque.

Engineering principles

Belt drives, speed ratios, and losses

Essential formulas · Quick reference

Symbol key: N = rotational speed in revolutions per minute (rpm); D = effective pulley diameter (mm or m, using the same unit in a ratio); R = dimensionless speed-reduction ratio; s = slip fraction (for example, 3% = 0.03); η (eta) = efficiency fraction (for example, 85% = 0.85); P = power in watts (W); τ (tau) = torque in newton-meters (N·m); ω (omega) = angular speed in radians per second (rad/s); π (pi) ≈ 3.14159. Subscripts in and out identify input and output. Actual output speed in the slip model is an approximation; power efficiency is a separate quantity.

Direction: In the usual parallel-shaft arrangement, an open belt turns the driven pulley in the same direction as the driver; a crossed belt turns it in the opposite direction. Direction does not change the speed magnitude equation.

1 · Why engineers use belt drives

A mechanical drive transfers motion and power from a motor to another machine component. A belt drive connects a driving pulley to a driven pulley with a flexible continuous belt. Unlike two meshing gears, the pulleys can be separated by a relatively long distance. A ventilation fan, water pump, woodworking machine, and conveyor may all use belts to connect a motor to the equipment it operates.

Belt drives can be economical, relatively quiet, and convenient to maintain. Some belts can slip when overloaded, which may limit sudden forces but can also make speed less predictable. Other belt systems use teeth on both the belt and pulley to prevent ordinary frictional slip. Selecting a drive means balancing cost, space, speed accuracy, load, efficiency, and maintenance rather than choosing one transmission for every machine.

2 · Driver, follower, and belt anatomy

The driver pulley is attached to the input shaft and receives motion from the motor. The driven pulley, also called the follower, is attached to the output shaft. A belt wraps around both pulley rims. Its two straight sections between pulleys are called belt spans. In a typical operating friction-belt drive, one span has higher tension than the other, and their tension difference produces the torque transmitted to the driven pulley.

3 · Equal belt speed produces the diameter ratio

A point on the rim of a pulley travels a distance equal to its circumference during one revolution. Circumference is πD, where D is the effective diameter. If a pulley completes N revolutions per minute, the belt's ideal linear travel per minute is πDN. A single no-slip belt must move at the same linear speed where it contacts both pulleys. Therefore πDinNin = πDoutNout, and π cancels to give the familiar diameter-ratio equation.

A smaller driven pulley rotates faster because it needs more revolutions to pass the same belt length. A larger driven pulley rotates more slowly. For instance, a 70-mm driver at 600 rpm turning a 140-mm follower gives 600 × 70/140 = 300 rpm. This is a 2:1 speed reduction. If you accidentally invert the fraction and obtain 1200 rpm, compare the pulley sizes: the larger follower should not rotate faster in the no-slip model.

4 · Reduction, increase, and pulley selection

A reduction ratio R is defined here as driven diameter divided by driver diameter. A ratio greater than one reduces output speed. A ratio smaller than one increases it. For a 40-mm driver and 120-mm follower, R = 3, meaning the input makes three revolutions while the output makes one. A 120-mm driver and 40-mm follower instead produces a threefold speed increase.

5 · Open and crossed belts

In an open belt arrangement, the belt does not cross itself between pulleys. With parallel shafts viewed from the same side, the driver and follower rotate in the same direction. In a crossed belt arrangement, the belt spans cross, so the follower turns in the opposite direction. These direction rules assume conventional pulley geometry and a fixed viewpoint. Changing your viewpoint can reverse the words clockwise and counterclockwise.

Crossing a belt can cause rubbing where the spans pass one another, and some belt types are not suitable for twisting or crossing. A crossed arrangement is not automatically a recommended practical design merely because its direction is easy to predict. Real machines may instead use a different motor direction, intermediate shaft, or gear arrangement. For our laboratory, the crossed option illustrates kinematics and direction; it does not model interference or belt wear.

6 · Slip and speed accuracy

Friction belts rely on contact forces between the belt and pulley. If the belt moves relative to the pulley surface, the actual speed relationship differs from the ideal no-slip equation. Slip may increase with inadequate belt tension, overload, worn surfaces, contamination, or poor alignment. A small amount of elastic belt creep can also alter the measured speed ratio. Synchronous belts use meshing teeth and are chosen when precise indexing is especially important.

For a simplified introductory calculation, define s as the fractional loss of output speed relative to the ideal predicted output. Then Nactual ≈ Nideal(1 − s). If the ideal output is 400 rpm and s = 0.04, the modeled output is 400 × 0.96 = 384 rpm. This is a deliberately simplified performance model, not a universal equation for all belt materials and operating conditions. The laboratory uses the same stated convention so that its results are reproducible.

7 · Tension, friction, and load

The belt transmits tangential force through a difference between the tensions of its tight and slack spans. The driven pulley converts that tangential force into shaft torque. A larger effective radius provides a larger moment arm for the same tangential force. Increasing belt tension can improve traction within the manufacturer's limits, but excessive tension can overload bearings, stretch the belt, and reduce service life.

8 · Power, efficiency, and output torque

The motor supplies input power Pin. Some energy becomes heat, sound, and deformation rather than useful shaft output. Define efficiency η = Pout/Pin. A drive that receives 800 W and operates at 85% efficiency supplies 0.85 × 800 = 680 W of useful output power. The remaining 120 W is lost from the useful mechanical output under this simplified accounting. Efficiency cannot exceed 100% for an ordinary passive transmission.

Rotational power is P = τω, where torque τ is in newton-meters and angular speed ω is in radians per second. To convert rpm to rad/s, use ω = 2πN/60. Thus if a drive supplies 628 W at 300 rpm, its output torque is 628/(2π × 300/60), approximately 20.0 N·m. This calculation uses actual output speed and actual useful power, so it is appropriate even when the belt-drive model includes losses.

9 · Selecting a drive for a real machine

Suppose a fan motor turns at 900 rpm but the fan shaft should turn at 600 rpm. A 100-mm driver with a 150-mm follower provides the required ideal ratio. If the motor delivers 1000 W and the belt drive is 90% efficient, the useful output power is 900 W. If the model predicts 2% slip, the actual output speed is approximately 588 rpm rather than 600 rpm. A designer must decide whether this speed difference is acceptable for the airflow requirement.

Belt tension, pulley alignment, guarding, service intervals, temperature, contamination, and belt wear are all practical concerns.

Worked example 1 · A reduction for a ventilation fan

Given and find: A 75-mm motor pulley runs at 960 rpm and drives a 200-mm fan pulley. Find ideal fan speed.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Nout = 960 × (75/200) = 360 rpm.

Engineering interpretation: The larger follower rotates more slowly. The ratio is 200/75 ≈ 2.67:1.

Worked example 2 · Speed increase for a small spindle

Given and find: A 150-mm driver turns at 400 rpm and drives a 60-mm follower. Find ideal speed.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Nout = 400 × (150/60) = 1000 rpm.

Engineering interpretation: The smaller follower rotates 2.5 times as fast; output torque capability is correspondingly lower in an ideal system.

Worked example 3 · Selecting the driven diameter

Given and find: An 80-mm driver at 750 rpm must drive a shaft at 500 rpm. Find the ideal driven diameter.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Dout = Din × Nin/Nout = 80 × 750/500 = 120 mm.

Engineering interpretation: A larger follower gives the required reduction; confirm that an appropriate pulley is available.

Worked example 4 · Open versus crossed direction

Given and find: An input shaft turns clockwise at 600 rpm with a 90-mm driver and a 135-mm follower. Compare open and crossed arrangements.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Speed = 600 × 90/135 = 400 rpm for either ideal arrangement. Open: clockwise. Crossed: counterclockwise.

Engineering interpretation: Crossing reverses direction without changing the theoretical diameter ratio.

Worked example 5 · Estimating output with slip

Given and find: An ideal calculation predicts 450 rpm, but the specified simplified slip fraction is 4%. Estimate actual output speed.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Nactual ≈ 450 × (1 − 0.04) = 432 rpm.

Engineering interpretation: The 18-rpm decrease is a modeled speed deviation, not an automatic 4% power-efficiency loss.

Worked example 6 · Calculating useful output power

Given and find: A belt drive receives 1250 W and has 88% overall efficiency. Find useful output power and lost power.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Pout = 0.88 × 1250 = 1100 W; loss = 1250 − 1100 = 150 W.

Engineering interpretation: The efficiency describes power transfer, not the percentage of speed lost to slip.

Worked example 7 · Combined design with torque

Given and find: A 90-mm driver at 900 rpm drives a 180-mm follower. Input power is 800 W, overall efficiency 90%, and modeled slip 2%. Find actual speed, useful power, and output torque.

Model, substitution, and calculation: Nideal = 900 × 90/180 = 450 rpm; Nactual ≈ 450 × 0.98 = 441 rpm. Pout = 0.90 × 800 = 720 W. ωout = 2π × 441/60 ≈ 46.18 rad/s. τout = 720/46.18 ≈ 15.6 N·m.

Engineering interpretation: Speed slip and overall power efficiency are treated separately. Torque uses actual modeled speed and useful output power.

Investigation A · Diameter ratio prediction

Set input to 900 rpm, driver diameter to 80 mm, and driven diameter to 160 mm. Predict the ideal output speed before reading the metric. Record the result, then double the driver diameter while keeping the follower unchanged. Compare the two results and explain why the speed changes.

Engineering analysis

Misconceptions and design checks

Diameter ratio orientation

A large follower must turn more slowly in a no-slip belt drive. If your answer predicts that a 160-mm follower turns twice as fast as an 80-mm driver, the ratio is inverted. Write Nout = Nin × Din/Dout before substituting and predict the direction of the speed change.

Slip is not efficiency

The laboratory's slip percentage measures modeled output-speed reduction from the ideal result. Efficiency measures useful output power relative to input power. A 5% slip setting does not automatically mean 95% power efficiency. Do not double-count losses by multiplying output power by both a specified overall efficiency and an additional slip factor.

Use the correct speed when calculating torque

When calculating output torque from useful power, convert the actual modeled output rpm to rad/s. If a belt slips, actual output speed differs from ideal output speed. Using ideal rpm with actual output power produces a torque estimate inconsistent with the specified model.

Direction and practical belt geometry

Open and crossed arrangements differ in rotation direction for parallel shafts. A crossed belt may rub or twist in a real machine, so a direction calculation is not sufficient to approve a physical design. Always check belt type, alignment, guarding, and manufacturer recommendations.

Practice 1

A 65-mm driver at 840 rpm drives a 130-mm follower. Find ideal output rpm.

Show step-by-step solution

Nout = 840 × 65/130 = 420 rpm. The follower is twice the diameter and rotates at half the speed.

Practice 2

A 180-mm driver at 360 rpm drives a 72-mm follower. Find ideal output rpm.

Show step-by-step solution

Nout = 360 × 180/72 = 900 rpm. The smaller follower rotates faster.

Practice 3

A 60-mm driver at 1000 rpm must produce 400 rpm ideally. Find the required follower diameter.

Show step-by-step solution

Dout = 60 × 1000/400 = 150 mm. Check: 1000 × 60/150 = 400 rpm.

Practice 4

An input pulley turns counterclockwise. Predict the follower's direction in open and crossed arrangements.

Show step-by-step solution

Open: counterclockwise. Crossed: clockwise. The crossing changes direction, not the ideal speed magnitude.

Practice 5

A belt system predicts 560 rpm ideally. The specified slip model uses 5% speed slip. Find actual modeled speed.

Show step-by-step solution

Nactual ≈ 560 × (1 − 0.05) = 532 rpm. The modeled deviation is 28 rpm.

Practice 6

A drive receives 950 W and delivers 760 W. Find its efficiency and useful power loss.

Show step-by-step solution

η = 760/950 = 0.80 = 80%. Power loss = 950 − 760 = 190 W.

Practice 7

A 100-mm driver at 600 rpm turns a 150-mm follower. Overall efficiency is 80%, input power 600 W, and modeled slip 4%. Find output rpm and torque.

Show step-by-step solution

Nideal = 600 × 100/150 = 400 rpm. Nactual ≈ 400 × 0.96 = 384 rpm. Pout = 0.80 × 600 = 480 W. ω = 2π × 384/60 ≈ 40.21 rad/s. τ = 480/40.21 ≈ 11.9 N·m.

Vocabulary & glossary

Vocabulary and key concepts

Select any term to expand its definition. Each entry opens independently.

Driver pulley

The pulley attached to the input shaft, supplying motion to the belt.

Driven pulley

The pulley receiving motion from the belt and turning the output shaft.

Belt drive

A mechanical transmission using a flexible belt and pulleys to transfer rotation.

Effective diameter (D)

The operating or pitch diameter relevant to the belt speed calculation.

Revolutions per minute (N)

The number of complete shaft rotations in one minute, abbreviated rpm.

Reduction ratio (R)

Driven effective diameter divided by driver effective diameter in this lesson.

Open belt

A belt arrangement with uncrossed spans that preserves rotation direction for parallel shafts.

Crossed belt

A belt arrangement with crossing spans that reverses output direction in the ideal model.

Tight and slack spans

Belt sections carrying higher and lower tension during torque transmission.

V-belt

A friction belt that operates in a grooved pulley.

Synchronous belt

A toothed belt engaging matching pulley teeth to maintain positive indexing.

Slip fraction (s)

Here, the specified fractional decrease of actual output rpm from ideal predicted rpm.

Efficiency (η)

Useful output mechanical power divided by input mechanical power.

Angular speed (ω)

Rotational rate in radians per second, related to rpm by ω = 2πN/60.

Torque (τ)

A turning effect on a shaft, measured in newton-meters.

Power (P)

Rate of energy transfer, measured in watts.

Belt tension

Pulling force along a belt; the tension difference between spans transmits torque.

Pitch circle

An imaginary reference circle used to describe effective pulley motion.

Engineering synthesis

Lesson summary

A belt drive transfers motion between pulleys through a flexible belt. Under ideal no-slip conditions, output speed equals input speed multiplied by driver effective diameter divided by driven effective diameter. Larger followers reduce speed; smaller followers increase it. An open belt normally preserves rotation direction, while a crossed belt reverses it for parallel shafts.

Slip is a speed deviation from the ideal ratio, while efficiency measures useful output power relative to input power. They are not interchangeable. Use the specified slip model to estimate actual rpm, the efficiency equation to find useful power, and P = τω to determine output torque. Real design also requires attention to belt tension, wear, alignment, guarding, and manufacturer limits.

The engineering task is to meet speed, torque, direction, and power requirements simultaneously. Check units and assumptions before calculation, use physical intuition to catch inverted ratios, and record laboratory trials to support your conclusions.
