Why ideal Bernoulli is not enough

B3.3 used Bernoulli’s equation to track the exchange among pressure, velocity, and elevation in an ideal fluid. Real pipes behave differently. Water rubbing against pipe walls and moving through elbows, valves, and transitions loses useful mechanical energy. Engineers call this effect head loss, and the related reduction in pressure is often called pressure loss. A pump may have to replace that energy to maintain the desired flow. Understanding these losses is essential for designing plumbing, irrigation, cooling, and water distribution systems.

Viscosity and friction at a pipe wall

Viscosity describes a fluid’s resistance to shearing motion. In a real pipe, fluid touching a stationary wall is approximately motionless relative to that wall under the usual no-slip condition. Layers farther from the wall move at different speeds. The resulting shear transfers momentum and dissipates mechanical energy. This is why even a perfectly straight, horizontal pipe can have lower downstream pressure than upstream pressure while its average velocity remains nearly constant.

Head: an energy measure in meters

A convenient way to express mechanical energy is head, energy per unit weight of fluid. In B3.3 the ideal energy equation was P/(ρg) + v²/(2g) + z = constant. Its three terms are pressure head, velocity head, and elevation head, all in meters. The extended form for two sections with no pump or turbine between them is P₁/(ρg) + v₁²/(2g) + z₁ = P₂/(ρg) + v₂²/(2g) + z₂ + hL. The new positive term hL represents mechanical energy lost per unit weight.

Pressure loss and units

For constant-density water, pressure drop across a same-diameter, same-elevation segment is ΔP = ρghL. With ρ = 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s², each meter of head corresponds to 9,810 Pa, or 9.81 kPa. Thus 2.5 m of head loss corresponds to 24.525 kPa. Always convert kilopascals to pascals when substituting into SI equations.

Major losses in straight pipe

The Darcy–Weisbach equation estimates straight-pipe friction loss: hf = f(L/D)[v²/(2g)]. Here hf is the major head loss in meters, f is the dimensionless Darcy friction factor, L is pipe length in meters, D is internal diameter in meters, and v is average flow speed in meters per second. The ratio L/D is dimensionless, and v²/(2g) has units of meters. These unit checks help students detect common mistakes.

Length and the role of diameter

If f, D, and v stay fixed, Darcy–Weisbach predicts hf proportional to pipe length L. Doubling the length doubles friction head loss. If the diameter changes while the volumetric flow Q is held fixed, the effect is much larger. Continuity gives v = 4Q/(πD²). A smaller diameter both increases the L/D factor and increases velocity, so the squared-velocity term rises sharply.

Why velocity matters so much

At fixed f, L, and D, the friction equation contains v². If average velocity doubles, the predicted head loss increases by a factor of four. For example, increasing speed from 1 m/s to 2 m/s multiplies the velocity-head term v²/(2g) from approximately 0.051 m to 0.204 m. The result can be a much larger pressure demand from a pump even though the volume flow merely doubles.

This quadratic comparison is not a universal law for every fluid or regime. In laminar flow, the friction factor changes inversely with Reynolds number, and pressure loss at fixed pipe geometry can be approximately proportional to velocity rather than velocity squared. We will use supplied, fixed friction factors for the numerical exercises, and every comparison must name that assumption.

Minor losses from fittings

Water also loses energy when it passes through fittings. Elbows change the direction of motion, valves restrict flow, and abrupt entrances or expansions disturb the velocity field. Engineers call these minor losses or local losses and often estimate them with hm = K[v²/(2g)]. The dimensionless loss coefficient K depends on the fitting and its geometry. For multiple fittings evaluated at the same reference velocity, the coefficients may be added.

Total loss and an energy balance

When the modeled losses are independent contributions, the total head loss is hL = hf + Σhm. For a uniform-diameter pipe with several fittings, hL = [f(L/D) + ΣK]v²/(2g). Once hL is known, it can be inserted into the extended Bernoulli equation. This creates a bridge between the ideal energy balance of B3.3 and the pumping calculations in B3.5.

Suppose the total friction and fitting loss is 4 m of water head. For equal elevations and velocities, downstream pressure is approximately 39.24 kPa lower than upstream pressure. If the pipe rises by 5 m as well, the pressure difference includes both the 4 m loss and the 5 m elevation rise, for a total 9 m head decrease. Treating all pressure changes as friction would misrepresent the engineering cause.

Reynolds number and flow regimes

The Reynolds number Re = ρvD/μ compares inertial and viscous effects, where μ is dynamic viscosity. It helps engineers identify laminar flow, transition, and turbulent flow in pipes. Laminar flow has a more orderly velocity pattern, while turbulent flow contains fluctuating motion and enhanced mixing. Both can experience energy loss, but they do not always follow the same friction-factor relationship.

Roughness, pipe materials, and design choices

The internal surface of a pipe may be relatively smooth or rough. Relative roughness compares characteristic roughness height with pipe diameter. In many turbulent-flow situations, roughness influences friction and therefore the pressure required to maintain flow. Older pipes can also accumulate deposits that reduce effective internal diameter. Both effects can increase resistance, but they are not represented by a simple material label alone.

Limits of the educational model

The interactive lab treats water as incompressible at 1,000 kg/m³ and uses g = 9.81 m/s². It applies a user-selected, fixed Darcy friction factor and summed fitting-loss coefficient. The system is assumed to have steady flow in a full circular pipe. The model calculates the pressure drop needed to maintain a selected flow; it does not determine whether an actual pump can deliver that flow.

It also omits detailed entrance profiles, transient pressure surges, cavitation, heat transfer, and uncertainty in loss coefficients. A passing candidate means only that the selected idealized constraints are satisfied. Before physical installation, an engineer would check actual pipe dimensions, fittings, operating range, pump curve, allowable pressure, and safety requirements. Learning to state those boundaries is as important as obtaining the arithmetic answer.

Seven fully worked engineering examples

Use the same engineering method each time: identify assumptions, convert units, calculate velocity head, apply the loss relationship, and interpret the result. Unless stated otherwise, take water density as 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s². Supplied friction factors are held constant for these exercises.

Worked example 1: Straight-pipe friction

A 40 m horizontal pipe has internal diameter 0.040 m. Water speed is 1.5 m/s and the supplied Darcy friction factor is 0.025.

  1. Step 1: List L = 40 m, D = 0.040 m, v = 1.5 m/s, f = 0.025, and g = 9.81 m/s².
  2. Step 2: Find L/D = 40/0.040 = 1,000.
  3. Step 3: Compute velocity head: v²/(2g) = 2.25/19.62 = 0.11468 m.
  4. Step 4: Apply hf = 0.025 × 1,000 × 0.11468 = 2.867 m.
  5. Step 5: Interpret 2.867 m as lost mechanical energy per unit weight, not a loss of water volume.

Worked example 2: Convert head loss to pressure loss

A same-diameter, same-elevation pipe section loses 2.5 m of head while carrying water of density 1,000 kg/m³.

  1. Step 1: Use ΔP = ρghL because velocity and elevation terms cancel.
  2. Step 2: Substitute ΔP = 1,000 × 9.81 × 2.5 = 24,525 Pa.
  3. Step 3: Convert to 24.525 kPa.
  4. Step 4: The downstream pressure is 24.525 kPa lower than upstream pressure, assuming no pump between points.

Worked example 3: Length comparison

Two 0.050 m pipes carry water at 2 m/s with f = 0.020. Compare 20 m and 60 m lengths.

  1. Step 1: Calculate v²/(2g) = 4/19.62 = 0.20387 m.
  2. Step 2: For 20 m: hf = 0.020(20/0.050)(0.20387) = 1.631 m.
  3. Step 3: For 60 m: hf = 0.020(60/0.050)(0.20387) = 4.893 m.
  4. Step 4: The 60 m pipe is three times as long and has three times the loss.
  5. Step 5: The comparison assumes the same diameter, velocity, and friction factor.

Worked example 4: Diameter comparison at fixed flow

Compare 40 mm and 50 mm diameter pipes, each 40 m long, at Q = 2 L/s and f = 0.025.

  1. Step 1: Convert Q = 0.002 m³/s.
  2. Step 2: At D = 0.040 m, v = Q/[πD²/4] ≈ 1.5915 m/s.
  3. Step 3: Calculate hf,40 = 0.025(40/0.040)[1.5915²/(19.62)] ≈ 3.227 m.
  4. Step 4: At D = 0.050 m, v ≈ 1.0186 m/s.
  5. Step 5: Calculate hf,50 = 0.025(40/0.050)[1.0186²/(19.62)] ≈ 1.058 m.
  6. Step 6: The larger pipe cuts the modeled loss substantially; fixed f and fixed Q are assumed.

Worked example 5: Fittings and bends

Water travels at 2 m/s through two elbows with K = 0.9 each and one valve with K = 1.5.

  1. Step 1: Add the fitting coefficients: ΣK = 0.9 + 0.9 + 1.5 = 3.3.
  2. Step 2: Compute v²/(2g) = 4/19.62 = 0.20387 m.
  3. Step 3: Calculate hm = 3.3 × 0.20387 ≈ 0.673 m.
  4. Step 4: The coefficient is dimensionless; the head loss is measured in meters.
  5. Step 5: This assumes all three fittings use the same reference velocity.

Worked example 6: Combine straight pipe and fittings

A 30 m pipe has D = 0.050 m, v = 2 m/s, f = 0.020, and total fitting coefficient ΣK = 3.

  1. Step 1: Compute the straight-pipe factor: fL/D = 0.020 × 30/0.050 = 12.
  2. Step 2: Add fittings: 12 + 3 = 15.
  3. Step 3: Compute velocity head = 4/19.62 = 0.20387 m.
  4. Step 4: Total loss hL = 15 × 0.20387 ≈ 3.058 m.
  5. Step 5: Pressure loss for water at equal elevation and velocity = 1,000 × 9.81 × 3.058 ≈ 30.0 kPa.

Worked example 7: Engineering pipe selection

A 40 m water line must deliver 2 L/s, with f = 0.025 and ΣK = 2. Compare 40 mm and 50 mm internal diameters under a 20 kPa loss limit.

  1. Step 1: For 40 mm, v ≈ 1.5915 m/s and factor fL/D + ΣK = 25 + 2 = 27.
  2. Step 2: Calculate hL,40 ≈ 27 × 1.5915²/19.62 = 3.486 m, so ΔP ≈ 34.20 kPa: FAIL.
  3. Step 3: For 50 mm, v ≈ 1.0186 m/s and factor = 20 + 2 = 22.
  4. Step 4: Calculate hL,50 ≈ 22 × 1.0186²/19.62 = 1.164 m, so ΔP ≈ 11.41 kPa: PASS.
  5. Step 5: Recommend 50 mm on the stated pressure-loss constraint, while noting that material cost and pump performance remain unverified.

Engineering analysis and common misconceptions

Loss of pressure is not loss of water

The same amount of incompressible water can pass each section while the pressure decreases. Mass conservation and mechanical-energy dissipation are separate ideas. A falling pressure reading is not evidence of a leak unless the flow balance or other observations also indicate a leak.

“Minor” does not mean negligible

The term minor loss describes fittings and local transitions rather than straight pipe. A valve with a large K can cause more loss than a short straight segment. Calculate both terms before deciding which dominates. Likewise, a smooth pipe can still have friction losses because viscosity does not disappear.

Constant friction factor is an assumption

Darcy–Weisbach has a velocity-squared term, but f may vary with Reynolds number and relative roughness. Our model holds f fixed to isolate geometry and flow. Do not present a quadratic law as universally exact, especially in laminar flow. The fifth-power diameter comparison likewise depends on fixed Q, L, and f.

Head loss is not elevation loss

A head loss of 2 m means energy per unit weight was dissipated, not that water fell vertically 2 m. An uphill pipe may lose pressure from both elevation gain and friction. Only when velocity and elevation are unchanged can pressure drop be directly attributed to ρghL.

Feasibility requires more than one check

A candidate that meets a pressure-loss limit under a selected flow is feasible only for those modeled constraints. Real systems require a pump curve, actual roughness and fitting data, pressure ratings, cost estimates, and safe operating margins. The interactive tool is a learning model, not a construction specification.

Practice 1

A 25 m pipe has D = 0.050 m, v = 1.2 m/s, and f = 0.024. Calculate friction head loss.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: L/D = 25/0.050 = 500.
  2. Step 2: Velocity head = 1.2²/19.62 ≈ 0.07339 m.
  3. Step 3: hf = 0.024 × 500 × 0.07339 ≈ 0.881 m.
  4. Step 4: State that the model uses a supplied fixed f.

Practice 2

A level, constant-diameter water pipe loses 1.8 m of head. Find pressure loss in kPa.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Use ΔP = ρghL.
  2. Step 2: ΔP = 1,000 × 9.81 × 1.8 = 17,658 Pa.
  3. Step 3: Convert to 17.658 kPa.
  4. Step 4: Pressure decreases downstream; mass flow is conserved.

Practice 3

At unchanged f, D, and v, a pipe length increases from 15 m to 45 m. How does friction head loss change?

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Use hf proportional to L under the stated fixed conditions.
  2. Step 2: Length ratio = 45/15 = 3.
  3. Step 3: Head loss triples.
  4. Step 4: Do not assume the same relationship if actual flow or f changes.

Practice 4

At fixed Q, L, and f, diameter rises from 40 to 80 mm. What is the ideal friction-loss ratio?

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Substitute v = 4Q/(πD²) into Darcy–Weisbach.
  2. Step 2: At fixed Q and f, hf is proportional to D⁻⁵.
  3. Step 3: hf,new/hf,old = (40/80)⁵ = 1/32.
  4. Step 4: The new loss is 3.125% of the old modeled loss.

Practice 5

Three elbows each have K = 0.7, and one valve has K = 1.4. Water speed is 1.5 m/s. Find fitting head loss.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: ΣK = 3 × 0.7 + 1.4 = 3.5.
  2. Step 2: Velocity head = 1.5²/19.62 ≈ 0.11468 m.
  3. Step 3: hm = 3.5 × 0.11468 ≈ 0.401 m.
  4. Step 4: The calculation assumes a common reference velocity.

Practice 6

A 20 m, 40 mm pipe carries water at 1.5 m/s with f = 0.020 and ΣK = 2.5. Find total head and pressure loss.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: fL/D = 0.020 × 20/0.040 = 10.
  2. Step 2: Total coefficient = 10 + 2.5 = 12.5.
  3. Step 3: hL = 12.5 × 2.25/19.62 ≈ 1.433 m.
  4. Step 4: ΔP = ρghL ≈ 14.06 kPa at equal elevations and velocities.

Practice 7

For 2 L/s in a 40 m pipe with f = 0.025 and ΣK = 2, compare 40 and 50 mm under a 15 kPa maximum loss.

Show numbered solution

Lesson synthesis and preparation for B3.5

Real pipes lose mechanical energy through straight-pipe friction and fittings. Darcy–Weisbach estimates straight-pipe loss, hf = f(L/D)v²/(2g), while fittings contribute hm = Kv²/(2g). The total modeled head loss is the sum of these contributions. In a constant-diameter, level pipe without pumps, the pressure drop associated with those losses is ΔP = ρghL.

Pipe diameter is a particularly important design variable at fixed flow because it changes both velocity and the L/D ratio. Under a fixed friction factor, the modeled straight-pipe loss scales with the inverse fifth power of diameter. However, a larger pipe may cost more, and the model does not prove that a pump can provide the selected flow. B3.5 will examine pump hydraulic power and fluid-system applications.
