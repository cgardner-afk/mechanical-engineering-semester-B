Before designing a system, recall four tools from earlier lessons. From B3.1, flow rate is volume per time. From B3.2, continuity gives Q = Av. From B3.3, pressure, elevation, and velocity represent mechanical energy per unit weight. From B3.4, real pipes have friction and fitting losses. Show readiness solutions

(1) 0.0040 m³/s. (2) 50,000 Pa. (3) Raising water increases gravitational potential energy, which the pump must supply. (4) Smaller diameter increases mean velocity at fixed flow, increasing the modeled velocity-squared losses. Engineering design is a system, not a single calculation

A water distribution system connects a water source, a pump, a pipeline, and a delivery point. Each part influences the others. A pipe that is inexpensive to purchase may cause large friction losses, requiring a stronger pump and more electricity over time. A powerful pump might meet the pressure requirement but cost more than the installation budget allows. Engineers therefore evaluate an entire configuration rather than optimizing a single number. In this lesson you will use a virtual hillside water supply system to practice that approach. Your goal is to deliver enough water to a storage tank at a specified pressure while satisfying both capital and operating constraints.

Read the design brief before choosing components

The client needs 4.0 L/s through 150 meters of pipe to a tank 18 meters above the source. The water should arrive with at least 50 kilopascals of gauge pressure, and installation must cost no more than $12,000. The system operates eight hours per day. These are fictional classroom requirements, not municipal design standards. They deliberately create a situation in which different combinations of pipe diameter and pump size behave differently. Your task is to choose from a catalog and explain your choice with numbers, not simply choose the biggest or cheapest component.

Flow velocity links demand to pipe diameter

The continuity equation for steady incompressible flow gives Q = Av, where Q is volumetric flow rate, A is internal cross-sectional area, and v is mean velocity. For a round pipe, A = πD²/4, so v = 4Q/(πD²). At fixed flow, a larger diameter produces a lower velocity. This matters because friction and fitting losses in our model depend on velocity squared. A 4 L/s flow must be entered as 0.004 m³/s, and a 50 mm diameter must be entered as 0.050 m. Forgetting either conversion can produce a result thousands of times too large or too small.

Friction and fittings consume head

Real flow loses mechanical energy through viscous resistance along the pipe and through disturbances at elbows, valves, and entrances. The Darcy–Weisbach equation estimates major loss as hf = f(L/D)(v²/2g). Here f is a specified dimensionless Darcy friction factor, L is length, D is inside diameter, and g is gravitational acceleration. The fitting contribution is hm = ΣK(v²/2g), where ΣK represents the combined minor-loss coefficients. The total head loss is hL = hf + hm. Head loss is measured in meters of fluid, not watts or pascals.

Pump head must cover the entire energy demand

The required pump head combines elevation, desired delivery pressure, and pipe losses. For our fixed source and delivery boundaries, Hreq = Δz + ΔP/(ρg) + hf + hm. The pressure term is the pressure at the destination relative to the source, converted into meters of water. The elevation term is the vertical rise. Both losses are positive additions because they represent energy the pump must supply. A pump capable of 18 meters of head cannot necessarily lift water 18 meters and still provide pressure after overcoming friction.

Pump curves prevent unrealistic selections

A pump performance curve relates the head a pump can provide to its flow rate. Most ordinary centrifugal pumps provide less head at greater flow. In our fictional catalog, each pump follows a simplified quadratic relationship Hp(Q) = H0 − aQ_L², where QL is the flow in liters per second. The constants are supplied for each pump; they are not universal pump properties. To serve the client, a selected pump must provide at least Hreq at the required 4.0 L/s. A pump with a large shutoff head can still fail if its head falls too much at the design flow.

Hydraulic power, input power, and operating cost

The hydraulic power delivered by the pump is Ph = ρgQH_p. This uses the pump head supplied at the chosen operating flow, not merely the minimum head the system needs. The input power is Pin = Ph/η, where η is the assumed overall wire-to-water efficiency expressed as a decimal. The energy used over t hours is E = (Pin/1000)t kilowatt-hours. Multiplying that energy by a specified electricity price gives a modeled operating cost. A pump with lower purchase price can be more expensive to operate over several years.

Installation budget and lifecycle trade-offs

The installation cost includes a fixed base allowance, a length-dependent pipe cost, and the selected pump purchase cost. Our classroom catalog assigns prices for comparison only; these are not vendor quotes. A larger pipe costs more initially but may lower friction and reduce the pump head requirement. A more efficient pump may cost more to purchase but reduce electricity consumption. A smaller pump may save money yet fail the pressure requirement. A good engineer checks feasibility first, then compares the feasible candidates using a clearly stated decision rule.

An engineering recommendation needs evidence

An engineering recommendation identifies a selected configuration, shows why it meets the requirements, compares at least one credible alternative, and explains remaining uncertainty. The strongest recommendations include pipe diameter, pump model, required head, supplied head, input power, installation cost, and five-year modeled cost. They also mention whether the pump has a large excess-head margin that may imply control losses. The virtual laboratory will save your trials and prepare a comparison table so that you can reason from measured or computed evidence rather than memory.

Seven fully worked engineering examples

Each example identifies the known values, chooses the appropriate relationship, shows the substitution, and interprets the result. Use water density 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s².

Worked example 1: Calculate velocity in a selected pipe

A pipeline carries 4.0 L/s of water through a pipe with 80 mm internal diameter. Find the mean velocity.

  1. Step 1: Convert flow: Q = 4.0/1000 = 0.0040 m³/s. Convert diameter: D = 80/1000 = 0.080 m.
  2. Step 2: Find area: A = π(0.080)²/4 = 0.005027 m².
  3. Step 3: Calculate velocity: v = 0.0040/0.005027 = 0.796 m/s.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: the 80 mm pipe carries the required flow at less than 1 m/s. A smaller pipe would increase velocity and likely increase losses.

Worked example 2: Estimate pipe friction

The 80 mm pipe is 150 m long, with f = 0.025 and v = 0.796 m/s. Calculate the major loss.

  1. Step 1: Use hf = f(L/D)v²/(2g).
  2. Step 2: Calculate L/D = 150/0.080 = 1,875 and v²/(2g) = 0.796²/19.62 ≈ 0.0323 m.
  3. Step 3: Find hf = 0.025(1,875)(0.0323) ≈ 1.51 m.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: the pump must provide about 1.51 additional meters of head to overcome straight-pipe friction.

Worked example 3: Add fitting losses

The pipeline has a combined fitting coefficient ΣK = 3.0 and velocity 0.796 m/s. Find total head loss.

  1. Step 1: Use hm = ΣK v²/(2g) = 3.0(0.0323) = 0.0969 m.
  2. Step 2: Combine with friction: hL = 1.51 + 0.0969 ≈ 1.61 m.
  3. Step 3: The fitting contribution is smaller than the friction loss for this example, but it must still be counted.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: the total loss is approximately 1.61 m of water head.

Worked example 4: Find the required pump head

The source-to-delivery elevation rise is 18 m, the required delivery pressure is 50 kPa gauge, and total pipe losses are 1.61 m.

  1. Step 1: Convert pressure to head: ΔP/(ρg) = 50,000/(1,000×9.81) ≈ 5.10 m.
  2. Step 2: Add elevation and losses: Hreq = 18 + 5.10 + 1.61 = 24.71 m.
  3. Step 3: Compare with a pump curve at the required flow, not merely the pump shutoff head.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: a pump offering 23 m at 4 L/s fails even though 23 m exceeds the elevation rise.

Worked example 5: Evaluate a pump curve

A fictional pump has H₀ = 34 m and a = 0.35 m/(L/s)². Determine its head at 4.0 L/s.

  1. Step 1: Use Hp = H₀ − aQ_L².
  2. Step 2: Substitute: Hp = 34 − 0.35(4.0)² = 34 − 5.6 = 28.4 m.
  3. Step 3: Compare to the 24.71 m required head: margin = 28.4 − 24.71 = 3.69 m.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: the pump has enough modeled head at the required flow, but a controller must account for the excess head.

Worked example 6: Calculate power and energy

The selected pump supplies 28.4 m head at 4 L/s and has 72% overall efficiency. It runs 8 hours daily at $0.16/kWh.

  1. Step 1: Hydraulic power: Ph = 1,000×9.81×0.004×28.4 ≈ 1,114 W.
  2. Step 2: Input power: Pin = 1,114/0.72 ≈ 1,548 W = 1.548 kW.
  3. Step 3: Daily energy: E = 1.548×8 ≈ 12.38 kWh. Daily cost: 12.38×$0.16 ≈ $1.98.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: a lower-efficiency pump would consume more energy for the same head and flow.

Worked example 7: Compare design alternatives

Two feasible designs meet all requirements. Design A costs $8,000 to install and uses 1.55 kW. Design B costs $9,000 and uses 1.20 kW. Both operate 8 h/day at $0.16/kWh.

  1. Step 1: Annual hours: 8×365 = 2,920 h.
  2. Step 2: A annual energy cost = 1.55×2,920×0.16 = $724.16. B = 1.20×2,920×0.16 = $560.64.
  3. Step 3: Five-year totals: A = $8,000 + 5($724.16) = $11,620.80. B = $9,000 + 5($560.64) = $11,803.20.
  4. Step 4: Interpretation: A is slightly cheaper over five years under these assumptions, despite higher electricity use. A different time horizon could change the ranking.

Final engineering recommendation

Phase 1 · Establish a baseline

Start with the 80 mm pipe and P-200 Balanced pump. Before changing anything, calculate the velocity and required head by hand. Compare your results with the dashboard. Explain whether the pump has sufficient head at the required flow. Do not treat a large head margin as automatically beneficial; unused head must be controlled.

Phase 2 · Investigate pipe diameter

Hold the pump constant and try 40, 50, 65, 80, and 100 mm pipes. Record which choices fail, how velocity changes, and how installation cost changes. Find one case where increasing pipe diameter makes a previously failing design feasible. Explain why friction can change dramatically even though the flow requirement remains constant.

Phase 3 · Compare pumps and cost

Hold the pipe diameter fixed and compare all four pumps. Note that the pump curve, efficiency, and purchase cost differ. Save at least three candidates, including one failure and two passing configurations if the scenario permits. Compare five-year totals only among feasible candidates. Identify whether the lowest initial cost and lowest modeled lifecycle cost are the same choice.

Phase 4 · Challenge a different community

Switch to the Growing Community or Ridge Settlement scenario. Explain why a configuration that passed the base scenario may now fail. Choose a feasible configuration and justify it with the changed requirements. Compare your new solution with your original design, focusing on the trade-off among flow, pressure, pipe diameter, pump capacity, and cost.

Phase 5 · Submit the recommendation

Write a concise engineering recommendation naming the scenario, pipe, and pump. Cite at least four computed values and compare an alternative that you saved. Explain two limitations of the simplified model, such as the assumed friction factor, fixed efficiency, unmodeled cavitation, or the simplified pump curve. Engineering analysis: what makes a good design?

Start with feasibility. If a design fails the required flow or pressure, do not compare its low price against the successful alternatives as though they provide the same service. If a design exceeds the installation budget, it also fails even when its hydraulic performance is excellent. Once the mandatory constraints are satisfied, compare energy use, five-year cost, and head margin. A very large head margin can indicate unnecessary pumping and control losses, while a very small margin makes the result sensitive to uncertainty in friction and pump performance.

The simulator uses the supplied pump curve at the required flow and explicitly accounts for extra head as a control burden. This prevents the common error of assigning every pump a fixed head independent of flow. However, the model does not size the throttling valve or determine a variable-speed operating point. Treat its power calculation as a controlled fixed-flow comparison under stated assumptions, not as a complete manufacturer-certified pump design.

An authentic recommendation also states limitations. Real projects would need manufacturer curves, variable demand, water quality requirements, pipe ratings, suction-side checks, cavitation protection, surge analysis, controls, and an itemized cost estimate. These checks matter even when the virtual system passes. The purpose of this capstone is to practice the reasoning process and identify what evidence would be needed next.

A 100 m pipe has D = 0.060 m, f = 0.025, and v = 1.061 m/s. Calculate friction head.

Show numbered solution

  2. Step 2: L/D = 100/0.060 ≈ 1,666.7.
  3. Step 3: hf = 0.025×1,666.7×0.0574 ≈ 2.39 m.

For the previous pipe, ΣK = 4.0. Calculate fitting and total head losses.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: hm = 4.0×1.061²/19.62 ≈ 0.229 m.
  2. Step 2: hL = 2.39 + 0.229 ≈ 2.62 m.
  3. Step 3: The total includes both distributed and local losses.

Practice 4

A system rises 12 m, needs 40 kPa delivery pressure, and loses 2.62 m head. Calculate required pump head.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Pressure head = 40,000/9,810 ≈ 4.08 m.
  2. Step 2: Hreq = 12 + 4.08 + 2.62 ≈ 18.70 m.
  3. Step 3: A pump must provide at least 18.70 m at the design flow.

Practice 5

Pump X follows Hp = 27 − 0.5Q_L². Does it meet 18.70 m at 4 L/s?

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: At 4 L/s, Hp = 27 − 0.5(16) = 19 m.
  2. Step 2: Margin = 19 − 18.70 = 0.30 m.
  3. Step 3: The simplified model passes, but the small margin and curve assumptions require careful real-world verification.

  2. Step 2: Pin = 745.56/0.70 ≈ 1,065 W.

Technical vocabulary glossary

Highlighted technical terms in the reading are defined below. Expand the definitions as needed, and use the terms accurately in your final engineering recommendation.

Water distribution system

Connected equipment that moves water from a source to a destination.

Design brief

Written description of a client’s requirements and constraints.

Flow requirement

Minimum volumetric flow a system must deliver.

Delivery pressure

Pressure required at the destination under the stated conditions.

Continuity equation

Mass conservation relationship; for steady incompressible flow, Q = Av.

Internal diameter

Actual diameter of the flow passage inside a pipe.

Darcy–Weisbach equation

Model for distributed pipe friction head loss.

Friction factor

Dimensionless coefficient representing pipe resistance under stated conditions.

Minor loss coefficient

Dimensionless coefficient describing local energy losses at fittings.

Total head loss

Sum of distributed and local losses expressed as head.

Required pump head

Energy per unit weight needed to meet elevation, pressure, and loss demands.

Pressure head

Pressure divided by fluid specific weight, P/(ρg).

Pump performance curve

Relationship between pump head and flow for specified operating conditions.

Shutoff head

Pump head at zero flow in the simplified curve.

Operating point

Flow and head where pump and system characteristics meet.

Hydraulic power

Rate of mechanical energy transfer to the liquid.

Input power

Electrical or mechanical power supplied to a modeled pump system.

Wire-to-water efficiency

Hydraulic output divided by electrical input.

Installation cost

Estimated cost of the selected pipe, pump, and fixed components.

Lifecycle cost

Cost over a stated period including specified initial and operating costs.

Feasible design

Candidate satisfying every mandatory modeled requirement.

Head margin

Available pump head minus required head at the specified flow.

Engineering recommendation

Evidence-based selection and explanation of a preferred design.

Simplified engineering model

Mathematical representation with explicitly limited assumptions.

Lesson synthesis and design reflection

The complete water-distribution design connects continuity, energy, friction, pump performance, and cost. First convert the required flow to SI units and calculate velocity from the selected internal pipe diameter. Then calculate distributed and fitting head losses. Add the elevation rise and the pressure head required at delivery. Compare that demand with the selected pump curve at the design flow. A feasible pump must provide sufficient head at the required flow; a zero-flow head rating alone does not establish this.

Next calculate hydraulic power from the head actually supplied by the selected pump and calculate input power using efficiency. Translate input power into daily energy and five-year modeled operating cost. Compare only the candidates that satisfy the flow, pressure, and budget requirements. A larger pipe may reduce friction enough to permit a smaller pump, while a more efficient pump may justify a higher purchase price. The preferred choice depends on the stated time horizon and constraints.

Finally, communicate uncertainty. The classroom model is steady, single-line, and deliberately simplified. It does not guarantee a safe real-world installation. Your final deliverable should be a short engineering recommendation that names the chosen pipe and pump, cites at least four calculated values, compares an alternative, and states at least two model limitations.
