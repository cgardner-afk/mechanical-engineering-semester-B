Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Defining a thermodynamic system

Thermodynamics studies energy, its transfer, and its transformations. A thermodynamic system is the matter or region chosen for analysis. Everything outside is the surroundings. A boundary separates them. Choosing the boundary determines which energy transfers count as external. For example, with only water inside the system, heating from an electric element crosses the boundary. With both water and element inside, that transfer is internal.

Engineers draw boundaries before writing equations because different choices produce different accounting diagrams. Boundaries may be real, such as a steel tank wall, or imaginary, such as a line across the entrance to a turbine. They may be fixed or move with a piston. A useful diagram shows the system, surroundings, and arrows for heat, work, and mass crossing the boundary.

2. Open, closed, and isolated systems

An open system exchanges mass and energy with its surroundings. A running pump takes in and discharges fluid, so it is open. A closed system contains a fixed amount of matter, but heat or work can cross its boundary. Gas sealed inside a piston-cylinder assembly is a closed system even while the piston moves. An isolated system exchanges neither mass nor energy.

A perfectly isolated system is an idealization. A well-insulated container can approximate one for a limited time, but some heat usually leaks. A sealed bottle that cools is closed, not isolated. The categories refer to transfers across the selected boundary, not whether an object has a lid. Identifying the category helps determine whether a calculation must include mass flow.

3. Control volumes and flowing matter

A control volume is a region in space selected to analyze equipment with flowing matter. Its boundary is the control surface. Air flowing into and out of a compressor carries energy across the control surface. A turbine similarly has inlet and outlet streams, plus a shaft through which energy may leave as work.

A mass flow rate describes mass crossing a boundary per unit time. In a steady-flow device with no accumulation, mass entering per second equals mass leaving per second. Flowing matter carries internal, kinetic, and other forms of energy. The property enthalpy becomes useful in later open-system calculations because it includes internal energy and flow-related energy.

4. Forms of stored energy

A system can store internal energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy. Internal energy concerns microscopic motion and interactions of particles. Kinetic energy concerns the motion of the system as a whole. Gravitational potential energy concerns position relative to a reference height. Total energy can be represented as E = U + KE + PE.

A stationary tank of warm water has internal energy even if it has no macroscopic motion. A moving cart has kinetic energy KE = ½mv². A raised load has gravitational potential energy PE = mgz near Earth's surface. Engineers may neglect changes in kinetic or potential energy if they are small, but should state that assumption rather than silently omitting them.

E = U + KE + PE   ·   KE = ½mv²   ·   PE = mgz

5. Heat transfer

Heat transfer is energy crossing a boundary because of a temperature difference. It is not a material stored inside an object. Conduction occurs through interactions within matter, convection involves a surface and a fluid, and radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves. These modes were studied in Unit B4.

When warm water cools in a room, energy crosses the water's boundary as heat. If the system is expanded to include the room, some of those transfers become internal. Heat tends to flow from higher to lower temperature, but the rate and direction of transfer depend on the selected surfaces and conditions. Energy stored within matter should not be called heat.

6. Work transfer

Work transfer occurs when energy crosses a boundary through organized interactions such as a moving piston, rotating shaft, or electrical supply. Boundary work is associated with movement of a system boundary. Shaft work transfers energy through rotation. Work differs from heat because it is not defined by a temperature difference.

For a gas expanding against a constant external pressure, work may be estimated as W = PΔV under suitable assumptions. More complex processes require more detailed methods. In this lesson, heat into a system is positive and work done by the system is positive. Work done on the system therefore has a negative sign under this convention.

W = PΔV   (constant external pressure)

7. Temperature and equilibrium

Temperature describes thermal state and helps determine the direction of spontaneous heat transfer. Two systems in thermal equilibrium have no net heat transfer due to a temperature difference. The zeroth law of thermodynamics states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other.

Temperature and stored energy are not identical. A large warm tank may contain more internal energy than a small hot object. The total energy depends on material, mass, and state. A thermometer measures temperature by approaching thermal equilibrium with the object. Understanding this distinction prevents the mistaken assumption that the hottest object always stores the most energy.

8. Energy balance and sign conventions

An energy balance tracks energy entering, leaving, and accumulating. The first law of thermodynamics expresses energy conservation. For a closed system using heat into the system as positive and work done by the system as positive, ΔE = Q − W. This is a preliminary introduction; the next lesson develops the first law in greater depth.

Suppose 500 J of heat enters a sealed system and the system performs 120 J of work. The stored energy increases by 380 J. If 200 J of heat leaves while 50 J of work is done on the system, Q = −200 J and W = −50 J, giving ΔE = −150 J. The negative value means stored energy decreases. State the sign convention before substituting numbers.

ΔE = Q − W   (closed system, heat in and work out positive)

9. Engineering assumptions

An adiabatic process has negligible heat transfer across its boundary. That does not necessarily mean constant temperature. A rigid tank does not change volume, while a moving piston changes the volume of its contained gas. Idealizations can make a model easier to analyze, but they must be identified clearly.

Real devices may lose heat through insulation, experience friction, or leak mass. A closed-system model cannot be used unchanged if fluid flows across the boundary. The objective is to choose a defensible model, label each energy arrow, and recognize what additional data would be required for a full engineering prediction.

4. Kinetic energy

Situation: A 12 kg component moves at 5 m/s.

Step 2 — Select the governing relationship. Use KE = ½mv², or ΔKE = ½m(v₂² − v₁²) when comparing two speeds.

Step 3 — Substitute and calculate. Square each speed before multiplying by half the mass.

Step 3 — Substitute and calculate. Multiply mass, gravitational acceleration, and height change, keeping units in joules.

Lesson Summary

Thermodynamics examines energy, its storage, transfer, and transformation. Engineers select a system and draw a boundary before analyzing energy. An open system exchanges mass and energy; a closed system exchanges energy but not mass; an isolated system ideally exchanges neither. Boundaries can be real or imaginary, fixed or moving.

Energy may be stored as internal, kinetic, or potential energy. Heat crosses a boundary because of a temperature difference, work crosses through organized interactions, and flowing matter transports energy into and out of open systems. Under the convention used here, ΔE = Q − W for a closed system. Lesson B5.2 will develop this first-law relationship more fully.

E = U + KE + PE   ·   ΔE = Q − W
