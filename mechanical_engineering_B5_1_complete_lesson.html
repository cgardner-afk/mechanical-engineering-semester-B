Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Fluid mechanics and its applications

Fluid mechanics studies the behavior of liquids and gases, whether they are at rest or moving. Mechanical engineers use fluid principles in brakes, hydraulic presses, water tanks, pipelines, pumps, aircraft, and ventilation equipment. A fluid continuously deforms when a shear force acts on it. Unlike a rigid solid, it does not keep a fixed shape under sustained shear stress. This ability to flow is what makes fluids useful for transporting materials and transmitting pressure.

A liquid generally has a nearly fixed volume and adopts the shape of its container. A gas expands to occupy the available volume and is usually much more compressible. Compressibility describes how much a substance's volume changes with pressure. We will treat liquids as incompressible in our calculations, a useful approximation for many engineering situations. Actual liquid properties change somewhat with temperature and pressure, so accurate designs require measured data.

2. Density and mass

Density is mass per unit volume, represented by ρ = m/V. The SI unit is kg/m³. For introductory problems, fresh water has density about 1000 kg/m³; many oils are less dense, while seawater is somewhat denser. To find the mass of a stored liquid, multiply its density by volume. To find volume, divide mass by density. These rearrangements let engineers estimate shipping loads and tank capacity.

Unit conversion matters. One cubic meter contains 1000 liters. A volume of 500 liters equals 0.50 m³, so it holds about 500 kg of fresh water. That mass has weight because gravity acts on it. Specific weight is weight per unit volume, γ = ρg. Near Earth's surface, g ≈ 9.81 m/s², so water has a specific weight of approximately 9810 N/m³. Mass in kilograms and weight in newtons are not interchangeable.

ρ = m/V   ·   m = ρV

3. Pressure and its units

Pressure is normal force divided by the area over which it acts: P = F/A. Its SI unit is the pascal, equal to one newton per square meter. Engineers often use kilopascals because one kilopascal equals 1000 pascals. If 600 N acts uniformly on 0.020 m², the pressure is 30,000 Pa, or 30 kPa. For the same force, reducing area increases pressure.

In a fluid at rest, pressure acts perpendicular to surfaces, including vertical walls and the bottom of a tank. Pressure and total force are not identical: a larger wall area can experience a larger total force even at the same pressure. A pressure sensor measures pressure at a particular location. Engineers must select a sensor with a suitable pressure range and must know whether its reading is gauge or absolute.

P = F/A

4. Atmospheric, gauge, and absolute pressure

Atmospheric pressure is exerted by the air around us. A standard reference near sea level is 101.325 kPa, although actual values vary with weather and elevation. Gauge pressure is measured relative to local atmospheric pressure. Absolute pressure is measured relative to a perfect vacuum. The relationship is Pabsolute = Patmospheric + Pgauge. A gauge reading of 120 kPa with atmospheric pressure of 100 kPa means 220 kPa absolute.

At the surface of an open tank, gauge pressure is approximately zero, but absolute pressure equals atmospheric pressure. The distinction matters when comparing pressure instruments or specifying equipment. A negative gauge pressure indicates pressure below local atmospheric pressure; it does not mean negative absolute pressure. Label your answers with their pressure reference so that the numbers cannot be misinterpreted.

Pabsolute = Patmospheric + Pgauge

5. Hydrostatic pressure

Hydrostatic pressure describes pressure in a stationary fluid. For a uniform-density liquid open to the atmosphere, gauge pressure at vertical depth h is P = ρgh. The pressure increases linearly with depth and density. If depth doubles, pressure doubles when the other variables stay fixed. If a denser liquid replaces a lighter one at the same depth, pressure increases. These relationships help engineers estimate loads on storage tanks and dams.

Tank width does not appear in the equation because pressure at a point depends on vertical depth, density, and gravity, not on the container's width. However, the total force on a horizontal bottom is pressure times its area. Pressure on a vertical wall varies from top to bottom, so total wall force requires more careful treatment. The model assumes the liquid is stationary, its density is uniform, and gravity is constant.

Pgauge = ρgh

6. Pascal's principle

Pascal's principle states that an applied pressure change in a confined fluid is transmitted throughout the fluid under ideal static conditions. A hydraulic system uses this principle to transmit force between movable pistons. For pistons at the same elevation with negligible losses, F1/A1 = F2/A2. If the output piston has ten times the input area, the ideal output force is ten times the input force.

The ratio of output force to input force is the mechanical advantage. A hydraulic lift can raise a heavy load with a smaller applied force, but it cannot create energy. A larger output piston moves a shorter distance for a given input movement. The volume displaced must balance: A1d1 = A2d2. This is the hydraulic force–distance tradeoff that makes a press useful for heavy lifting.

F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂   ·   A₁d₁ = A₂d₂

7. Work and conservation of energy

The displaced volume is the volume of fluid moved by a piston. In an ideal incompressible hydraulic system, input and output displaced volumes are equal. Work is force multiplied by displacement in the direction of that force. Therefore, ideal input work F1d1 equals ideal output work F2d2. When output force is multiplied by five, output travel is divided by five.

This tradeoff illustrates energy conservation. Real hydraulic systems lose some energy to friction, fluid motion, and leakage. Engineers must also account for cylinder strength, pressure ratings, seals, and operating conditions. A classroom model can predict ideal behavior, but it is not a safety certification for a real lift.

8. Engineering limitations

Fluid systems must operate within structural and performance limits. A tank can meet a calculated pressure limit but still fail at a weak joint. A hydraulic lift may theoretically generate sufficient force but have inadequate piston travel or excessive operating pressure. The engineer must examine all relevant constraints rather than selecting the design with the largest single output.

Viscosity measures a fluid's resistance to shearing flow and becomes important when fluids move through pipes and valves. Our stationary-fluid models do not include viscosity-driven losses, acceleration, air bubbles, or pressure changes caused by height differences between hydraulic pistons. When you evaluate the laboratory designs, state which conclusions follow from the ideal equations and which would require physical testing.

Seven Worked Examples

1. Density of a sample

Situation: A liquid sample has mass 1.84 kg and volume 0.0020 m³.

ρ = 1.84/0.0020 = 920 kg/m³.

Interpretation: The material density is below water's typical density.

2. Mass in a storage tank

Situation: A tank holds 0.75 m³ of water at 1000 kg/m³.

m = ρV = 1000(0.75) = 750 kg.

Interpretation: Mass determines storage load, while weight depends on gravity.

3. Pressure under a support

Situation: A force of 1200 N acts uniformly on 0.030 m².

P = F/A = 1200/0.030 = 40,000 Pa = 40 kPa.

Interpretation: Spreading the force over a larger area lowers pressure.

4. Gauge to absolute pressure

Situation: A gauge reads 180 kPa; local atmosphere is 99 kPa.

Pabsolute = 180 + 99 = 279 kPa absolute.

Interpretation: Pressure reference must be labeled.

5. Water pressure at tank bottom

Situation: A tank contains water 3.0 m deep; ρ = 1000 kg/m³.

Pgauge = 1000(9.81)(3) = 29,430 Pa = 29.43 kPa.

Interpretation: Pressure depends on depth, density, and gravity.

6. Water versus oil

Situation: Compare 1000 kg/m³ water and 850 kg/m³ oil at 2.5 m depth.

Water: 1000(9.81)(2.5) = 24.53 kPa. Oil: 850(9.81)(2.5) = 20.85 kPa.

Interpretation: The denser fluid produces greater pressure at equal depth.

7. Hydraulic force and travel

Situation: Input area 0.008 m², output area 0.080 m², input force 250 N, input travel 0.50 m.

Output force = 250(0.080/0.008) = 2500 N. Output travel = 0.50(0.008/0.080) = 0.050 m.

Interpretation: Force multiplication is balanced by reduced travel.

Independent Practice

Attempt each problem before opening the worked solution.

Problem 1: Find density for 2.7 kg of liquid in 0.003 m³.

Worked solution: ρ = 2.7/0.003 = 900 kg/m³.

Problem 2: Find mass in 1.4 m³ of oil with density 850 kg/m³.

Worked solution: m = 850(1.4) = 1190 kg.

Problem 3: A force of 900 N acts over 0.045 m². Find pressure.

Worked solution: P = 900/0.045 = 20,000 Pa = 20 kPa.

Problem 4: Gauge pressure is 120 kPa and atmosphere is 98 kPa. Find absolute pressure.

Worked solution: Pabsolute = 120 + 98 = 218 kPa.

Problem 5: Find gauge pressure 4 m below the surface of fresh water.

Worked solution: P = 1000(9.81)(4) = 39,240 Pa = 39.24 kPa.

Problem 6: Find pressure 3 m deep in oil of density 870 kg/m³.

Worked solution: P = 870(9.81)(3) = 25,604 Pa ≈ 25.60 kPa.

Problem 7: Hydraulic areas are 0.012 m² and 0.060 m²; input force 300 N, travel 0.40 m. Find output force and travel.

Worked solution: Fout = 300(0.060/0.012) = 1500 N; dout = 0.40(0.012/0.060) = 0.080 m.

Lesson Summary

Fluid mechanics examines liquids and gases at rest and in motion. Density is mass per unit volume, while pressure is force per unit area. Atmospheric pressure is the pressure of surrounding air; gauge pressure measures pressure relative to that atmosphere, and absolute pressure measures pressure relative to a vacuum. Hydrostatic pressure in a stationary liquid increases with density and depth, not directly with tank width.

Pascal's principle explains how pressure changes transmit through confined fluids. Hydraulic machines multiply force by using different piston areas, but the output piston travels a correspondingly shorter distance. The ideal system conserves work. Engineers use these relationships to compare designs, then account for material strength, friction, leakage, and safety in real applications.

ρ = m/V   ·   P = F/A   ·   Pgauge = ρgh
Pabsolute = Patmospheric + Pgauge   ·   F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂
