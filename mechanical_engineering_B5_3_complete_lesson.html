Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Why engineers use heat engines

A heat engine is a device that operates in a cycle and produces net work by receiving heat from a high-temperature source and rejecting some heat to a lower-temperature sink. Engines power cars, ships, electrical generators, and industrial equipment. The central engineering challenge is not merely to receive thermal energy but to convert some of it into useful mechanical output. The engine does not create energy. It changes how energy is transferred and used.

Consider a power station. A fuel, nuclear, or solar thermal source supplies energy to a working fluid. The fluid drives a turbine connected to a generator, and a cooling system removes remaining heat. The electrical output ultimately depends on the mechanical work delivered by the turbine and generator losses. A heat engine is therefore one part of a larger energy-conversion system, and its performance must be evaluated using clearly defined boundaries.

2. Hot and cold reservoirs

A heat engine interacts with a hot reservoir at a higher temperature and a cold reservoir at a lower temperature. A thermal reservoir is an idealized body large enough to exchange heat without a significant temperature change. Heat input Qin is energy supplied from the hot side; heat rejection Qout is energy delivered to the cold side. In the equations in this lesson, both Qin and Qout represent positive magnitudes.

The cold reservoir might be outside air, cooling water, or another lower-temperature environment. The hot reservoir might be a combustion chamber or a boiler. Rejected heat is not necessarily evidence of a broken engine: it is part of how a cyclic heat engine operates. The First Law tracks the quantities of energy, while the Second Law, studied in B5.4, explains why complete conversion of heat from a single reservoir into work is not possible for a cyclic engine.

3. The working fluid and thermodynamic cycle

The working fluid is the substance undergoing the engine's processes. Steam, air, and combustion gases are common examples. A thermodynamic cycle is a sequence of processes that returns the working substance to its original thermodynamic state. The substance may be compressed, heated, expanded, and cooled as it travels through the cycle. In an idealized closed cycle, the fluid returns to its initial state after each complete repetition.

Because internal energy and other stored-energy properties depend on state, their net changes over a complete cycle are zero. The engine can still deliver net work during the cycle because heat enters and leaves at different points. This distinction is essential: zero net change in stored energy does not mean zero energy transfer. The engine can receive a large amount of heat, deliver some as work, and reject the remainder while its working fluid returns to its starting state.

4. Applying the First Law to a complete cycle

The First Law of Thermodynamics states that energy is conserved. For a closed system, ΔE = Q − W when heat entering and work performed by the system are positive. Over a complete cycle, ΔE = 0. Therefore, net work equals net heat transfer. For a heat engine receiving Qin and rejecting Qout, the magnitude form is Wnet = Qin − Qout.

Suppose an engine receives 1000 kJ and rejects 650 kJ during one cycle. It delivers 350 kJ of net work. All 1000 kJ are accounted for: 350 kJ leave as work and 650 kJ leave as heat. If a student adds the two outputs and obtains a number different from the input, either the values, system boundary, or assumptions need to be checked. A clear energy-flow diagram makes this conservation relationship visible.

Wnet = Qin − Qout   (positive heat magnitudes)

5. Thermal efficiency

Thermal efficiency, symbol ηth, measures the fraction of supplied heat converted into net work. It is defined by ηth = Wnet/Qin. Using the cyclic energy balance, it can also be written ηth = 1 − Qout/Qin. Efficiency is dimensionless because work and heat are measured in the same energy units. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.

For example, an engine producing 400 kJ of net work from 1000 kJ of supplied heat has an efficiency of 0.40, or 40%. The remaining 600 kJ are rejected as heat in this simplified engine balance. An efficiency of 40% does not mean the other 60% of energy disappears. Energy is conserved, but only part becomes the useful output we want. Engineers distinguish the fraction of input energy converted into work from the total amount of work produced.

ηth = Wnet/Qin = 1 − Qout/Qin

6. Efficiency versus work and power

An engine can produce more net work without becoming more efficient. If its heat input doubles and its efficiency stays constant, work output doubles but the fraction converted remains unchanged. Power is the rate of energy transfer or work production, measured in watts or kilowatts. A machine delivering 300 kJ of work every 10 seconds produces an average power of 30 kW.

The distinction matters when comparing equipment. A small efficient engine may deliver less total power than a larger, less efficient engine. A designer may prefer the larger engine if the task demands high power, but fuel costs and rejected heat may make it less desirable. Always compare devices under the same assumptions and clearly state whether you are evaluating efficiency, work per cycle, or work per unit time.

Power = work / elapsed time   ·   1 kW = 1 kJ/s

7. Internal combustion engines

An internal combustion engine releases fuel energy through combustion inside the engine's working chambers. Many gasoline engines follow a four-stroke cycle: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. During intake, a fuel-air mixture or air enters the cylinder. Compression raises pressure and temperature. Combustion and expansion drive the piston during the power stroke. Exhaust removes burned gases.

A diesel engine also uses compression and combustion but typically ignites injected fuel using high air temperature produced by compression rather than a spark. Real engines do not behave exactly like simple ideal closed cycles: gases enter and leave, combustion occurs, friction consumes power, and exhaust carries energy away. Engineers use idealized cycles to understand the main processes, then refine predictions with measurements and more complete models.

8. External combustion and steam power

An external combustion engine receives heat from a source outside the main working-fluid space. A steam power plant typically heats water in a boiler or steam generator, expands steam through a turbine, condenses the steam, and pumps the water back toward the boiler. This repeating sequence is often modeled using the Rankine cycle.

The turbine converts part of the working fluid's energy into shaft work. The condenser rejects heat to a cooling system, while the pump requires work input. The plant's net work equals turbine work output minus the work required by components such as pumps. Steam plants can use different heat sources, including combustion, nuclear energy, or concentrated solar thermal energy. Their common feature is a circulating working fluid and heat transfer from an external source.

9. Where the energy goes

Real engines have multiple mechanisms that reduce useful output. Heat leaves in exhaust gases and cooling systems. Friction, pumps, fans, and accessory equipment consume some of the produced work. Mechanical efficiency describes how much of a mechanical input or internally produced work reaches a specified useful output. It is not identical to thermal efficiency, which compares net work with supplied heat.

A Sankey diagram represents energy streams with widths proportional to their magnitudes. A 1000 kJ input might be represented by 350 kJ of net work and 650 kJ of rejected heat. In a more detailed engine diagram, the heat rejection and internal losses can be separated into several paths. When evaluating such a diagram, verify that the input and output streams balance under the chosen system boundary and time interval.

10. Engineering tradeoffs and physical limits

Designers evaluate fuel consumption, useful power, heat rejection, cost, reliability, maintenance, and environmental impacts. A higher-efficiency engine may cost more or require a more complex cooling system. A compact engine may be preferred when weight and space matter more than maximum efficiency. The best design depends on the application and the constraints rather than on a single performance number.

The interactive simulator treats efficiency as an assigned performance value for comparing hypothetical designs. It does not claim that every selected efficiency is physically attainable. In particular, reservoir temperatures impose limits that will be explored in Lesson B5.4. A valid design must also respect heat rejection capacity and energy supply limits. Energy conservation alone is necessary, but not sufficient, to demonstrate that a proposed engine can be built.

Seven Worked Examples

1. Net work from heat transfers

Engineering situation: A cyclic engine receives 1200 kJ of heat and rejects 780 kJ.

Wnet = Qin − Qout = 1200 − 780 = 420 kJ.

Interpretation: The working fluid returns to its initial state, but the engine delivers 420 kJ of net work.

2. Thermal efficiency from heat and work

Engineering situation: An engine receives 900 kJ and delivers 315 kJ of net work.

ηth = 315/900 = 0.350 = 35.0%.

Interpretation: 35% of supplied heat becomes net work.

3. Rejected heat from efficiency

Engineering situation: An engine receives 1500 kJ at 42% thermal efficiency.

Wnet = 0.42(1500) = 630 kJ. Qout = 1500 − 630 = 870 kJ.

Interpretation: The rejected heat accounts for the remaining 58% of supplied energy.

4. Required heat input

Engineering situation: An engine must deliver 480 kJ of work at 30% efficiency.

Qin = Wnet/η = 480/0.30 = 1600 kJ.

Interpretation: The required heat input is larger than useful work output.

5. Comparing two engines

Engineering situation: Engine A receives 1000 kJ and produces 380 kJ; Engine B receives 1400 kJ and produces 490 kJ.

ηA = 380/1000 = 38%. ηB = 490/1400 = 35%.

Interpretation: B produces more work, but A converts a larger fraction of heat into work.

6. Power over operating time

Engineering situation: An engine receives heat at 500 kW and operates at 32% thermal efficiency for 60 s.

Net power = 0.32(500) = 160 kW. Work = 160(60) = 9600 kJ. Rejected heat rate = 340 kW; rejected heat = 20,400 kJ.

Interpretation: Rates must be multiplied by time to obtain energy.

7. Engine design constraint check

Engineering situation: A proposed engine receives 1200 kJ and delivers 480 kJ of work. Requirements: at least 35% efficiency, at least 450 kJ work, and no more than 750 kJ rejected heat.

η = 480/1200 = 40%; Qout = 1200 − 480 = 720 kJ.

Interpretation: All three stated numerical constraints are met in the simplified model.

Independent Practice

Solve each problem independently before expanding its worked solution.

Problem 1: A heat engine receives 1800 kJ and rejects 1170 kJ. Find net work.

Worked solution: W = 1800 − 1170 = 630 kJ.

Problem 2: An engine produces 240 kJ of net work from 800 kJ of heat. Find efficiency.

Worked solution: η = 240/800 = 0.30 = 30%.

Problem 3: An engine operates at 25% efficiency with 2000 kJ heat input. Find work and rejected heat.

Worked solution: W = 0.25(2000) = 500 kJ; Qout = 2000 − 500 = 1500 kJ.

Problem 4: An engine must deliver 750 kJ of work at 50% efficiency. Find required heat input.

Worked solution: Qin = 750/0.50 = 1500 kJ.

Problem 5: Engine A produces 300 kJ from 1000 kJ; Engine B produces 400 kJ from 1600 kJ. Which is more efficient?

Worked solution: A = 30%; B = 25%. A is more efficient despite lower total work.

Problem 6: A 200 kW heat supply feeds an engine at 35% efficiency for 30 seconds. Find work produced.

Worked solution: Work rate = 0.35(200) = 70 kW; work = 70(30) = 2100 kJ.

Problem 7: A design receives 1400 kJ and operates at 40% efficiency. It must produce at least 500 kJ work and reject no more than 800 kJ. Does it qualify?

Worked solution: W = 0.40(1400) = 560 kJ; Qout = 1400 − 560 = 840 kJ. Work passes; rejected heat fails.

Lesson Summary

A heat engine operates in a cycle, receives heat from a high-temperature reservoir, produces net work, and rejects heat to a lower-temperature reservoir. Because the working fluid returns to its initial state after a complete cycle, its net stored-energy change is zero. The First Law therefore gives Wnet = Qin − Qout when heat inputs and rejections are expressed as positive magnitudes.

Thermal efficiency is the fraction of heat input converted into net work: ηth = Wnet/Qin. Higher work output does not necessarily imply higher efficiency, and rejected heat remains part of the energy balance. Internal combustion engines and steam power systems use different mechanisms, but both can be studied through energy-flow accounting. The Second Law, introduced in B5.4, will explain the physical limits on converting heat into work.

Wnet = Qin − Qout
ηth = Wnet/Qin = 1 − Qout/Qin
Work = power × elapsed time   ·   1 kW = 1 kJ/s
