Understanding pressure underwater

A diver feels more pressure at the bottom of a swimming pool than near the surface, even when the water is not moving. This is a consequence of the weight of the water above the diver. The study of fluids at rest is hydrostatics. A stationary fluid is modeled as having no bulk flow or acceleration relative to its container. Engineers apply this idea to tanks, reservoirs, dams, pressure sensors, and underwater equipment.

In B2.2 you calculated density as mass per volume and pressure as force per area. Hydrostatics connects those ideas using gravity. You will calculate the pressure increase below a fluid surface, distinguish two pressure references, compare fluids, and make a simplified tank design decision. A sound solution names the depth reference, converts units, and explains whether its numerical result is physically reasonable.

Deriving the fluid-column equation

Imagine a vertical column of fluid of height h and cross-sectional area A. Its volume is Ah. For uniform density ρ, its mass is ρAh, and its weight is ρAhg. Dividing this weight by the column area gives the pressure difference between bottom and top: ΔP = ρgh. This is the hydrostatic pressure difference.

The symbol g represents gravitational acceleration, approximately 9.81 m/s² near Earth's surface. If density is in kg/m³ and height is in meters, the result is in pascals. The derivation assumes the liquid is stationary, density is approximately constant, and gravity is uniform. The equation does not describe friction losses in a flowing pipe or transient pressure from a rapidly moving piston.

Vertical depth rather than distance traveled

The height h is the vertical depth beneath a reference surface. It is not the length of a sloping tube, the horizontal distance from a wall, or the distance along a curved pipe. A sensor three meters vertically below the water surface has h = 3 m regardless of tank width. For water at 1000 kg/m³, the pressure increase is 1000 × 9.81 × 3 = 29,430 Pa.

Pressure in a connected, stationary, uniform-density liquid is equal at points of the same elevation when their surface pressure reference is the same. This principle explains the hydrostatic paradox: differently shaped containers filled to the same height with the same liquid can have identical bottom pressure despite containing different volumes and weights of liquid. Bottom force may still differ because bottom areas differ.

Gauge and absolute pressure

A gauge pressure is measured relative to the surrounding atmosphere. At the surface of an open tank, gauge pressure is zero, so at depth h the gauge pressure is ρgh. An absolute pressure is measured relative to a vacuum. In an open tank, absolute pressure at depth is atmospheric pressure plus ρgh.

For example, at a location where atmospheric pressure is 101 kPa, water at 2 m depth produces a 19.62 kPa gauge increase and an absolute pressure of 120.62 kPa. A gauge reading of zero does not mean there is no physical pressure. Always identify the pressure reference before adding or subtracting a surface pressure; otherwise you may count atmospheric pressure twice.

Pressure at a sealed liquid surface

Not every tank is open. If pressure at the liquid surface is P₀, pressure at depth h is P = P₀ + ρgh. This works with gauge values or absolute values, provided both use the same reference. For a sealed water tank with surface pressure 150 kPa absolute and a sensor 4 m below the surface, pressure is 150 + 39.24 = 189.24 kPa absolute.

If the given surface pressure were instead 150 kPa gauge, the result would be 189.24 kPa gauge. The arithmetic is identical, but the interpretation changes. You must not automatically add 101 kPa unless the problem explicitly asks for conversion between gauge and absolute references. Read instrument labels carefully because a sensor can be designed to measure absolute, gauge, or differential pressure.

Fluid density and the pressure gradient

At a fixed depth, denser liquids produce greater hydrostatic pressure differences. The pressure gradient is ρg pascals per meter for a stationary liquid of uniform density. For water at 1000 kg/m³, the gradient is 9.81 kPa/m. For oil at 850 kg/m³, it is approximately 8.34 kPa/m.

An engineer estimating tank level from a bottom pressure sensor needs to know the fluid density. If temperature or composition changes density, the same measured pressure can represent a different liquid height. The laboratory uses illustrative fixed densities so you can isolate this mathematical relationship. Real designs may require measured property data and temperature compensation.

Pressure differences between sensors

For two points in the same stationary liquid, P₂ − P₁ = ρg(h₂ − h₁) when depth increases downward. A sensor at 4 m in water reads 29.43 kPa more than a sensor at 1 m, because their vertical separation is 3 m. The surface pressure cancels when subtracting the two readings.

This calculation is especially useful when only the difference between readings matters. If a problem says deeper minus shallower, the result should be positive under the stated assumptions. A negative result often indicates that the subtraction order or depth sign was reversed. When comparing points in different liquids, be careful: a single constant density may not apply across both regions.

Solving for depth from pressure

Rearrange ΔP = ρgh to obtain h = ΔP/(ρg). If a gauge sensor in an open water tank reads 24,525 Pa, then h = 24,525/(1000 × 9.81) = 2.50 m. This is a basic model for hydrostatic level measurement.

The calculation requires a known density and a correctly referenced pressure difference. A sealed tank may have nonzero surface gauge pressure; in that case subtract the surface pressure before solving for depth. Real instruments also have calibration uncertainty. Pressure alone cannot uniquely determine liquid depth when both density and surface pressure are unknown.

Solving for density from pressure

If the depth and pressure difference are known, rearrange to ρ = ΔP/(gh). A 2 m column producing 17,658 Pa gives density 17,658/(9.81 × 2) = 900 kg/m³. The unit result is kg/m³ because pressure divided by gravitational acceleration and length simplifies to mass per volume.

This inverse calculation can help check whether a fluid matches an expected density. It is valid only when the pressure difference is due to hydrostatic weight under the model assumptions. Flowing or accelerating fluids can exhibit additional pressure effects. A single calculated density is not proof of chemical identity or purity.

Pressure at a point versus force on a wall

The pressure at the bottom of a tank is not the average pressure over an entire vertical wall. In a stationary uniform liquid, gauge pressure rises linearly from zero at the open surface to ρgH at depth H. This is a triangular pressure distribution.

For a rectangular vertical wall extending from the surface to depth H, the average gauge pressure is half the bottom gauge pressure. The resultant horizontal hydrostatic force magnitude is F = (ρgH/2)A, where A is the wetted area of the wall. This simple expression assumes constant density and a rectangular vertical wall. More advanced analysis determines the force's location of application and evaluates stresses in the structure.

Why tank shape does not change bottom pressure

Imagine three containers: a straight-sided tank, one wider at the top, and one narrower at the top. Fill all three with the same liquid to the same vertical height and expose the surfaces to the same pressure. Their bottom pressure is identical, because it depends on density, gravity, height, and surface pressure.

The containers may hold different fluid masses. Their walls can exert forces with vertical components, so bottom force need not equal the total liquid weight in a sloped-sided container. Also, if bottom areas differ, identical bottom pressure can produce different bottom forces. The lab changes a schematic tank shape without changing the height-to-pressure equation; it does not calculate wall loads or structural stresses.

Design constraints and model limits

A pressure rating is an allowable pressure under specified conditions. The virtual tank challenge checks whether calculated bottom gauge pressure is no more than 50 kPa. This is a design constraint, not a statement that a real tank is safe.

Real tanks require evaluation of material strength, wall thickness, joints, corrosion, temperature, transient loads, and safety factors. The simulation assumes uniform density, constant gravity, and stationary liquid. A gas column, a strongly varying density, or a moving tank may require a different model. State these limitations when reporting results rather than claiming that a virtual pressure pass certifies real equipment.

Seven worked examples

Each example shows the calculation in numbered steps. Identify the pressure reference before substituting numbers, and explain the result using units and physical meaning.

Worked example 1 · Water pressure at depth

Situation: A sensor is 3 m below open water, ρ = 1000 kg/m³.

  1. Write ΔP = ρgh.
  2. Substitute 1000 × 9.81 × 3.
  3. Calculate 29,430 Pa = 29.43 kPa gauge.

Interpretation: Gauge pressure increases with depth.

Worked example 2 · Compare oil and water

Situation: Compare 4 m depth in water (1000 kg/m³) and oil (850 kg/m³).

  1. Water: 1000 × 9.81 × 4 = 39,240 Pa.
  2. Oil: 850 × 9.81 × 4 = 33,354 Pa.
  3. Difference = 5,886 Pa.

Interpretation: Oil has less pressure increase because it is less dense.

Worked example 3 · Absolute pressure

Situation: Atmosphere is 101 kPa; water depth is 2 m.

  1. Gauge increase = 1000 × 9.81 × 2 = 19,620 Pa.
  2. Convert 19,620 Pa to 19.62 kPa.
  3. Add 101 + 19.62 = 120.62 kPa absolute.

Interpretation: Do not count atmospheric pressure twice.

Worked example 4 · Two pressure sensors

Situation: Sensors are at 1 m and 4 m in water.

  1. Vertical separation = 4 − 1 = 3 m.
  2. Use ΔP = ρgΔh.
  3. Calculate 29.43 kPa.

Interpretation: The common surface pressure cancels.

Worked example 5 · Find depth

Situation: Water gauge pressure is 24.525 kPa.

  1. Convert to 24,525 Pa.
  2. Rearrange h = ΔP/(ρg).
  3. 24,525/(1000 × 9.81) = 2.50 m.

Interpretation: The result assumes known water density.

Worked example 6 · Find density

Situation: A 2 m column creates 17,658 Pa difference.

  1. Rearrange ρ = ΔP/(gh).
  2. Substitute 17,658/(9.81 × 2).
  3. Calculate 900 kg/m³.

Interpretation: Check: 900 × 9.81 × 2 = 17,658 Pa.

Worked example 7 · Wall force and pressure constraint

Situation: A water tank has a 2 m high by 3 m wide vertical wall; bottom limit is 25 kPa.

  1. Bottom gauge pressure = 1000 × 9.81 × 2 = 19.62 kPa.
  2. 19.62 ≤ 25 kPa, so modeled pressure passes.
  3. Area = 2 × 3 = 6 m².
  4. Average gauge pressure = 19.62/2 = 9.81 kPa.
  5. Force = 9810 × 6 = 58,860 N.

Interpretation: Pressure pass is not structural certification.

Analysis and misconceptions

Depth is vertical

The length of a sloping pipe is not the depth. Use vertical separation when applying ρgh. Equal elevation in connected stationary liquid means equal pressure under the model assumptions.

Tank shape does not set point pressure

Different tank shapes can hold different masses of water but produce the same bottom pressure when height, density, and surface pressure match. Bottom force may differ because bottom area differs.

Gauge and absolute are not interchangeable

An open tank has zero gauge pressure at the surface but nonzero absolute pressure. When given surface absolute pressure, add the hydrostatic increase once. Never add atmospheric pressure twice.

Wall force is not bottom pressure times wall area

Pressure increases with depth, so a vertical wall has a nonuniform distribution. For a rectangular wall from the surface to H, use half bottom gauge pressure as the average before multiplying by area.

Virtual pass is not structural approval

The tank challenge checks one pressure inequality. Real structures also require strength, fatigue, corrosion, joint, and safety-factor analysis.

Practice 1

Find gauge pressure 5.0 m below open water.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s², h = 5 m.
  2. Select equation. ΔP = ρgh.
  3. Substitute. 1000 × 9.81 × 5.
  4. Calculate. 49,050 Pa = 49.05 kPa gauge.
  5. Interpret. Greater depth means greater gauge pressure.

Practice 2

Compare water (1000 kg/m³) and oil (850 kg/m³) at 3 m. Find pressure difference.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify constants. g = 9.81 and h = 3 m for both.
  2. Water pressure. 1000 × 9.81 × 3 = 29,430 Pa.
  3. Oil pressure. 850 × 9.81 × 3 = 25,015.5 Pa.
  4. Subtract. 29,430 − 25,015.5 = 4414.5 Pa.
  5. Interpret. Water creates 4.4145 kPa more pressure.

Practice 3

Atmospheric pressure is 100 kPa. Find absolute pressure 4 m underwater.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. P₀ = 100 kPa absolute; ρ = 1000; h = 4 m.
  2. Hydrostatic increase. 1000 × 9.81 × 4 = 39,240 Pa.
  3. Convert. 39,240 Pa = 39.24 kPa.
  4. Add. 100 + 39.24 = 139.24 kPa absolute.
  5. Check. Atmospheric pressure is added only once.

Practice 4

Oil sensors at 0.5 m and 3.5 m; density 850 kg/m³. Find deeper-minus-shallower pressure.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify depths. 0.5 m and 3.5 m.
  2. Calculate difference. Δh = 3.0 m.
  3. Select formula. ΔP = ρgΔh.
  4. Substitute. 850 × 9.81 × 3.
  5. Calculate. 25,015.5 Pa = 25.02 kPa.

Practice 5

A water sensor reads 58.86 kPa gauge. Estimate depth.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. ΔP = 58.86 kPa, ρ = 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Convert. 58,860 Pa.
  3. Rearrange. h = ΔP/(ρg).
  4. Calculate. 58,860/9810 = 6.0 m.
  5. Check. 9810 Pa/m × 6 m = 58,860 Pa.

Practice 6

A 2.5 m column creates 22,072.5 Pa. Find density.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. ΔP = 22,072.5 Pa; h = 2.5 m.
  2. Rearrange. ρ = ΔP/(gh).
  3. Substitute. 22,072.5/(9.81 × 2.5).
  4. Calculate. 900 kg/m³.
  5. Check. Units simplify to kg/m³.

Practice 7

A 3 m deep water tank has a 3 m by 2 m vertical wall. Bottom pressure limit is 35 kPa. Find pressure, pass/fail, and wall force.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Identify givens. ρ = 1000 kg/m³, H = 3 m, width = 2 m.
  2. Bottom pressure. 1000 × 9.81 × 3 = 29.43 kPa.
  3. Check constraint. 29.43 ≤ 35 kPa: modeled check passes.
  4. Find wall area. A = 3 × 2 = 6 m².
  5. Average pressure. 29,430/2 = 14,715 Pa.
  6. Resultant force. 14,715 × 6 = 88,290 N.
  7. Interpret. Wall structural strength still needs checking.

Lesson summary

Hydrostatic pressure increases with depth because the fluid above a point has weight. For a stationary uniform-density liquid, ΔP = ρgh, where h is vertical depth. The equation predicts equal pressure at equal depth regardless of tank shape, provided surface pressure and fluid density are the same.

Absolute pressure includes the reference surface absolute pressure; gauge pressure is relative to atmosphere. Pressure differences between two depths depend on their vertical separation. Rearranging the formula allows depth or density to be inferred from suitable measurements.

Bottom pressure is not the same as the average pressure on a vertical wall. The next lesson will use fluid pressure to explain Pascal's principle and hydraulic force transmission.
