From pressure losses to pump work

B3.4 showed that moving water loses useful mechanical energy as it travels through a real pipe. A pump is a machine that adds mechanical energy to a flowing liquid, often so that water can reach a higher elevation, maintain a useful delivery pressure, or overcome resistance in a distribution network. Pumps do not create water or cancel friction. They supply energy that compensates for the demands of a particular flow path. The key engineering question is not simply whether a pump turns on; it is whether it can supply the required flow and head at an acceptable input power.

Flow rate and pressure rise are different quantities

Volumetric flow rate Q measures volume per time, usually cubic meters per second or liters per second. Pressure rise ΔP measures how much the pump increases pressure, expressed in pascals or kilopascals. A system can demand a high pressure rise at a small flow, or a relatively low pressure rise at a large flow. Neither number by itself determines hydraulic power. The two quantities must be multiplied, using compatible SI units, to describe the rate at which pressure energy is transferred to the fluid.

Hydraulic power from pressure and flow

The simplest hydraulic power equation is Pₕ = ΔP Q, where ΔP is the pressure rise across the pump and Q is the volume flow rate. The units prove the result: Pa × m³/s = (N/m²)(m³/s) = N·m/s = W. This pressure-based form is especially convenient for a pump whose inlet and outlet have essentially the same average velocity and elevation. It can also be interpreted as the rate of increase of pressure energy alone, rather than automatically the entire mechanical energy added in every configuration.

Pump head: energy per unit weight

Pump head Hₚ expresses mechanical energy added by the pump per unit weight of fluid, measured in meters. For a liquid of density ρ, Pₕ = ρgQHₚ. If the pump's mechanical energy addition is represented entirely by pressure rise across equal-diameter, equal-elevation connections, then Hₚ = ΔP/(ρg). For water at 1,000 kg/m³, each meter of head corresponds to approximately 9.81 kPa of pressure. This relationship allows engineers to connect pressure measurements, elevation requirements, and pump selection.

The extended energy balance with a pump

Between two points in a steady, incompressible system, a useful model is P₁/(ρg) + v₁²/(2g) + z₁ + Hₚ = P₂/(ρg) + v₂²/(2g) + z₂ + hL. Here z is elevation, hL is the positive total head loss from pipe resistance and fittings, and Hₚ is the head supplied by one pump. Rearranging gives Hₚ = (P₂−P₁)/(ρg) + (v₂²−v₁²)/(2g) + (z₂−z₁) + hL.

This form assumes no turbine extracting energy and no other significant shaft-work interactions between the selected points. It is not a complete model of every possible pipe network. Nevertheless, it makes the engineering logic visible: to deliver water to a higher location with a required outlet pressure, the pump must provide the elevation rise, the desired pressure-head increase, any velocity-head change, and the losses. A student who adds only elevation and ignores friction may select an undersized pump.

Hydraulic output and actual input power

A pump's efficiency η is the ratio of useful hydraulic power delivered to the fluid to the relevant power input: η = Pₕ/Pᵢₙ. Therefore Pᵢₙ = Pₕ/η. An efficiency of 75% is entered as 0.75, not 75. If a pump transfers 600 W to the water at 75% efficiency, it requires 800 W at the efficiency boundary being modeled. The remaining 200 W is associated with losses such as internal fluid friction, leakage, and mechanical or electrical losses, depending on how the efficiency was defined.

Energy use over time

Energy consumption is power multiplied by operating time. If a pump draws 0.80 kW for five hours, it uses 4.0 kWh of energy. At a hypothetical electricity rate of $0.15 per kWh, the energy charge is $0.60 for that operating period. This simple calculation helps students connect engineering choices with operating cost. A small reduction in input power may become important when a pump runs for many hours over months or years.

Why real pumps have operating limits

In the simplified exercises, required head and flow are calculated from stated system conditions. In reality, the flow a pump delivers depends on the intersection of its pump performance curve and the system curve. The pump curve shows the head a particular pump can supply at different flows, while the system curve shows the head the piping network demands. Increasing resistance often shifts the operating point toward lower flow. A pump rated for a particular maximum head does not necessarily provide that head at its maximum flow.

Other practical considerations include {v('cavitation')}, the formation and collapse of vapor bubbles when local pressure becomes too low, and safe operating limits for seals, bearings, and motors. These topics are introduced qualitatively so students do not mistake a mathematical power calculation for a verified equipment selection. The lesson's explorer checks the energy requirement and an assumed available-head rating, but it does not reproduce a real manufacturer's pump curve or certify an installation.

Fluid forces: drag on an object

Fluid flow can also exert a force on objects immersed in or moving through the fluid. Drag force acts opposite the object's motion relative to the surrounding fluid, or opposite the relative flow direction when the object is stationary. Drag matters for vehicles, bicycles, underwater equipment, structural supports, and instruments placed in moving water. In many engineering applications, reducing drag decreases the force and power required to maintain motion.

The square-law effect in the drag model

With density, coefficient, and reference area held fixed, drag varies with the square of relative speed. Doubling speed multiplies modeled drag by four; tripling it multiplies drag by nine. This is a powerful design insight for a cyclist riding into the wind or a sensor mounted in a water channel. It also explains why reducing frontal area or improving shape can reduce the force required to move through a fluid at the same speed.

Comparing pressure, drag, and pump power

Pressure and force are related but not interchangeable. Pressure is force per area; drag is a net force caused by fluid motion around a body. The drag equation uses dynamic pressure ½ρv² multiplied by a coefficient and reference area. Pump hydraulic power, by contrast, describes the rate at which a machine adds mechanical energy to a flowing liquid. A pipe system can require significant pumping power even when the drag on one small object inside the stream is modest.

Engineering selection and the limits of models

A practical pump choice balances sufficient head, required flow, electrical demand, purchase and maintenance costs, and safe operating conditions. The lowest-power option is not automatically the best if it cannot meet the delivery requirement. Conversely, a large pump with more head than necessary may consume extra energy or require controls. Engineers compare feasible alternatives using the same assumptions and report margins to the stated limits.

Seven fully worked engineering examples

For each example, check assumptions, SI units, equations, and interpretation. For water, use ρ = 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s² unless otherwise stated.

Worked example 1: Hydraulic power for a pressure booster

Water flows at 3.0 L/s through a pump that raises pressure by 120 kPa. Assume equal inlet and outlet velocities and elevations. Find hydraulic power.

  1. Step 1: Convert Q = 3.0/1,000 = 0.0030 m³/s and ΔP = 120,000 Pa.
  2. Step 2: Use Pₕ = ΔP Q = 120,000 × 0.0030 = 360 W.
  3. Step 3: Interpret: the pump transfers 360 joules of mechanical energy to the water each second. This is not electrical input power.

Worked example 2: Pump input power from efficiency

A pump provides 600 W of hydraulic output at 75% overall efficiency. Calculate input power and modeled losses.

  1. Step 1: Write η = 0.75. Percent must be converted to a decimal.
  2. Step 2: Pᵢₙ = 600/0.75 = 800 W.
  3. Step 3: The difference is 800 − 600 = 200 W. It represents modeled conversion losses at the stated efficiency boundary.

Worked example 3: Convert pressure rise to pump head

A pump adds 147.15 kPa to water of density 1,000 kg/m³. The inlet and outlet have equal velocity and elevation. Find pump head.

  1. Step 1: Convert pressure: 147.15 kPa = 147,150 Pa.
  2. Step 2: Hₚ = ΔP/(ρg) = 147,150/(1,000 × 9.81) = 15.0 m.
  3. Step 3: Interpret: 15 m is added energy per unit weight, not necessarily the vertical dimension of the pump.

Worked example 4: Pump head for an uphill line

Water must rise 12 m, overcome 4 m of head loss, and gain 49.05 kPa of pressure at the outlet. Inlet and outlet velocities are equal. Find required pump head.

  1. Step 1: Pressure-head gain = 49,050/(1,000 × 9.81) = 5.0 m.
  2. Step 2: Velocity-head difference is zero; elevation change is +12 m and losses are +4 m.
  3. Step 3: Hₚ = 5 + 12 + 4 = 21 m. Ignoring losses would underestimate the pump requirement.

Worked example 5: Pump power for a distribution line

A water line requires 18 m of pump head at 4.0 L/s. Overall efficiency is 80%. Find hydraulic and input power.

  1. Step 1: Q = 0.0040 m³/s and η = 0.80.
  2. Step 2: Pₕ = ρgQHₚ = 1,000 × 9.81 × 0.0040 × 18 = 706.32 W.
  3. Step 3: Pᵢₙ = 706.32/0.80 = 882.9 W. State that this is modeled input at the defined overall efficiency.

Worked example 6: Drag on an underwater sensor

A stationary sensor faces water moving at 2.0 m/s. Use ρ = 1,000 kg/m³, Cᴅ = 1.1, and projected area A = 0.020 m². Find drag.

  1. Step 1: Use Fᴅ = ½ρCᴅAv² with the specified reference area.
  2. Step 2: Fᴅ = 0.5 × 1,000 × 1.1 × 0.020 × (2.0)² = 44 N.
  3. Step 3: If speed doubles with the same coefficient, the model predicts 176 N. Mounting strength and changing coefficients need further verification.

Worked example 7: Choose between two preliminary pumps

A line requires 16 m of head at 3 L/s. Candidate A is assumed capable of 18 m at that flow and has 65% overall efficiency; candidate B is assumed capable of 22 m and has 80% efficiency. Compare input power using the required 16 m head.

  1. Step 1: Both stated head capabilities exceed the 16 m requirement at the specified flow; A has 2 m margin and B has 6 m margin.
  2. Step 2: Required hydraulic power is 1,000 × 9.81 × 0.003 × 16 = 470.88 W.
  3. Step 3: At the required operating point, A needs 470.88/0.65 = 724.4 W and B needs 470.88/0.80 = 588.6 W. Recommend B on modeled energy use, but verify actual pump curves, control behavior, and costs before purchase.

Engineering analysis and misconceptions

A pump calculation is a preliminary model, not proof that a particular product will deliver the selected flow. The most common error is to treat head and flow as independent fixed ratings that can both be reached simultaneously. Actual pump curves must be checked at the operating point. Likewise, a system may have enough theoretical hydraulic power but fail because suction pressure is inadequate, a motor overheats, or the pipe losses are underestimated.

A second error is to confuse hydraulic output with electrical input. Dividing by efficiency gives input power; multiplying by efficiency gives useful output when input is known. Because efficiency is below 1, the input must exceed the hydraulic output for an ordinary real pump. If a calculated input is smaller than output, revisit the efficiency operation and whether the percent was converted to a decimal.

Pressure rise alone does not always describe pump energy: velocity and elevation matter too. Drag is a force in newtons, not a pressure, and its coefficient depends on the specified conditions.

Compare only feasible candidates using input power, head margin, and operating cost. Verify pump curves and safety before purchasing equipment.

  1. Step 1: η = 0.60.

  3. Step 3: Modeled losses = 800 − 480 = 320 W.

Water gains 98.1 kPa of pressure across a pump with equal inlet and outlet velocities and elevations. Find head.

  2. Step 2: Hₚ = 98,100/(1,000 × 9.81) = 10 m.

Practice 4

A line rises 8 m, loses 3 m to friction and fittings, and must deliver 19.62 kPa more pressure at the outlet than at the inlet. Velocities are equal. Find required pump head.

Show numbered solution
  1. Step 1: Pressure-head gain = 19,620/9,810 = 2 m.
  2. Step 2: Hₚ = 8 + 3 + 2 = 13 m.

A pump supplies 25 m head to water flowing at 1.5 L/s. Overall efficiency is 75%. Find hydraulic and input power.

  2. Step 2: Doubling velocity quadruples modeled drag: 108 N.
  3. Step 3: This conclusion assumes unchanged coefficient, density, and area.

  2. Step 2: X: 274.68/0.70 = 392.4 W; Y: 274.68/0.85 ≈ 323.2 W.

Lesson synthesis and preparation for B3.6

A pump adds mechanical energy to liquid flow. For equal inlet and outlet velocity and elevation, hydraulic power can be calculated from pressure rise and flow rate: Pₕ = ΔP Q. More generally, hydraulic power is ρgQHₚ, where pump head is the energy added per unit weight. Input power exceeds hydraulic output because efficiency is below 100%, so Pᵢₙ = Pₕ/η. Elevation rise, desired pressure increase, velocity changes, and pipe losses all contribute to required head.

Drag is a separate fluid-force application. Under the specified quadratic model, Fᴅ = ½ρCᴅAv², and drag increases fourfold when speed doubles with all other quantities fixed. Both pump calculations and drag predictions depend on explicit assumptions. In B3.6, you will combine flow, continuity, resistance, pump selection, and energy use to design a water-distribution system under multiple constraints.
