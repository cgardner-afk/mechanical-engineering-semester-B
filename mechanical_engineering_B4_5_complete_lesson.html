Readiness Check

Before studying this lesson, recall that conduction transfers energy through a solid without bulk motion, and that a heat transfer rate in watts is not the same as accumulated energy in joules. Temperature differences can be expressed in kelvins or Celsius degrees, but the absolute temperatures used in radiation calculations must be in kelvins. To convert, add 273.15 to a Celsius temperature.

For example, a surface at 60°C is at 333.15 K. Air at 20°C is at 293.15 K, so their temperature difference is 40 K. When you multiply a heat transfer rate by elapsed time in seconds, you obtain energy transferred in joules. These conversions are important because the two heat-transfer models in this lesson use temperature in different ways.

Check yourself: A cooling system removes 80 W continuously for five minutes. How much energy does it remove?
Reveal calculation

Five minutes is 300 s, so Q = 80 J/s × 300 s = 24,000 J = 24 kJ.

Core Concepts

Three ways thermal energy moves

Heat transfer is the movement of energy because of a temperature difference. In Lesson B4.4, you examined conduction: energy traveling through matter by microscopic interactions without bulk movement of the material. Convection and radiation are different mechanisms, and they frequently operate alongside conduction in real engineering systems. A warm electronic component may conduct heat into a metal heat sink, transfer energy from the heat sink into moving air by convection, and emit radiation toward surrounding surfaces at the same time.

Engineers must distinguish the mechanisms because each responds to different design variables. Thick insulation can reduce conduction through a wall, but it may not stop air leakage. A fan can increase convection without changing the thermal conductivity of the metal it cools. A surface coating can change radiation without changing the dimensions of the object. Understanding the differences allows an engineer to select an intervention that actually addresses the dominant source of heating or cooling.

Convection requires a fluid

Convection is heat transfer between a surface and a fluid such as air or water when the fluid moves. A warm surface transfers energy to adjacent air; as that air moves away, cooler air can replace it. The process continually brings fluid at different temperatures into contact with the surface. Convection is not simply a synonym for hot air rising: forced air moving across a surface can also carry heat away. The rate depends on surface area, the surface-to-fluid temperature difference, and the flow conditions.

In natural convection, fluid movement develops because temperature changes its density. Air warmed near a heater often becomes less dense than nearby cooler air, and buoyancy drives circulation. Natural convection can occur without a fan or pump, but the flow can be slow and sensitive to orientation. A vertical warm wall may create an upward plume, while a horizontal surface may behave differently. This is why a single convection coefficient cannot describe every geometry and situation.

Forced convection and cooling equipment

Forced convection occurs when a fan, pump, or other device moves fluid across a surface. Computer cooling fans are a familiar example. They carry warmed air away from heat-sink fins and replace it with cooler air. Liquid-cooling systems use pumps to circulate coolant through channels and radiators. Forced convection can increase the heat transfer rate, but it also consumes power and may add noise, vibration, maintenance requirements, or points of failure.

The thin region of fluid next to a solid surface is called a boundary layer. Temperature and fluid velocity change significantly across this region. Faster flow and turbulence can alter the boundary layer and often increase heat transfer. In introductory calculations, these complicated fluid effects are represented by an effective convection coefficient h. Engineers obtain h from experiments, correlations, or more detailed models rather than treating it as a universal constant.

Newton's law of cooling

Q̇conv = hA(Ts − T∞)

Here Q̇conv is the signed convection heat rate in watts, h is the convection coefficient in W/(m²·K), A is exposed surface area in m², Ts is the surface temperature, and T∞ is the fluid temperature away from the surface. A positive result represents heat leaving a warmer surface. If the fluid is warmer than the surface, the signed result is negative, indicating heating of the surface. The magnitude is found by using the absolute temperature difference.

If h, area, and temperature difference are fixed, doubling area doubles the convection heat rate. Doubling h also doubles the predicted rate. However, h may change with temperature, orientation, airflow speed, and geometry, so these proportional relationships apply only when the other assumptions truly remain fixed. Engineers often use this equation to estimate whether a cooling fan or larger heat sink can reject a specified amount of heat.

Thermal radiation needs no moving air

Every object above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation. Thermal radiation can transfer energy through empty space, so it does not require air or direct contact. The Sun warms Earth primarily through radiation across space. A warm machine enclosure also emits infrared radiation toward cooler surrounding surfaces. Unlike convection, radiation can continue in a vacuum, which makes it especially important in spacecraft thermal control.

A perfect blackbody is an idealized surface that emits the maximum possible thermal radiation at a given temperature and absorbs all incident radiation. Real surfaces are described by emissivity, symbol ε, a number between zero and one in the simplified gray-surface model. A high-emissivity surface radiates more effectively than a low-emissivity surface at the same absolute temperature. Surface color in visible light does not uniquely determine infrared emissivity; finish, coating, wavelength, and temperature also matter.

The Stefan–Boltzmann law

Q̇rad = εσA(Ts4 − Tsur4)

In this simplified model, σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, approximately 5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). Ts is the surface's absolute temperature in kelvins, and Tsur is an effective surrounding radiation temperature in kelvins. The expression assumes a gray diffuse surface exchanging radiation with a large, approximately isothermal surrounding environment, with an effective view factor of one. More complicated geometries require view factors and additional surface properties.

The fourth powers are essential. Radiation does not increase linearly with absolute temperature. A hot surface can emit dramatically more energy than a moderately warm one. Celsius values must never be substituted directly into the fourth-power expression. Convert both temperatures to kelvins first, then raise each to the fourth power before subtracting. The result is the net exchange: energy emitted toward the surroundings minus energy absorbed from them under the stated model.

Combined heat transfer and energy balance

Q̇out = Q̇conv + Q̇rad

A warm panel exposed to air and surrounding surfaces can lose heat by both mechanisms simultaneously. If the convection and radiation models apply to the same surface, their predicted rates can be added. At thermal equilibrium, the rate of heat generated inside an enclosure equals the total rate leaving it, assuming there are no other energy inputs or outputs. The component may stay hot, but its temperature no longer rises because the incoming and outgoing energy rates balance.

This equilibrium principle can be used in reverse. If an electronic device generates 100 W, an engineer can estimate the surface temperature at which convection plus radiation would remove 100 W. The answer is a simplified surface temperature, not necessarily the temperature of the hottest chip inside. Thermal contact resistance, internal conduction, uneven airflow, and local hotspots can cause important differences. A safety-critical design needs more complete modeling and testing.

Engineering applications

Electronics designers combine conductive heat spreaders, finned heat sinks, and forced airflow to protect processors and power components. Building engineers consider natural convection near windows, forced convection in ventilation ducts, and radiation between interior surfaces. Industrial equipment designers may use cooling jackets and fans, while spacecraft engineers rely heavily on radiation because ordinary atmospheric convection is unavailable in vacuum. The same physical principles support very different design choices.

No single heat-transfer mechanism is always the most important. At modest temperatures with strong airflow, convection may dominate. At high surface temperatures, radiation can become substantial because of the fourth-power dependence. In a sealed vacuum enclosure, radiation may be the main path between separated surfaces. The engineering task is to identify the actual conditions, select appropriate models, calculate predicted performance, and state the uncertainty and limitations.

Seven Worked Examples

1 · Still-air convection

Engineering situation and known values: A warm metal panel has area 0.60 m², surface temperature 65°C, surrounding air temperature 25°C, and estimated natural-convection coefficient h = 6 W/(m²·K).

Q̇conv = hA(Ts − T∞) = 6(0.60)(65 − 25) = 144 W.

Engineering interpretation: This is the estimated heat rate transferred to the air by convection alone. Radiation can remove additional energy.

2 · Fan-assisted cooling

Engineering situation and known values: A component's 0.25 m² cooling surface is at 70°C while air is at 30°C. A fan raises the estimated convection coefficient from 5 to 35 W/(m²·K).

Still air: Q̇ = 5(0.25)(40) = 50 W. Fan: Q̇ = 35(0.25)(40) = 350 W.

Engineering interpretation: The fan increases the modeled convection rate sevenfold at the same temperatures; real h depends on airflow geometry and operating conditions.

3 · Required heat-sink area

Engineering situation and known values: A heat sink must reject 90 W by convection at a surface temperature of 60°C in 30°C air, with h = 20 W/(m²·K).

A = Q̇/[h(Ts − T∞)] = 90/[20(30)] = 0.150 m².

Engineering interpretation: A finned heat sink increases exposed surface area. Actual fin efficiency and airflow would require further analysis.

4 · Polished versus dark surface

Engineering situation and known values: Two surfaces have the same area 0.40 m², surface temperature 350 K, and surroundings at 290 K. Their emissivities are 0.10 and 0.90.

σA(Ts4 − Tsur4) ≈ 180.1 W. Polished: 0.10(180.1) = 18.0 W. Dark: 0.90(180.1) = 162.1 W.

Engineering interpretation: Under equal temperatures, the higher-emissivity surface radiates nine times as much net thermal energy.

5 · Radiative heat loss

Engineering situation and known values: A panel has ε = 0.80, area 1.2 m², temperature 400 K, and surroundings 300 K.

Q̇rad = εσA(Ts4 − Tsur4) = 0.8(5.670374419×10−8)(1.2)(4004 − 3004) ≈ 952.5 W.

Engineering interpretation: Absolute temperature is required; substituting Celsius values into the fourth-power equation would give an incorrect answer.

6 · Combined convection and radiation

Engineering situation and known values: A 0.50 m² surface is at 80°C (353.15 K); both air and radiative surroundings are at 20°C (293.15 K). h = 10 W/(m²·K), ε = 0.80.

Q̇conv = 10(0.50)(60) = 300 W. Q̇rad ≈ 0.8σ(0.50)(353.154 − 293.154) ≈ 185.3 W. Total ≈ 485.3 W.

Engineering interpretation: Both mechanisms operate simultaneously. Their heat rates can be added when each model applies to the same surface and specified surroundings.

7 · Temperature limit

Engineering situation and known values: An electronic enclosure dissipates 120 W, has A = 0.40 m², air at 25°C, and convection coefficient h = 15 W/(m²·K). First ignore radiation.

Ts = T∞ + Q̇/(hA) = 25 + 120/(15×0.40) = 45°C.

Engineering interpretation: The convection-only estimate is below a 55°C limit. Including outward radiation would lower the equilibrium estimate, while thermal contact resistance and hotspots could raise component temperatures.

Independent Practice

Work through each problem independently before opening the full solution. Remember to use kelvins in fourth-power radiation calculations.

Problem 1: A plate has h = 8 W/(m²·K), A = 0.75 m², surface temperature 55°C, and air at 25°C. Calculate convection heat rate.

Worked solution: Q̇ = hAΔT = 8(0.75)(30) = 180 W.

Problem 2: A fan raises h from 5 to 25 W/(m²·K) with all other variables unchanged. By what factor does convection heat transfer increase?

Worked solution: Q̇ is proportional to h. The new heat rate is 25/5 = 5 times the original.

Problem 3: A device must release 200 W by convection with h = 20 W/(m²·K) and surface-air difference 40 K. Find required area.

Worked solution: A = Q̇/(hΔT) = 200/(20×40) = 0.25 m².

Problem 4: Convert 85°C and 20°C to kelvins for radiation calculations.

Worked solution: T₁ = 85 + 273.15 = 358.15 K; T₂ = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15 K.

Problem 5: A surface with emissivity 0.20 emits 60 W of net radiation under fixed geometry and temperatures. Estimate net radiation if emissivity rises to 0.80.

Worked solution: Radiation rate is proportional to emissivity. 60×(0.80/0.20) = 240 W.

Problem 6: A panel loses 125 W by convection and 75 W by radiation. How much energy does it lose in 30 minutes?

Worked solution: Total rate = 200 W; time = 30×60 = 1800 s; Q = 200×1800 = 360,000 J = 360 kJ.

Problem 7: A 150 W electronic load must stay below 65°C in 25°C air with A = 0.50 m². Ignoring radiation, find the minimum h.

Worked solution: h ≥ Q̇/[A(Tmax−Tair)] = 150/[0.50(40)] = 7.5 W/(m²·K).

Vocabulary

Each highlighted term expands independently. Try defining it yourself before opening the explanation.

Convection

Heat transfer between a surface and a moving fluid.

Natural convection

Fluid motion driven by buoyancy from density differences.

Forced convection

Fluid motion produced by a fan, pump, or other external device.

Buoyancy

Upward force caused by pressure differences in a fluid.

Convection coefficient

Parameter h relating surface-air temperature difference to convection heat rate.

Newton's law of cooling

Model Q̇ = hA(Ts − T∞) for convection.

Ambient temperature

Temperature of the surrounding fluid away from a surface.

Surface temperature

Temperature at the heat-exchanging surface.

Heat transfer rate

Thermal energy transferred per second, measured in watts.

Thermal radiation

Heat transfer by electromagnetic radiation.

Emissivity

Ratio of a real surface's thermal emission to an ideal blackbody at the same temperature.

Blackbody

Ideal surface that absorbs all incident radiation and has emissivity one.

Stefan–Boltzmann constant

Physical constant σ ≈ 5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴).

Stefan–Boltzmann law

Radiative heat transfer relationship involving fourth powers of absolute temperature.

Absolute temperature

Temperature measured from absolute zero, expressed in kelvins.

Heat sink

Component designed to transfer heat away from a source.

Cooling fan

Device that forces airflow to increase convective cooling.

Thermal management

Engineering control of temperatures and heat transfer.

Radiative surroundings

Surfaces represented by an effective surrounding radiation temperature.

Net radiation

Radiation emitted minus radiation absorbed from surroundings.

Equilibrium temperature

Temperature at which modeled outgoing heat rate balances incoming heat rate.

Energy balance

Accounting for energy entering, leaving, and stored in a system.

Boundary layer

Region near a surface where fluid velocity and temperature change strongly.

Model limitation

An assumption or omitted effect that restricts how accurately a model predicts reality.

Lesson Summary

Convection transfers energy between a surface and moving fluid, while radiation transfers energy through electromagnetic waves and requires no material medium. Natural convection arises from buoyancy, whereas forced convection uses equipment such as fans or pumps to move fluid. Newton's law of cooling models convection through a coefficient h, exposed area, and the surface-to-fluid temperature difference.

Thermal radiation depends on emissivity, area, and the fourth powers of absolute temperatures. The Stefan–Boltzmann law must use kelvins, not Celsius temperatures. In a real system, conduction, convection, and radiation can all occur simultaneously. Engineers can estimate total outward heat transfer by adding compatible heat-rate models and can use an energy balance to predict an equilibrium surface temperature.

Good thermal design is not only about maximizing cooling. It also involves size, cost, energy use, noise, reliability, material properties, and safety. A classroom calculation can identify promising options, but actual components require measurements and more complete modeling before engineers can claim reliable performance.
