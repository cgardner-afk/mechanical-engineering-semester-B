How can a person raise a heavy load with a modest effort? Discover why machine geometry changes the force you need—and why the distance you move matters just as much.

Prerequisite support

Math and vocabulary checkpoint

Engineering calculations describe physical quantities, not just numbers. A force is measured in newtons (N), a distance in meters (m), and mechanical work in joules (J), where one joule equals one newton-meter of work. For a constant force acting in the direction of movement, work is force multiplied by distance: W = F × d. When the force is not parallel to the movement, only the component in the direction of travel contributes to work; our introductory models use aligned forces or equivalent travel along their paths.

From Semester A, recall that a moment describes the turning effect of a force about a pivot. For a perpendicular force, moment magnitude is M = F × r, where r is the perpendicular lever arm. A balanced lever has equal opposing moments about its fulcrum. This earlier concept will explain why a longer effort arm can reduce the force needed to support a load.

Engineering principles

Why machines make difficult work manageable

Imagine a technician installing a heavy motor above a workshop floor. The motor is too heavy to lift directly by hand, but a carefully chosen pulley arrangement can let the technician raise it by pulling on a rope with much less force. Nothing mysterious has happened to the motor's weight. The pulley changes how the effort is transmitted, and the technician must pull a greater length of rope to obtain a smaller rise in the load. That exchange is the central idea of mechanical advantage.

The six classical simple machines

Lever

A rigid bar rotates about a fulcrum. Changing the distances from the fulcrum to the effort and load changes the force ratio. Crowbars and hand-operated presses use this principle.

Inclined plane

A ramp raises a load gradually rather than vertically. A longer path with the same rise reduces the ideal force needed along the ramp.

Pulley

A grooved wheel guides a rope or cable. A fixed pulley changes pull direction; multiple supporting rope segments can reduce ideal effort at the cost of additional rope travel.

Wheel and axle

Two rigidly connected rotating radii share an angle of rotation. A larger effort radius can provide a larger turning moment with less applied force.

Wedge

A moving inclined surface redirects an applied force sideways, as in a splitting wedge or cutting edge. The material's resistance and friction matter greatly in real use.

Screw

An inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder converts rotation into axial motion. A small pitch, or advance per revolution for a single-start screw, means more turns for a given lift but greater ideal mechanical advantage.

Conservation of work: the rule behind every machine

For a lossless machine operating steadily, the work supplied at the input equals the useful work delivered at the output. If the forces act along their respective travel paths, the ideal work balance is Fin × din = Fout × dout. Suppose a machine raises a 600 N load by 0.20 m. The useful output work is 120 J. If the operator moves the input through 1.20 m, the ideal effort is 120 J ÷ 1.20 m = 100 N. The operator applies one-sixth of the load force but moves six times as far.

Ideal work balance: Findin = Foutdout
IMA = din / dout = Fout / Fin,ideal

Ideal mechanical advantage (IMA) compares input travel with output travel, or output force with the ideal input force. IMA = 4 means the ideal output force is four times the input force and the input travels four times as far as the output. An IMA below one is also possible: a mechanism may sacrifice force to increase motion or speed. A machine with an IMA of 0.5 requires twice the load force at the input but can move the output twice as far.

Levers: geometry controls the force ratio

On a lever, the effort arm is the perpendicular distance from the fulcrum to the line of action of the applied force, and the load arm is the corresponding distance for the load. For forces perpendicular to a straight bar in static equilibrium, moment balance gives FeffortLeffort = FloadLload. Rearranging shows that the ideal mechanical advantage is Leffort/Lload. This is a ratio of effective moment arms, not simply a comparison of arbitrary bar lengths.

Inclined planes: longer travel, smaller effort

Consider a frictionless ramp of length L that raises a load through vertical height h. The load gains gravitational potential energy equal to its weight multiplied by h. If a force is applied parallel to the ramp at constant speed, ideal input work is FinL. Equating these quantities gives FinL = Floadh and therefore IMA = L/h. Doubling the ramp length without changing the height halves the ideal force, but doubles the required travel. A longer ramp can also require more floor space, so the lowest-force solution is not always the most practical design.

Inclined-plane IMA = ramp length / vertical rise = L / h
Ideal effort = load force × h / L

Pulleys, wheels, screws, and wedges

A single fixed pulley redirects a pull without providing ideal force multiplication: one supporting rope segment corresponds to IMA = 1. A movable pulley can have two segments supporting the moving load, giving ideal IMA = 2 when both segments share tension. In a more complex ideal block-and-tackle arrangement, the number of rope segments directly supporting the moving assembly gives its mechanical advantage, provided the rope routing and attachment are interpreted correctly. Raising the load by 0.5 m with four supporting segments requires approximately 2 m of rope travel. Friction and pulley weight make real performance worse than this ideal prediction.

For an ideal wheel and axle with effort applied tangentially at radius R and the load resisted tangentially at radius r, torque balance gives FinR = Foutr. The ideal force ratio is therefore R/r. Because both rotate through the same angle, the point on the larger radius travels farther. A larger handwheel makes it easier to turn a valve stem, but the hand must move through a longer arc for the same rotation of the axle. The same trade-off appears in hand cranks and winches.

A screw converts rotation into linear advance. For a single-start screw, one revolution advances the load by the thread pitch p. If the operator pushes tangentially at handle radius R, the input point travels 2πR each turn. Under ideal assumptions, IMA = 2πR/p. Reducing pitch increases the number of turns needed for the same lift while reducing ideal input force. Actual screw efficiency can be much lower because sliding contact creates friction; some screw mechanisms are intentionally self-locking. A wedge works through a related inclined-surface principle, but real splitting and cutting involve changing contact forces, friction, and material failure, so a simple geometric IMA alone is not a complete prediction.

Actual mechanical advantage and efficiency

Engineers measure the force a real operator must supply rather than assume every mechanism is frictionless. Actual mechanical advantage (AMA) is the measured output force divided by the measured input force. If a pulley lifts a 360 N load and requires 120 N of effort, its AMA is 3. An ideal four-segment pulley might have IMA = 4, so this actual force ratio is lower than the ideal prediction. The difference indicates losses in the operating system, not an error in conservation of energy.

AMA = Fout / Fin,actual
Efficiency η = (AMA / IMA) × 100%, for matching steady operating conditions

Using the preceding example, efficiency is (3/4) × 100% = 75%. The formula follows from useful output work divided by actual input work when the geometric travel ratio equals the stated IMA. It assumes the same load and operating configuration and consistent force definitions. Efficiency is not simply the difference between two forces. It is a ratio of energy or work; the AMA/IMA shortcut is valid for the idealized steady relationships taught here.

Worked engineering methods

Step-by-step worked examples

Example 1 · Select a lever for a maintenance lift

Given: A lever has a perpendicular effort arm of 1.20 m and a load arm of 0.30 m. The load force is 480 N. Find: IMA and ideal effort. Model: Rigid lever, perpendicular forces, negligible weight and friction, static balance.

IMA = 1.20 / 0.30 = 4
Feffort = 480 / 4 = 120 N

Interpretation: The technician applies one-quarter of the load force. If the load rises 0.10 m, the effort point moves 0.40 m along its arc for the same angular displacement. In a real device, the arm's weight and friction at the pivot may increase the effort.

Example 2 · Calculate the effort on an access ramp

Given: A frictionless ramp is 6.0 m long and rises 1.5 m. A loaded cart exerts 800 N downward. Find: Ideal force parallel to the ramp at constant speed. Model: No rolling resistance or friction; the input force acts uphill along the ramp.

IMA = 6.0 / 1.5 = 4
Fin,ideal = 800 × 1.5 / 6.0 = 200 N
Input work = 200 × 6.0 = 1,200 J; output work = 800 × 1.5 = 1,200 J

Interpretation: The ramp reduces the required uphill force to one-quarter of the weight, but the cart must move four times the vertical rise. An engineer would next check whether a six-meter ramp fits the site and whether the surface provides safe traction.

Example 3 · Compare ideal and actual pulley performance

Given: Four rope segments support a moving load of 500 N. The operator measures an effort of 160 N. Find: IMA, AMA, and efficiency. Model: Each ideal supporting segment carries equal tension; measured effort includes mechanical losses.

IMA = 4
AMA = 500 / 160 = 3.125
η = (3.125 / 4) × 100% = 78.125% ≈ 78.1%

Interpretation: The actual force ratio is below the ideal value. If the load rises 0.25 m and the operator pulls 1.00 m of rope, useful work is 125 J while input work is 160 J. Their ratio, 125/160, confirms 78.125% efficiency.

Example 4 · Determine wheel-and-axle handle force

Given: A handwheel radius is 0.24 m and the axle radius is 0.06 m. A tangential resisting force of 360 N acts at the axle. Find: Ideal tangential hand force and force ratio. Model: Rigid assembly, steady equilibrium, negligible friction.

IMA = 0.24 / 0.06 = 4
Fhand = 360 × 0.06 / 0.24 = 90 N

Example 5 · Compare two screw-jack pitches

Given: A screw jack uses a 0.20 m crank radius. Compare pitches of 5 mm/revolution and 2.5 mm/revolution for a 50 mm lift. Find: turns and ideal mechanical advantages. Model: Single-start screw, tangential crank force, no friction.

Turns at 5 mm pitch = 50 / 5 = 10; IMA = 2π(0.20)/0.005 ≈ 251.3
Turns at 2.5 mm pitch = 50 / 2.5 = 20; IMA = 2π(0.20)/0.0025 ≈ 502.7

Guided practice: fade the supports

Guided step B: A ramp rises 0.8 m over a slope length of 4.0 m and carries a 700 N load. First calculate IMA yourself. Then compare with 4.0 ÷ 0.8 = 5. Next divide the load force by 5. Your ideal result should be 140 N; check it by comparing 140 × 4.0 J with 700 × 0.8 J.

Guided step C: A three-segment pulley lifts 300 N with a measured effort of 125 N. Determine AMA first, then divide by IMA = 3 to find efficiency. Work independently before opening the solution below.

Reveal guided step C solution

AMA = 300/125 = 2.4. Efficiency = (2.4/3) × 100% = 80%. The force ratio of 2.4 is smaller than the ideal 3 because the actual mechanism has losses.

Investigation procedure

Predict: In lever mode, what happens to effort if you double the effort arm while holding the load arm and weight fixed? Record a baseline trial, change only the effort arm, and record a second trial. Compare the predicted and calculated efforts. Explain the change using moment balance, not merely the displayed number.

Observe and calculate: In ramp mode, keep the rise and load fixed while increasing ramp length. Record two trials. Calculate the work for a 0.20 m rise or, equivalently, compare input and output work for the full rise. Explain why a gentler ramp reduces effort but does not eliminate the energy required to raise the load.

Analyze losses: In real-versus-ideal mode, choose a physically consistent measured effort at or above the ideal effort. Record one trial, increase measured effort without changing rope segments or load, and record another. Describe what happens to AMA and efficiency. An increased measured force at the same load and geometry represents more mechanical loss, not an increase in useful output.

Apply: Design a lever that lifts a 600 N load using no more than 200 N ideal effort. The effort arm must be at most 1.50 m and the load arm must be at least 0.30 m. Identify one feasible setting, calculate its IMA and effort, and state how far the effort point moves if the load moves 0.10 m. Then explain one real-world factor that could make the design unsafe or less effective than the ideal model.

Design challenge: example feasibility check (open after attempting)

One feasible choice is a 1.20 m effort arm and a 0.30 m load arm. IMA = 1.20/0.30 = 4, so ideal effort = 600/4 = 150 N, below the 200 N limit. For a 0.10 m load displacement, the effort point travels 0.40 m along its arc. This meets the stated geometry limits, but a real engineer must still check bending stress, pivot strength, stability, and operating clearance.

Engineering judgment

Common errors and reasoning checks

Error 3: Claiming a machine creates work. If a 400 N load rises 0.50 m, the output work is 200 J. An ideal input force of 100 N must move through 2.0 m, not 0.50 m. The mistaken calculation of 100 × 0.50 = 50 J overlooks the longer input path. Always check whether the work balance is satisfied.

Error 4: Confusing ideal and actual forces. Suppose a four-segment pulley raises 400 N. Its ideal effort is 100 N. If a measurement gives 125 N, AMA = 400/125 = 3.2 and efficiency is 80%. Dividing the actual input force by the load reverses the definition. Likewise, reporting 3.2 as 3.2% efficiency confuses a force ratio with a percentage.

Error 5: Accepting impossible passive-machine data. For the same load and travel geometry, a passive machine with friction cannot require less work than the lossless ideal. A measured input effort smaller than the ideal effort would imply efficiency greater than 100% under our assumptions. Recheck the measurements, support count, or whether an external energy source is present before accepting such a result.

Good engineers also question whether their idealized assumptions fit the application. A lever's fulcrum may bend, a ramp's surface may resist rolling, and a pulley may have heavy moving components. This lesson isolates the force–distance principle; subsequent engineering analysis must consider loads on components, allowable stresses, stability, and the consequences of failure.

Independent practice

Practice with guided solutions

Practice 1 · Lever geometry

A technician uses a lever with a 1.50 m effort arm and a 0.50 m load arm. What is its IMA? If the load is 720 N, what ideal effort is required? Solution: IMA = 1.50/0.50 = 3. Ideal effort = 720/3 = 240 N. Because the effort arm is three times the load arm, the effort point travels three times as far as the load point for the same lever rotation.

Practice 2 · Ramp force and work

A frictionless 7.5 m ramp raises a 900 N load by 1.5 m. Determine IMA, ideal effort, and input work for the full rise. Solution: IMA = 7.5/1.5 = 5. Effort = 900/5 = 180 N. Input work = 180 × 7.5 = 1,350 J. Output work = 900 × 1.5 = 1,350 J, confirming ideal work balance.

Practice 3 · Pulley efficiency

Five ideal rope segments support a 750 N load, but the measured effort is 180 N. Find AMA and efficiency. Solution: IMA = 5. AMA = 750/180 ≈ 4.167. Efficiency = (4.167/5) × 100% ≈ 83.3%. The result is physically plausible because actual effort exceeds the ideal 150 N.

Practice 4 · Input travel

An ideal machine has IMA = 6 and raises a platform by 0.35 m. How far must the input move? If the load force is 1,200 N, what is the ideal effort? Solution: Input travel = 6 × 0.35 = 2.10 m. Ideal effort = 1,200/6 = 200 N. Both sides perform 420 J of work, as expected.

Practice 5 · Wheel-and-axle trade-off

A 0.30 m handle radius drives a 0.05 m axle radius. Find IMA and ideal hand force for a 480 N tangential axle load. What happens if the handle radius is reduced to 0.15 m? Solution: Initial IMA = 0.30/0.05 = 6 and hand force = 480/6 = 80 N. With the smaller handle, IMA = 3 and effort = 160 N. The smaller handle requires more force but less hand travel per revolution and may fit a tighter space.

Practice 6 · Screw-jack design

A single-start screw has a 4 mm pitch. How many revolutions are needed to lift a load by 64 mm? If pitch is reduced to 2 mm, how many turns are required and what happens to ideal mechanical advantage for the same crank radius? Solution: At 4 mm/revolution, 64/4 = 16 turns. At 2 mm/revolution, 64/2 = 32 turns. Since IMA = 2πR/p, halving pitch doubles IMA and doubles turns, assuming the same crank radius and ideal conditions.

Vocabulary & glossary

Vocabulary and key concepts

Force, distance, and work

Force is a push or pull measured in newtons. Distance is movement along a path, measured in meters. Work is energy transferred by a force acting through displacement; for aligned constant force, W = Fd, measured in joules.

Simple machine and mechanical advantage
Ideal mechanical advantage (IMA)

The geometric force ratio of a lossless machine: IMA = input travel/output travel. For a lever, IMA = effort arm/load arm; for a frictionless inclined plane, IMA = slope length/vertical rise.

Actual mechanical advantage (AMA)

The measured force ratio, AMA = output force/actual input force. It incorporates the effects of losses and other real operating conditions.

Efficiency

The fraction of input work converted into useful output work, usually reported as a percentage. For matching force and travel definitions in these steady mechanisms, η = (AMA/IMA) × 100%.

Fulcrum, effort arm, load arm, and moment

The fulcrum is a lever's pivot. Effort and load arms are perpendicular distances from the pivot to the relevant force lines of action. A moment is the turning effect of a force about a pivot, equal to force times perpendicular arm length.

Inclined plane, pulley, and supporting segment
Wheel and axle, screw pitch, and wedge

A wheel and axle has rigidly connected radii that rotate together. Screw pitch is axial advance per revolution for a single-start screw. A wedge is a moving inclined surface that redirects applied force, often to separate or cut material.

Lesson synthesis

Lesson summary

Use the ideal work balance to connect input and output, then choose the geometry equation appropriate to the machine. For levers, compare perpendicular moment arms; for ramps, compare sloped length with vertical rise. When measurements are available, calculate AMA from the actual forces and efficiency from AMA divided by IMA. Finally, ask whether the result is physically plausible and whether the design meets all constraints. Those habits—model, calculate, check, and justify—will carry into rotational speed, torque, gear trains, and power transmission later in Unit B1.
