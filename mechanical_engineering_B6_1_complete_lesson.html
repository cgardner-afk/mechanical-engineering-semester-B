Core Concepts and Engineering Models

1. Why machines need power

Every mechanical system needs an energy supply to do useful work. A crane raises loads, a conveyor moves materials, and a motor drives the wheels of a vehicle. The supply may be fuel burned in an engine, electricity delivered through a cable, or energy stored in a battery. The power source must meet the machine's task requirements. A device can contain enough energy for a job yet still be unable to deliver it quickly enough.

Imagine a portable winch that must lift a heavy object in a short time. A small battery might contain the required total energy, but its electrical output limit may prevent the motor from generating the necessary power. Conversely, a powerful motor can perform a task quickly but exhaust a small battery in minutes. Engineers therefore examine both the quantity of energy available and the rate at which it can be supplied. These two questions are related but not interchangeable.

2. Energy and work

Energy is a conserved quantity that can be stored or transferred. Work is one way energy crosses a system boundary: a force acting through a displacement can transfer energy mechanically. In the simplest aligned-force situation, W = Fd. A force of 100 N moving an object 3 m in the same direction transfers 300 J of work.

The SI unit of energy and work is the joule (J). One kilojoule (kJ) is 1000 J, and one megajoule (MJ) is one million joules. Energy is not the same as force. A force can be large while the displacement is zero, producing zero mechanical work at the point of application. In actual machines, energy also leaves as unwanted heating, vibration, and sound. An energy balance accounts for these outputs rather than assuming all supplied energy becomes useful motion.

3. Power: the rate of doing work

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or work is performed. For an average over an interval, P = E/t or P = W/t. One watt (W) equals one joule per second. One kilowatt (kW) equals 1000 watts. The unit of power contains time in its definition; energy by itself does not.

If two hoists each lift the same load to the same height, they perform the same useful work. If one completes the lift in half the time, its average useful power is twice as large. A 2000 W machine transfers energy at 2000 J each second while operating at that power. This does not tell us how many seconds it can operate, because runtime also depends on how much usable energy is available. Engineers often specify both rated power and expected operating time.

P = E/t   ·   E = Pt   ·   1 W = 1 J/s

4. Power and mechanical motion

For an aligned force and velocity, instantaneous mechanical power is P = Fv. More generally, P = Fv cos θ, where θ is the angle between force and velocity. When force and motion are perpendicular, that force contributes no instantaneous power. This is why the direction of force matters as well as its magnitude.

A lift pulling upward with 2000 N while rising steadily at 0.5 m/s transfers mechanical power of 2000 × 0.5 = 1000 W. The calculation assumes the pulling force is aligned with the upward velocity. A moving conveyor, winch, or vehicle can be analyzed in the same way when the relevant force component is known. If acceleration or changing forces are involved, power may vary during the motion, and a single calculation may represent only one instant.

P = Fv (aligned force and velocity)

5. Converting units and horsepower

Mechanical equipment specifications use watts, kilowatts, and sometimes horsepower (hp). Mechanical horsepower is approximately 746 W, so a 10 hp motor corresponds to about 7460 W or 7.46 kW. Horsepower and kilowatts both describe power, not stored energy.

Another important conversion is 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ = 3600 kJ. A kilowatt-hour is an energy unit: it is the energy transferred by one kilowatt operating for one hour. A 2 kW motor operating for 3 hours receives 6 kWh if the 2 kW is its electrical input rating. When working with time in seconds and energy in joules, the answer is watts; with time in hours and energy in kilowatt-hours, the answer is kilowatts.

1 mechanical hp ≈ 746 W   ·   1 kWh = 3.6 MJ

6. Chemical fuels and combustion engines

A fuel stores chemical energy. Gasoline, diesel, natural gas, and hydrogen can release energy through chemical reactions, although the equipment needed to use them differs. In an internal combustion engine, combustion releases thermal energy. Expanding gases then produce mechanical work through pistons or turbines. Not all chemical energy becomes useful shaft work; some is rejected as exhaust heat, cooling-system heat, and frictional losses.

Fuels can offer substantial energy storage per unit mass, and many machines can be refueled relatively quickly. However, combustion equipment needs fuel delivery, cooling, exhaust handling, and maintenance. Fuel prices, emissions, noise, and safety requirements influence selection. A fuel is an energy carrier, while an engine is an energy converter. The fuel does not directly turn a vehicle's wheels without the intervening machinery.

7. Batteries, grids, and electric motors

A battery stores energy electrochemically and supplies electrical energy to a circuit. An electric motor converts electrical input into mechanical output. A grid-connected machine may receive electricity continuously rather than carrying a local energy store. Batteries, motors, and electrical grids play different roles in the energy system.

Battery-electric equipment may offer precise motor control, quiet operation, and no combustion exhaust at the point of use. Its operating time depends on usable stored energy and electrical power demand. Charging time, battery mass, current limits, temperature, and degradation can matter. A motor's conversion efficiency does not describe the entire battery-to-wheel system because power electronics, wiring, gearboxes, and other components also introduce losses.

8. Renewable energy sources

Renewable energy comes from naturally replenished flows such as sunlight, wind, and moving water. A photovoltaic panel converts sunlight to electricity. A wind turbine converts moving air's kinetic energy into rotor motion and then electrical energy through a generator. A hydroelectric turbine extracts energy from flowing or falling water. These systems do not all produce the same energy form directly.

A solar panel's electrical output varies with illumination and installation conditions; wind output varies with wind speed; hydroelectric output depends on water flow and available height. If a machine must run at a fixed power continuously, an intermittent source may require storage, grid support, or another supply. Engineers compare expected output profiles with demand rather than assuming the rated peak power is available at every hour.

9. Energy-conversion chains and losses

An energy-conversion chain traces the transformations between source and useful output. For a battery-powered vehicle, chemical energy stored in the battery becomes electrical energy, passes through power electronics, becomes mechanical shaft power in the motor, and reaches the wheels through a drivetrain. Each stage has an efficiency below or equal to one.

If a controller is 95% efficient, a motor 90%, and a drivetrain 95%, the overall efficiency is 0.95 × 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.81225, or about 81.2%. The overall useful output is the input energy multiplied by this product. Multiplying efficiency percentages directly as 95 × 90 × 95 would be incorrect; convert them to decimals first. A conversion-chain diagram helps identify where losses occur and which component changes could improve performance.

ηoverall = η1 × η2 × η3

10. Efficiency and useful output

Conversion efficiency is useful output energy divided by input energy, or useful output power divided by input power under comparable steady conditions. η = Euseful/Einput = Puseful/Pinput. If a machine receives 5 kW and is 80% efficient, it delivers 4 kW useful output and dissipates 1 kW through other outputs.

Efficiency cannot exceed 100% for a device whose defined useful output is a conversion of the supplied energy into another form. This differs from heat-pump COP, which can exceed one because a heat pump transfers environmental heat in addition to consuming work. When evaluating a motor, efficiency is usually the appropriate ratio. Always identify the system boundary, because a component efficiency can differ from the overall efficiency of the complete machine.

η = Puseful/Pinput   ·   Ploss = Pinput − Puseful

11. Stored energy, power rating, and runtime

Stored energy determines how much input energy can be supplied before an energy store is depleted. Rated power describes a specified output or input capability under stated conditions. A battery holding 20 kWh can theoretically supply a constant 4 kW electrical load for 5 hours if all 20 kWh are usable and losses beyond the specified electrical load are ignored.

If the same battery supplies an 8 kW electrical load, theoretical runtime falls to 2.5 hours. If a motor converts electrical power at 80% efficiency, a 4 kW electrical input yields 3.2 kW useful mechanical output. If a task instead requires 4 kW of mechanical output, the motor must receive 4/0.80 = 5 kW electrical input, reducing the theoretical 20 kWh runtime to 4 hours. These distinctions prevent a common mistake: applying efficiency twice or confusing input and output power.

12. Energy density and power density

Energy density compares stored energy with mass or volume; power density compares the rate of power delivery with mass or volume. A source with high energy density can support long operating time without excessive storage mass. A source with high power density can deliver large power rapidly.

Portable machinery often requires a compromise among weight, operating duration, and peak power. A device that delivers high peak power for a short interval may not be able to sustain that power continuously. Engineers distinguish continuous power from peak power, and they check the manufacturer's ratings at expected operating temperatures. No single storage technology dominates every combination of energy density, power density, cost, and safety.

13. Duty cycle and real operating conditions

A machine's duty cycle describes its operating pattern. A lift motor may run briefly and rest between lifts, whereas a ventilation fan may run for hours. If a 3 kW electrical machine operates for 15 minutes each hour, its hourly energy consumption is 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 kWh, assuming zero power draw while off.

A motor rated for intermittent operation may overheat if operated continuously at its peak rating. Thermal management, start-up loads, variable speed, and ambient temperature can affect performance. For simplified calculations, a constant-power assumption is useful, but students should explicitly state it. Real operating time also depends on usable battery capacity, fuel consumption rate, and efficiency changing with load.

14. Selecting an engineering power source

A power-source selection begins with a specific task and measurable constraints. The engineer identifies required useful mechanical power, required operating duration, allowable mass or size, available infrastructure, and maximum operating cost. Candidate sources are then compared using their input power limits, stored energy, conversion efficiencies, and other practical characteristics.

For example, a stationary workshop machine may benefit from a grid-connected electric motor because continuous electricity is available. A remote portable machine may need a battery or fuel store. A system that runs continuously in an isolated location may require a hybrid arrangement with storage and renewable generation. The strongest recommendation states the calculated requirements and identifies the limitations of the chosen technology, rather than declaring one energy source universally superior.

Seven Worked Examples

Each example explains the problem, identifies quantities, chooses the relationship, substitutes values, calculates, and interprets the result.

1. Average power from energy and time

Engineering situation: An electric motor transfers 36,000 J of energy in 12 s. Find average power.

Step 1 — Identify the given information. Energy E = 36,000 J; time t = 12 s; unknown P.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. Use P = E/t because power measures energy transferred per unit time.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. P = 36,000 J / 12 s.

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

36,000 ÷ 12 = 3,000 J/s = 3,000 W = 3.0 kW.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. The average transfer rate is 3,000 joules each second.

2. Energy from power and duration

Engineering situation: A motor draws 2.5 kW for 4 h. Find input electrical energy.

Step 1 — Identify the given information. Input power P = 2.5 kW; operating time t = 4 h; unknown E.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. Rearrange P = E/t to E = Pt.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. E = (2.5 kW)(4 h).

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

E = 10 kWh = 36 MJ.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. The motor receives 10 kWh of electricity over the operating interval.

3. Watts, kilowatts, and horsepower

Engineering situation: A shaft delivers 10 mechanical horsepower. Convert to W and kW.

Step 1 — Identify the given information. Mechanical output = 10 hp; conversion 1 hp ≈ 746 W.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. Multiply horsepower by 746 W/hp; divide watts by 1,000 for kW.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. P = (10 hp)(746 W/hp).

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

P ≈ 7,460 W = 7.46 kW.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. Horsepower and kilowatts both measure power, not stored energy.

4. Mechanical power from force and velocity

Engineering situation: A winch pulls with 1,800 N along the direction of cable motion at 0.6 m/s. Find mechanical power.

Step 1 — Identify the given information. Aligned force F = 1,800 N; velocity v = 0.6 m/s.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. Use P = Fv because the force and motion are aligned.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. P = (1,800 N)(0.6 m/s).

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

P = 1,080 W = 1.08 kW.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. This is useful mechanical power at the cable, not necessarily electrical input power.

5. Useful power and losses

Engineering situation: A motor receives 5 kW electrical power and is 82% efficient. Find useful output and loss.

Step 1 — Identify the given information. Input P = 5 kW; efficiency η = 82% = 0.82.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. Use Puseful = ηPin; loss = Pin − Puseful.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. Puseful = 0.82(5 kW).

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

Puseful = 4.10 kW; loss = 5 − 4.10 = 0.90 kW.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. The missing 0.90 kW appears in other energy outputs such as heating and sound.

6. Multiple conversion stages

Engineering situation: A controller is 95% efficient, a motor 90%, and a gearbox 92%. Find overall efficiency and useful power from 10 kW input.

Step 1 — Identify the given information. η1 = 0.95; η2 = 0.90; η3 = 0.92; Pin = 10 kW.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. Multiply stage efficiencies as decimals; then multiply by input power.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. ηoverall = 0.95 × 0.90 × 0.92 = 0.7866.

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

Overall efficiency = 78.66%; useful output = 0.7866 × 10 = 7.866 kW.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. Losses across several stages accumulate even when each stage is relatively efficient.

7. Choose a power system

Engineering situation: A machine needs 4 kW useful mechanical output for 3 h. Candidate A stores 18 kWh and has 80% conversion efficiency and 6 kW maximum input power. Candidate B stores 12 kWh and has 90% efficiency and 5 kW maximum input power. Which meets the requirements?

Step 1 — Identify the given information. Required output = 4 kW for 3 h; A: 18 kWh, 80%, 6 kW input limit; B: 12 kWh, 90%, 5 kW input limit.

Step 2 — Select and explain the equation. For each source, calculate required input power Pout/η, then input energy = Pin × time. Check both energy and input-power limits.

Step 3 — Substitute the known values. A: Pin = 4/0.80 = 5 kW; E = 5 × 3 = 15 kWh. B: Pin = 4/0.90 ≈ 4.44 kW; E ≈ 4.44 × 3 = 13.33 kWh.

Step 4 — Calculate and state the answer.

A: 5 ≤ 6 kW and 15 ≤ 18 kWh, so A passes. B: 4.44 ≤ 5 kW but 13.33 > 12 kWh, so B fails runtime.

Step 5 — Interpret and check. Candidate A satisfies both power and duration; candidate B cannot sustain the task for three hours.

Lesson Summary

Energy is a quantity measured in joules or kilowatt-hours; power is the rate of energy transfer measured in watts or kilowatts. Work transfers energy mechanically through force and displacement. Power-source selection depends on both available stored energy and maximum power delivery. Chemical fuels, batteries, grids, and renewable sources supply energy through different conversion chains, and each conversion stage has losses.

Engineers calculate useful output with efficiency, estimate runtime from usable energy and input power, and check capacity, duration, cost, and other constraints before recommending a source. A design that has enough energy but cannot supply the required power will fail the task. Always distinguish input power from useful mechanical output and state the assumptions behind any runtime estimate.
